The Churches and Monasteries of Egypt

The text of this work is not yet hosted here. A public-domain English translation of part of it (published 1895) is available on the Internet Archive: B. T. A. Evetts, The Churches and Monasteries of Egypt and Some Neighbouring Countries, attributed to Abû Ṣâliḥ, the Armenian, translated from the original Arabic, with added notes by Alfred J. Butler (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1895), in a Google scan and a Cornell University Library scan. The book gives an introduction, the annotated translation (pp. 1–304) and the Arabic text of the Paris manuscript (Bibliothèque nationale, arabe 307, copied in 1338), and adds in an appendix the account of the Christian monasteries and churches of Egypt from al-Maqrīzī’s Khiṭaṭ (pp. 305–346). Evetts followed the manuscript’s note of ownership in naming Abū Ṣāliḥ the Armenian as author; the work is now assigned to the Coptic priest Abū al-Makārim Saʿdallāh ibn Jirjis ibn Masʿūd, who compiled it around 1200. It is a survey, district by district, of the churches and monasteries of the Copts and of other Christian communities, noting who founded or rebuilt them, the relics and wonders associated with them, the patriarchs and bishops connected with them, and what befell them under successive rulers. The Paris manuscript is only the second half of the original book: it covers Egypt south of Cairo, Nubia, Abyssinia and the rest of Africa, while the first half, now Munich, Bayerische Staatsbibliothek, Cod. arab. 2570, describes Lower Egypt as far as Cairo and the churches of the Near East. Both halves were edited in Arabic by Bishop Samuel al-Suryānī (Cairo, 1984). Other English translations: Mina al-Shamaa (translator), revised by Elizabeth, Abu al Makarem: History of the Churches and Monasteries in Lower Egypt in the 13th Century (Cairo: Institut des Études Coptes, 1992), a translation of the first part of Samuel’s edition, recorded in the Library of Congress catalogue; and a single unpublished passage on the fate of the defeated crusaders after the Second Crusade, translated by Dioscorus Boles in a comment on Roger Pearse’s blog. (Noted by Roger Pearse, 6 August 2009, and in his post of 11 March 2010.)
