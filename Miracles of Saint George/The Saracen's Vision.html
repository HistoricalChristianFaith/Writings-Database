The Saracen's Vision

No public-domain English translation of this work is available. A complete English translation of this miracle of St George (Miracle 6 in J. B. Aufhauser’s Miracula S. Georgii, Leipzig, 1913; De visione Saraceni), made by Daniel J. Sahas from the Greek text in PG 100, cols. 1201–12, was published in his article “What an Infidel Saw that a Faithful Did Not: Gregory Dekapolites (d. 842) and Islam”, Greek Orthodox Theological Review 31 (1986), pp. 47–67. It was reproduced, by permission of the author, on David Woods’s “Military Martyrs” website at University College Cork, on a page now preserved by the Internet Archive’s Wayback Machine: The Greek Miracles of St. George. Its licence is not stated. The story, which the manuscripts usually ascribe to Gregory Dekapolites, is told on the authority of the strategos Nicholas, called Joulas: a young kinsman of the emir of Syria, sent to oversee building work, stabled his camels inside a church of St George, and they fell dead; at the liturgy he saw the priest slaughter a child and give its flesh and blood to the communicants, and when the priest explained that he himself saw only bread and wine, the Saracen asked for baptism. Baptized at Mount Sinai, he became a monk named Pachomios, returned three years later to preach Christ to the emir, and was stoned to death by the Saracens; a star rested over his body for forty days. Woods notes that the tale seems to derive from the story of St Anthony Ruwah, martyred at Damascus in 799. (Noted by Roger Pearse, 2 August 2018.)
