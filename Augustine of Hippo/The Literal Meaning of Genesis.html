The Literal Meaning of Genesis

No public-domain English translation of this work is available. The complete work was translated by John Hammond Taylor, S.J., as The Literal Meaning of Genesis in two volumes (Ancient Christian Writers 41–42; Newman Press / Paulist Press, 1982): Volume 1, Books 1–6 (ISBN 978-0-8091-0326-3), and Volume 2, Books 7–12 (ISBN 978-0-8091-0327-0), with an introduction and full notes. A second complete translation, by Edmund Hill, O.P., is in On Genesis (The Works of Saint Augustine I/13; New City Press, 2002; ISBN 978-1-56548-175-6), available from the publisher, a volume that also contains Augustine’s two earlier works on Genesis. The De Genesi ad litteram libri duodecim, written between about 401 and 415, is Augustine’s longest commentary on the opening of Scripture: in twelve books it works through Genesis 1–3, from the creation to the expulsion from paradise, seeking the literal or historical sense rather than an allegorical one. Along the way it discusses whether all things were created at once, the “causal reasons” planted in creation, the meaning of the days and of light, the firmament and the waters above it, the making of Adam and Eve and the origin of the soul, and closes in Book 12 with an extended treatment of paradise and of the kinds of vision, bodily, spiritual and intellectual. It is a different work from Augustine’s early unfinished literal commentary (De Genesi ad litteram imperfectus liber), which is in this collection as The Literal Interpretation of Genesis.
