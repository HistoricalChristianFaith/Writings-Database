Poem to a Senator

No public-domain English translation of this work is available. The complete poem (Carmen ad quendam senatorem ex Christiana religione ad idolorum servitutem conversum, “Poem to a senator converted from the Christian religion to the service of idols”) is translated by Brian Croke and Jill Harries in Religious Conflict in Fourth-Century Rome: A Documentary Study (Sources in Ancient History; Sydney University Press, 1982; ISBN 0-424-00091-1), a collection of translated sources on the end of paganism at Rome; the book is out of print and no publisher page for it could be found, but it is recorded in the National Library of Australia catalogue. A complete translation, with the Latin text and a study of the poem’s date and background, is also contained in Ronald Bruce Begley’s unpublished doctoral dissertation, The “Carmen ad quendam senatorem”: Date, Milieu, and Tradition (University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill, 1984; UMI 8415790), available through ProQuest Dissertations & Theses. The poem, of 85 lines and by an unknown author, was handed down among the spurious works of Cyprian (and sometimes of Tertullian) and seems to have been written at Rome near the end of the fourth century. A Christian writes to a friend, a senator and former consul, who has abandoned his Christianity for the worship of the Great Mother (Cybele) and of Isis: he mocks the effeminate Galli and their blood-rites, and the spectacle of a man once painted at home with the consul’s fasces now walking in the goddess’s procession with a rattle and a dog’s mask, and urges him to repent and keep faith with God, since a second fall would be his own fault. (Noted by Roger Pearse, 28 April 2009, and again on 27 May 2010, when he quoted the Croke and Harries version.)
