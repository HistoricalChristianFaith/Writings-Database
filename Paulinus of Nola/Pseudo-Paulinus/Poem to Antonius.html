Poem to Antonius

No public-domain English translation of this work is available. The poem is translated complete in two modern books: by P. G. Walsh in The Poems of St. Paulinus of Nola (Ancient Christian Writers 40; Newman Press, 1975; ISBN 978-0-8091-0197-9), as poem 32, a book no longer listed by its publisher, Paulist Press, of which a copy can be borrowed from the Internet Archive; and by Brian Croke and Jill Harries in their anthology of sources Religious Conflict in Fourth-Century Rome: A Documentary Study (Sources in Ancient History; Sydney University Press, 1982; ISBN 978-0-424-00091-6), now out of print (library record). The Carmen ad Antonium is an anonymous Latin poem of the late fourth or early fifth century, addressed to a certain Antonius. It survives in a few manuscripts of the poems of Paulinus of Nola, where it follows his poems without a title or author’s name; Muratori, who first printed it, called it the Poema ultimum and gave it to Paulinus, and modern editions print it among his poems as carm. 32 (CSEL 30, pp. 329–338), though the attribution is doubtful. The poet, who says he has examined every kind of belief and found nothing better than faith in Christ, reviews in turn the ingratitude of Israel in the wilderness, the worship of man-made idols and blood sacrifice, the philosophers (Cynics, Platonists and the austere “Physici”), and the gods and cults of Rome, among them Jupiter, Janus, Saturn, Cybele and Attis, Mithras, Isis and Serapis, Vesta and Venus, before ending with an account of his own conversion, in which the Church brings him into a safe harbour. It is an important witness to the pagan cults still practised in the late Roman world. (Noted by Roger Pearse, 1 June 2010.)
