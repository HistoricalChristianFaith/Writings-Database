Commentary on Nahum

Latin source: Jerome's commentary on Nahum, in one book addressed to Paula and Eustochium (written at Bethlehem about 392–393), from the critical text of M. Adriaen, Corpus Christianorum, Series Latina 76A (Turnhout, 1970), pp. 525–578, verified against the page images, with the edition's few evident misprints corrected (three of them from the text of Vallarsi and Migne). Each lemma gives Nahum in Jerome's own version from the Hebrew and, in most places, then after "LXX:" in his Latin of the Septuagint; all Scripture is translated as Jerome quotes it, from his own Latin. Italics mark the lemmata and the other Scripture he quotes. Psalms keep the Latin (LXX) numbering, with the Hebrew number in parentheses. Greek words are kept as printed, with an English gloss in parentheses where Jerome gives none, and Hebrew words keep Jerome's own transliteration. This English translation is released into the public domain.

Prologue

According to the Seventy translators, in the order of the twelve prophets Nahum is placed after Jonah, because they seem to prophesy about the same city. For it is written in Jonah: The word of the Lord came to Jonah, son of Amittai, saying: Arise and go to Nineveh, the great city, and preach in it. But in Nahum this is the opening: The taking up of Nineveh. The book of the vision of Nahum the Elkoshite. Therefore both weave their prophecy about Nineveh, the metropolis of the Assyrians, which is now called Ninus. Moreover, in the Hebrew, Micah is placed after Jonah, and Micah is followed by Nahum, which means consoler. For the ten tribes had already been led away into captivity by the Assyrians under Hezekiah, king of Judah, under whom now also the vision against Nineveh is seen, for the consolation of the people carried away. Nor was it a small consolation, both for those who were already serving the Assyrians and for the rest, who under Hezekiah, of the tribe of Judah and Benjamin, were being besieged by the same enemies, to hear that the Assyrians too were to be taken by the Chaldeans, as will be shown in what follows in this book. But it must be understood — since Nineveh in our language, from the Hebrew, sounds beautiful; and this world is called beautiful, whence among the Greeks too κόσμος (world) received its name from adornment —, that whatever is now said against Nineveh is proclaimed figuratively of the world. For this reason the taking up, which the Seventy translate λῆμμα, and which Aquila translated ἄρμα, is given among the Hebrews as massa, that is, a heavy burden, because it presses down on her against whom it is seen, and does not allow her to raise her neck. Moreover, as to what is added, Nahum the Elkoshite, some think that Elkoshite was Nahum's father, and that according to the Hebrew tradition he too was a prophet, whereas Elkosh is to this day a hamlet in Galilee, small indeed, and scarcely showing in its ruins the traces of old buildings, but still known to the Jews, and pointed out to me as well by the man who was leading me around. At the same time this too must be noted, that this very taking up, or burden, or weight, is the prophet's vision. For he does not speak in ἐκστάσει (ecstasy), as Montanus and Prisca and Maximilla rave, but what he prophesies is the book of the vision of one who understands everything he speaks, and who makes the weight of the enemies a vision among his own people. Therefore, concerning the consummation of the world according to ἀναγωγὴν (anagogy), O Paula and Eustochium, the prophecy is woven for the consolation of the saints, so that whatever they see in the world they may despise as passing and perishable, and may prepare themselves for the day of judgment, where the Lord is to be the avenger against the true Assyrians.

1:2

A jealous God, and an avenging Lord. The voice of the prophet praising God because he has avenged on the Assyrians the wrong done to his people. Or, according to a higher understanding, because he hears the groaning of his saints, and at the consummation of the world makes the adversaries feel punishments. That zeal is taken in a good sense, the apostle Paul too shows, saying: Be zealous for the greater gifts, and in another place: For I am zealous for you with the zeal of God, and the Lord himself in the psalm: The zeal of your house has consumed me, and Elijah: Zealous, he says, I have been zealous for the Lord Almighty, the God of Israel. We read also of the zeal of Phinehas, and of Mattathias. And Simon the Zealot, the apostle of Jesus Christ, whom Mark the evangelist calls Simon the Cananaean. But the Lord is zealous for the salvation of those over whom he is zealous, so that those whom his clemency could not save, his jealousy may save. Hence also to Jerusalem, which because of its excessive sins did not deserve the visitation of his zeal and fury, God speaks in Ezekiel: My zeal has departed from you, and I will be angry with you no more. So as long as the world was doing repentance, its consummation did not come about; but after iniquity was multiplied and the charity of many grew cold, so that even the elect of God are tempted, then the zealous Lord comes for vengeance, not that he himself is an enemy and avenger, which is said of the devil, but that his vengeance is hostile, and like fire consumes wood, hay and stubble, so that pure gold and silver may remain.

The Lord avenging, and having fury: the Lord avenging on his foes, and he himself angry with his enemies. LXX: The Lord avenging with fury, the Lord avenging on his adversaries, and he himself taking away his enemies. According to both understandings, because the Lord loves the one whom he rebukes, and chastises every son whom he receives, he is therefore angry, so that he may take away whatever is adverse and hostile to him, and so that, when the foes and the contrary thoughts are broken and their words taken away, they may return to their former state. Then in what follows he says: The Lord is patient, and his strength long. But since we have once undertaken to expound the history as well, let us understand the enemies and foes of God to be the Assyrians, against whom, though he has long been patient, he will afterwards show himself an avenger with fury and wrath.

1:3

The Lord is patient and great in strength, and, cleansing, he will not make innocent. This is said more expressively in the Greek: καὶ ἀθῳῶν οὐκ ἀθῳώσει (and clearing he will not clear). And the sense is: he was indeed long patient with the crime of the Assyrians, and by the strength of his magnanimity bore their iniquities, calling them to repentance; but since, having despised the goodness of God, according to their impenitent heart they stored up for themselves wrath on the day of wrath, he who before was patient will by no means allow them to go away unpunished as though clean and innocent. Or certainly thus — since we wish at the same time to take what is said in a good sense —, he is patient who upholds all who are falling, and raises up those who are dashed down, who heals the troubled in heart and binds up their bruises, and great is his power, dissolving the enmities in the flesh, and not making the guilty innocent, since even he who applauds himself overmuch will be convicted of having been saved not by his own merit but by the mercy of God. For although he may say: Behold, I have served you so many years, and I have never passed over your command, yet since the Lord is good to all, and his mercies are over all his works; and all have sinned, and lack the glory of God, justified by him freely, he will hear: Or is your eye evil, because I am good? And so it will come about that in the very act by which he reproves and pardons, he allows no one to go away as innocent.

The Lord, in storm and whirlwind are his ways, and clouds are the dust of his feet. For storm and whirlwind the Seventy translated consummation and commotion, although where we have put in storm, and where in the Hebrew basupha is written, it can also be understood as in commotion. This signifies that at the end of the world all things will be moved, according to what is written in Haggai: Yet once more I too will move heaven and earth, sea and dry land; and when all things have been moved — so that they may believe the ways of the Lord, who says in the Gospel: I am the way, and the life, and the truth, and: When the Son of Man comes in the clouds, which in Isaiah are commanded not to rain rain upon the vineyard, and to which the truth of God reaches, as the psalmist says: Your truth even to the clouds —, then the clouds themselves too, namely the prophets and the souls of the saints, which before had been weighed down by their union with the flesh, will be lifted on high and become of a finer substance, and, brought down to be the footstool of God's feet, will minister among the angels in each of the lowest offices. For they will not be able to know the former and ancient things that pertain to the head. But another will take the clouds in the contrary sense, because they always trouble what is serene, and strive to veil the clear light of the sun and stars with their darkness, and because afterwards, subjected to the Lord's power, they are reduced to dust and to nothing, and the earth in them, which is fat, is dissolved.

1:4

Rebuking the sea, and drying it up, and leading all the rivers to the desert. According to the letter, the power of God is described: since he is about to vindicate Israel against its adversaries, it is no great thing for him to destroy the Assyrians, whose majesty is such that he can even change the elements. Or certainly, since we have said once for all that the prophecy concerns the consummation of the world, take this too in the simple sense. When the consummation of the world comes, and heaven and earth pass away, the sea too and the rivers are dried up. But to me, as I read that passage in the psalms: This great and wide sea, there are creeping things without number, small animals with great. There the ships pass, that dragon which you formed to mock him, it seems worthy of the goodness and clemency of God to destroy by his threat all the bitterness and saltiness of the sea, and to humble the dragon that reigns in the waters, and to dry up the whirlpools of malice in which the small creeping things swim, of which there is no number; for the things that dwell with the dragon are not worthy of number. Leading the rivers also to the desert: all knowledge falsely so called, which, raising itself against God, uses the river of eloquence and volubility of words, and, rolling its swelling whirlpools, is borne headlong to the wonder of those who watch. Look at Plato, behold Demosthenes, and Tully too, philosopher and orator alike, and consider the chiefs of the heretics, among whom were Valentinus, Marcion, Bardesanes, Tatian, and you will have no doubt about the rivers. But all these the Lord Jesus will consume with the spirit of his mouth, and destroy with the illumination of his coming, and will lead to the deserts. And at the same time observe that, according to the title, which is inscribed The taking up of Nineveh. The book of the vision of Nahum the Elkoshite, rightly both is the world understood figuratively in Nineveh, and its sea and rivers of eloquence are dried up at the consummation.

Bashan has grown weak, and Carmel, and the flower of Lebanon has languished. LXX: Basanitis has been diminished, and Carmel, and the things of Lebanon that flourished have failed. Μεταφορικῶς (metaphorically), through Bashan, Carmel and Lebanon, a fertile region and planted mountains, the devastation of the Assyrian is shown, because he, once powerful and flourishing and ruling over very many nations, is to be laid waste when the Lord is angry. But we can also understand it of the consummation of the world, that all the powerful and noble, and those abounding in excessive wealth, suddenly perish, and it is said to them: Fool, this night your soul will be taken from you; and the things you have prepared, whose will they be? Moreover, according to the interpretation of the names, since Bashan sounds confusion and disgrace, we assert that at the consummation of the world, when the Lord comes, everything that is worthy of disgrace and blushing grows weak, and that not only are sins reduced to nothing, but also Carmel, which is interpreted knowledge of circumcision, and those who seem to themselves rich and flourishing in good works, tremble at the coming of Christ, and that the saying in the Gospel is fulfilled: When the Son of Man comes, do you think he will find faith on the earth? For as iniquity increases, the charity of very many will grow cold, and on account of these things the wrath of God will come.

1:5

The mountains were moved by him, and the hills were made desolate, and the earth trembled before his face, and the world, and all who dwell in it. LXX: The mountains were moved by him, and the hills were shaken, and the earth shrank back before his face, the universe and all who dwell in it. It can also be taken simply: that at the consummation of the world, when the Savior comes in his majesty, the mountains and hills, the world and the earth, and all things are moved. For if at his passion the sun fled, the rocks were split, the earth trembled, much more will all things be thrown into turmoil at his glory. But figuratively too the mountains and hills are to be understood as the lofty and powerful, who at the coming of the Lord will be cast down to the ground, and, deposed from their throne, will cleave to the pavement. The face for of the Lord is upon those who do evil, to destroy their memory from the earth. Then too the earth will tremble, and the world and the universe will dread the face of the Lord, counting it the supreme torment and punishment that they will not dare to look on his face.

1:6

Before the face of his indignation who will stand, and who will resist in the wrath of his fury? LXX: From the face of his wrath who will endure, and who will resist in the wrath of his fury? In place of what we have put: And who will resist in the wrath of his fury? Symmachus translated more plainly: And who will endure the wrath of his fury? Therefore one will be found rarely, or not at all, who is not deserving of the wrath that rebukes. Nor will there be any soul that does not tremble at the judgment of God, since even the stars are not clean in his sight. Moreover, the Hebrew word iaccum, which Aquila and the Seventy translated will resist, let us transfer to that understanding of which it is said, in the second book of Kingdoms (2 Samuel) and in the first book of Chronicles, concerning the wrath of God, in the masculine gender. And no one doubts that there the wrath of God is understood as the devil, and the most wicked angels, who are sent to punish those who are worthy of wrath. So it will be hard to find, at the consummation of the world, one who, spotless and pure, dares to say: Behold, the prince of this world comes, and finds nothing in me, and stands against him with a free brow. But against the Assyrians it must be understood thus: that when the Lord comes in storm and whirlwind, drying up the empire of Babylon, which is interpreted sea, and overturning all its kingdoms, which are understood as rivers, and reducing to nothing its power and fertility, which μεταφορικῶς (metaphorically) are called Bashan and Carmel and the flower of Lebanon, and mountains and hills, and shaking the breadth of its empire, which is called the world, then no power will be able to resist God when he is angry and vindicating his people.

His indignation has been poured out like fire, and the rocks have been dissolved by him. LXX: His fury consumes principalities, and the rocks have been crushed by him. In place of what we have put, has been poured out, Aquila translated συνεχωνεύθη, that is, has been fused, Symmachus and Theodotion ἔσταξεν, that is, has dripped. Whether, then, the indignation of God has been fused like fire, or his fury has dripped in the likeness of a blaze, so that the hard hearts of men, which are called rocks, might be crushed and dissolved, the indignation of God is useful, which, long bearing our sins through patience, has scarcely ever been fused, and yet does not burst forth wholly into punishment, but will drip on us with a moderate heat. But if a drop of his indignation consumes the principalities, against whom we have the wrestling of battle, what would happen if the whole wrath of God were poured out on us? May Jesus grant that the heart of stone be taken away and changed in us into a heart of flesh, and that, its hardness softened, it may receive into itself the precepts of the Lord which are written: For a sacrifice to God is a troubled spirit; a contrite and humbled heart God will not despise. And that you may know that what has been said sounds of the clemency of God, not of severity, learn from what follows.

1:7

Good is the Lord, and strengthening in the day of tribulation, and knowing those who hope in him. LXX: Sweet is the Lord to those who wait for him in the day of tribulation, and recognizing those who fear him. When he has begun to be angry with the nations and to lay waste kingdoms once most powerful, he will know those who are his, and will not overwhelm those at sea in one and the same storm. But let us understand the day of tribulation, according to ἀναγωγὴν (anagogy), as the day of judgment, of which Isaiah wrote: Behold, the day of the Lord comes, incurable, of fury and of wrath, to make the world a desert and to destroy sinners out of it. Let us hope in the Lord, and through patience await his coming, so that when he comes we may experience him as good, not as a judge, and that he may recognize either those who hope in him or those who fear him. For the Lord knows those who are his.

1:8

And in a flood passing by he will make a consummation of its place, and darkness will pursue his enemies. LXX: And in a flood passing over he will make a consummation; darkness will pursue those rising up and his enemies. The Lord is patient and of great mercy, and he will not be angry to the end, nor will he be indignant forever; but when wickedness has grown upon the earth, and all flesh has corrupted its way, he will bring on a flood that is to pass through, not to remain forever. But he will make a consummation, or an end, of its place, that is, of the flood, so that, just as it is said of the impious man: And I passed by, and his place was not found, and: The way of the impious will perish, so the way of the flood may perish after the wrath of the Lord, with mercy alone appearing. This indeed can also be understood according to the history: when he has laid Israel waste, and has inundated the land of promise as if with a flood, he will make an end of the captivity, calling him back to his former dwellings. But on the contrary, darkness will pursue his enemies the Assyrians, who led the people into captivity. But what we have said of Israel and the Assyrians can be understood of the consummation of the world, and of the saints and of the persecutors, or the opposing powers: that God has mercy on the saints after his wrath; but as for the persecutors and their enemies, who have chosen darkness and not light, the very darkness that they chose will seize them; for they will be sent into the outer darkness: There will be weeping and gnashing of teeth. The word macoma, which we have translated "of its place", all have divided into two parts of speech, so that they turned ma into the preposition "from", that is, ἀπὸ, and interpreted coma as "rising up". Finally Aquila says ἀπὸ ἀνισταμένων, that is, "from those rising up"; the Seventy, "those rising up"; Theodotion, "for those rising up against him"; the Fifth edition, "from those rising up against him". Symmachus alone, agreeing with our translation, says: And in a flood passing over, he will make a consummation of its place. Some of our own interpret "those rising up and the enemies" as Marcion and all the old heretics, who dispute against the Creator.

1:9

What are you plotting against the Lord? He himself will make a consummation; a twofold tribulation will not rise up. LXX: What are you plotting against the Lord? He himself will make a consummation, he will not avenge twice on the same thing in tribulation. Symmachus more plainly: They will not withstand the onset of a second distress; Theodotion: A second tribulation will not rise up. But according to tropology he is speaking against Marcion, of whom we spoke above, and against all the old heretics, who, inventing some good God or other, say that he is the one who will make the consummation of the world, and accuse the God of the law as if of cruelty, because he punishes many and inflicts torments for sins. What then, he says, are you plotting against the Lord? He himself who created the world will also make its consummation. But if he seems to you cruel, harsh and bloody, because he blotted out the human race in the flood, rained fire and sulphur upon Sodom and Gomorrah, drowned the Egyptians in the waves, and laid low the corpses of the Israelites in the desert, know that he exacted punishments in the present for this reason: that he might not punish forever. Surely what the prophets say is either true or false. If what they seem to say about his severity is true, they themselves have said: The Lord will not avenge twice on the same thing in tribulation. But if they are false, then this too is false that is said: A twofold tribulation will not rise up; false, therefore, is also the cruelty that is described in the law. But if it is true, as they will not be able to deny, since the prophet says: The Lord will not avenge twice on the same thing in tribulation, then those who have been punished are not punished afterward. But if they are punished afterward, Scripture lies, which it is impious to say. Therefore those who perished in the flood, and the Sodomites, and the Egyptians, and the Israelites in the wilderness received their evil things in their lifetime. Here someone may ask, if a believer caught in adultery is beheaded, what becomes of him afterward. For either he will be punished, and this saying is false: The Lord will not avenge twice on the same thing in tribulation, or he will not be punished, and it is something for adulterers to wish for, that by a short and swift penalty in the present they may escape eternal torments. To this we will answer that God, as he knows the measures of all things, so knows the measures of punishments too; that he is not forestalled by the sentence of a judge, nor is the power taken from him of exercising punishment on the sinner thereafter; and that a great sin is washed away by great and long-lasting torments. But if anyone has been punished, like him in the law who had cursed the Israelites, and him who had gathered wood on the sabbath, such men are not punished afterward, because a light fault has been compensated by the present punishment. The Hebrew expounded this passage thus: What do you think, O Assyrians, you who plot iniquity against the Lord, that he himself would consume the people of Israel, that is, the twelve tribes, to the point of extermination? A twofold tribulation will not rise up, that is, he will not hand over Judah and Jerusalem to you as he handed over the ten tribes and Samaria.

1:10

For as thorns embrace one another, so is their banquet of men drinking together; it will be consumed like stubble full of dryness. LXX: For even to their foundations they will be reduced into brambles, and like bindweed wound about it will be devoured, and like stubble full of dryness. The three distinctions which the Lord set down in the parable of the sowing, apart from the good soil, which bears fruit thirtyfold and sixtyfold and a hundredfold, the prophet seems to me to unfold again: one, the seed that fell beside the way, another that fell among stones, and a third that fell among thorns; and the Apostle too, speaking of those who do not build well upon the foundation of Christ, indeed intimates this, that there are some who build wood, hay, stubble. Let us therefore refer the wood to what is now said: For even to their foundations they will be reduced to brambles. But the hay to what follows: And like bindweed that is wound about, it will be devoured. Moreover, the stubble is plainly referred to the stubble of which it is now said: And like stubble full of dryness. For this reason, then, the Lord will not avenge twice on the same thing, because the wickedness that had arisen in the authors of heresy will be consumed down to the foundation and to its roots. And if anyone among them seemed to have a pomp of words, that is, empty leaves that delight only the eye, he will be devoured like bindweed, which in Greek is called σμίλαξ, and will be consumed into nothing. Now bindweed is a plant like ivy, which commonly winds itself around vines and shrubs and creeps a long way. Whatever in them, too, seems to have the show of a crop, but has no ears and grains of wheat, will be handed over to the fire like stubble full of dryness. This is according to the Seventy. But according to the Hebrew, truly the alliances and close ties of the heretics are like thorns embracing one another, and their banquet and their mysteries, since they too say that they have the Lord's table, are a league of thorns, eating together and drinking together alike. For when, drunk from the vine of Sodom, they blaspheme against the Maker with rabid mouth, is it not a banquet of briars? Yet it will be consumed together with its authors, like stubble full of dryness.

1:11

For out of you will go forth one plotting wickedness against the Lord, turning over transgression in his mind. LXX: Out of you will go forth a thought against the Lord, most evil, thinking contrary things. Truly from the heretics will go forth a thought hostile to the Lord. Does it not seem to be wickedness and transgression against God to say, as Valentinus does, that the Creator was brought forth last, as if the abortion of an erring Wisdom? Is not the shamelessness of Basilides a thought against God, and the monstrous name ἄβραξας (Abraxas), which is set above the Lord the Maker? Moreover, since, having been taught by the Hebrews, we wish to follow their tradition too, and to set out for our own, that is, for Christians, an explanation of the history, it must be said: A twofold tribulation will not rise up, that is, the two tribes will not be captured by the Assyrians as the ten were captured. For while the Assyrians are still in the land of Judah, and like thorns embrace one another, glad and rejoicing, they will be consumed by the angel, when in one night one hundred and eighty-five thousand of the enemy were slain. Beautifully, too, he compared the multitude of their army to a banquet of drunkards, and said that the banquet itself was not of roses, not of lilies, not of flowers, but like thorns embracing one another, which are always handed over to the fire and, like stubble full of dryness, are burned up by a slight blaze. But what follows: Out of you will go forth one plotting wickedness against the Lord, turning over transgression in his mind, they want it understood of Rabshakeh, because, having come forth from the Assyrians, he blasphemed God and wished to persuade the people to despair of the Lord's help and hand themselves over to the Assyrians, to serve not God but idols.

1:12-13

Thus says the Lord: If they are complete, and so more numerous, even so they will be shorn, and he will pass through; I have afflicted you, and I will not afflict you any more; and now I will break his rod from your back, and I will burst your bonds. LXX: Thus says the Lord, reigning over many waters: and so they will be divided, and your report will not be heard any more; and now I will break his rod from you, and I will burst his bonds. According to the letter the sense is plain: Although, he says, the Assyrians are strong, and their might is increased by the number of all the nations, even so, as the angel lays waste, they are shorn. For just as the number of the hairs does not resist the sharp shears, so too the number of God's adversaries will be removed by an easy cutting down, and Asshur will pass through: either he will cease to exist, or, his army laid waste, he will return to his homeland, leaving you safe. And again the word is directed to Judah and Jerusalem: I have afflicted you, and I will not afflict you any more, not that he promises perpetual security, but only for that time, and from those enemies by whom it was then besieged. Finally he adds: And now I will break his rod, that is, the Assyrian's, from your back, and I will burst your bonds; either signifying his power by a metaphor, or else the rod with which he was trying to strike, and the bonds which he was preparing for the captives; although the siege of the shut-in multitude can also be taken for the bonds. But according to the LXX the sense is far different. For he still seems to be speaking against those to whom he had said above: What are you plotting against the Lord? and: Out of you will go forth a thought against the Lord, most evil, thinking contrary things. Thus, then, says the Lord, reigning over many waters, that is, over the powers, which are called the waters above the heavens, and which are commanded to praise the Lord, or else over the understandings and the wisdom and the teachings of God. For just as rivers will flow from the belly of the righteous man, and abundant springs unto eternal life, through the various and manifold meanings over which the word of the Lord rules, so the heresiarchs too have their own waters, over which they rule, and which first flowed from their spring. But what follows: And so they will be divided, can be understood either of the heavenly powers that serve God on high, in that each one is in its own office and ministry, or of the manifold variety of wisdom; lest, because he had said: Reigning over many waters, the number of meanings should be thought confused and undistinguished, but rather that each statement has meanings divided among themselves, and separate matters and its own ὑποθέσεις (subjects). For what is said: Your report will not be heard any more, is a rebuke against those who had thought things contrary to God: that, once the sophisms and snares by which the people of God was entangled have been exposed, their word may no longer run its course, nor be received by the peoples. But this too, which he adds: And now I will break his rod from you, and I will burst your bonds, is said on behalf of the very ones against whom the threat is made, that they may by no means be beaten by the devil, nor be subject to him at whose instigation they had devised and fabricated such great things. Therefore his dominion over them will be broken, and the bonds by which the souls of sinners were bound will be burst by the word of God, saying to those who are in bonds: Go out.

1:14

And the Lord will command against you: It shall not be sown from your name any more. From the house of your god I will slay the graven and the molten image; I will make your grave, for you are without honor. LXX: And the Lord will command concerning you: It shall not be sown from your name any more. From the house of your god I will destroy the graven images and the molten images, I will make your grave, for they are swift; behold upon the mountains the feet of one who brings good news and announces peace. I have set down more from the Seventy translators for this reason, that the second περικοπὴ (pericope) could not be separated from the first. For where it says, for you are without honor, for which the Fifth edition put ὅτι ὑβρίσθης, the Seventy translated: for they are swift, which in the Hebrew reads chi calloth. But unless you refer "swift" to the feet of the following section, the sentence is left hanging. Therefore, according to my custom, I will sift first the history, and afterward the sense of the common edition. The Lord, he says, will command against you, O Asshur, so that what you are to suffer may not come by chance and without any judge, but you may suffer it at God's pronouncement. It shall not be sown from your name any more: for indeed Sennacherib, as soon as he had returned to Nineveh, was killed by his sons. Read Isaiah: and he was killed in the house of his god, which he had entered to worship. For this is what he says: From the house of your god I will slay: you will be punished from the place from which you hoped for help. The graven and the molten image will be your grave, so that amid the altars and the sacred couches the impious blood of one worshipping idols may be poured out. But according to the Seventy translators, join what follows to what precedes: It shall not be sown, he says, from your name any more. By no means, O heretics, will the souls of the deceived take from your dogmas the names which they had previously invoked upon their lands, as is sung figuratively in the forty-eighth (49th) psalm. Whoever sees the ruins of Nineveh, and her set as an example for all, will be terrified and will marvel, and will say: Nineveh is torn apart, who will shake the head over you? That is, who will grieve over you, who will be able to be your consoler? You who, as long as you were powerful, like a cruel mistress, had no pity on the old man, nor regarded the little child, nor prepared for yourself a companion in your mourning, you who would have no partner in reigning. But he who despises these earthly things, and has scorned the sorceries of Nineveh the poisoner, and has not been ensnared by her false beauty, when he has seen all her foulness within and has begun to hate what the rest love, will flee and spring back from her, or, as is said by the Seventy, will go down. For as long as we honor earthly things and think them lofty, we are, as it were, once at the pinnacle of pride, and we admire the beauty of Nineveh. But when we have considered her nature, and have despised all bodily goods as lowly, subjecting ourselves to the power of the hand of God, then we shall pity Nineveh, and judge all earthly goods worthy of lamentation, and we shall say: Wretched Nineveh, how many have been ensnared by your snares, how many you hold bound with your chains. Who, do you think, will spring back from you and go down from your pride, and judge you wretched? Now what he says, who, we ought to take not so much as meaning what is difficult, as what is rare, as we have often said: Who, do you think, is the faithful and prudent steward? and: Who is wise, and he will understand these things? and: Who will ascend into the mountain of the Lord? Who, then, will groan over Nineveh? Who can be found who, weighed down by this tabernacle, may say with Paul: Wretched man that I am, who will deliver me from the body of this death? We see daily that if death has come near to anyone, and he has understood that he is being taken away from this world by fever, or by a wound, or by any kind of disease, he panics, he trembles, and, shaking in his whole body, he clings to the embraces of Nineveh and is scarcely torn away from the body of the beautiful harlot. Now what follows, Whence shall I seek consolation, or a consoler for you, who may tune the string? It is still said in the person of him who will spring back or go down from Nineveh, and will say: Wretched Nineveh, who will groan for her? He is discoursing on the confused way of life of this age, in which nothing can please anyone for ever; but what pleased displeases, and what had displeased pleases again. Who, then, will be able to be found as such a consoler? And, so to speak, a lyric writer and a lyre-player who could draw her discordant strings into one harmony, and make of them a concert of voices in the praises of God? This which we have expounded, who may tune the string, or tuning the string, which in Greek is said ἁρμόσαι χορδὴν, we have found neither in the Hebrew nor in the other translators, but in its place the beginning of another passage: Are you better than Amon, which dwells among the rivers? And he said that in Hebrew Alexandria is called No, and Amon a multitude, or peoples; and that the order of the reading is: Are you better than Alexandria the populous, or of the peoples, which dwells among the rivers, water round about her? Not that it was called Alexandria at that time, since it received its name long afterwards from Alexander the Great of Macedon, but because under its first name, that is, No, it was always the metropolis of Egypt and most abounding in peoples. Finally, those too who have handed down to memory the deeds of Alexander affirm that it was the chief city of Egypt. Moreover the prophet Jeremiah too understands Amon, or No, as Alexandria in the vision against Egypt, to which he says: Egypt is a beautiful heifer; a goader will come to her from the north. He also adds this more plainly: The daughter of Egypt is confounded, she has been delivered into the hand of the people of the north, says the Lord of powers, the God of Israel. Behold, I will visit upon Amon Menno, that is, upon the people from Alexandria. For Amon, as we have said, means peoples, men the preposition 'from', and No Alexandria. And I will visit, he says, upon Pharaoh, and upon Egypt and upon her gods; and upon her kings, and upon Pharaoh, and upon those who hope in him; and I will give them into the hands of those who seek their lives, and into the hand of Nebuchadnezzar king of Babylon, and into the hand of his servants. And so it is said to Nineveh: Are you more populous, or more powerful, than Alexandria? And the site of Alexandria is described, which, set upon the Nile and the sea, is surrounded on this side and that by waters and rivers. Water is round about her, whose riches are the sea, waters are her walls. For on one side she is girded by the stream of the Nile, on another by Lake Mareotis, on another side by the sea. And that Ethiopia and Egypt, and Africa, for which in the Hebrew Phut is put, and the Libyans are her protection, the very situation of the provinces and of the city shows. She too, therefore, whom my discourse, says the prophet, describes, will be taken by the Babylonian king, and the same man will be the devastator both of you and of her. These things Josephus too, the writer of the Jewish history, relates in his books: Her little ones will be dashed at the crossroads; her nobles will be divided by the lot of the victors, and princes once most powerful will be led captive in chains. Since, then, Alexandria is to suffer these things, you too, O Nineveh, will drink from the same cup, and will be drunk, and lying stupefied you will be despised, and you will come into such need that you will ask help from the Babylonians, or against the Babylonians, from your enemies. For what is read in the Seventy, Tune, or set in order, the string, is still said to Nineveh. And the sense is: What is disordered and out of tune in you, and jarring in different directions, tune like strings, Nineveh, for your beauty and your greatness, which you believe you possess in a pre-eminent degree, will profit you nothing unless you tune yourself for singing. For consider the whole part of the lot of the sons of Ammon, and how whatever goods they are reckoned to possess did not defend them from going into captivity and from their little ones being dashed in the ways. What did the rivers profit her, beside which the city of Ammon is set? What did so great a multitude of wells and springs, besides the rivers, beginning from the Dead Sea and walling in her region, profit her? What protection did Ethiopia and Egypt, once her allies, afford her? As, then, the help of her allies did not aid her, so neither will there be an end of your fleeing, O Nineveh, but you will be laid waste hither and thither. Why should I speak of the Ethiopians and the Egyptians, who were the protectors of the sons of Ammon, when even the Libyans were her allies? She too, therefore, will be led into captivity; her little ones, because they will not be able to walk, will be slain in the ways, struck down before the faces of their parents, and all her wealth will perhaps be divided by the victors. Nor will any of her princes escape, for they will be bound with iron and fetters. And you therefore, O Nineveh, will be drunk, and you who were once rich and beautiful, who had so many lovers, will be despised by all, and with your enemies pursuing you will seek rest and will not find it. All your warriors and all your auxiliaries will be a prey to the enemies, and will be taken without any labor, like the early figs of a fig tree, which, when shaken, fall not to the ground, so that there may not be even the small labor of gathering them, but straightway into the mouth of the devourer. Let these things be said παραφραστικῶς (by way of paraphrase) according to the Seventy translators: for we have once for all resolved to follow the common edition too, lest we seem to have given the Hydra and Sardanapalus any occasion for censure. However, the example of the overthrow of Nineveh does not seem to me to fit well enough with the sons of Lot, who are called Amman. For first, it is called Ammon and not Amman; then Ammana, which is now called Philadelphia, is not set upon rivers, nor is its wealth gathered from the sea, since it is inland; nor are waters its walls, nor does it have Ethiopia and Egypt and Africa and the Libyans as allies; whereas all these things, as to the example, as to power, and as to the description of the place and region and of the friendly nations, are rather to be fitted to Alexandria; and Nineveh, a most powerful city, compared with lesser Philadelphia, would never hear from the prophet: Are you better? But the one to whom it is said, Are you better, is shown to be less than the one with which she is compared, and that she ought not to take it ill if she is captured, since a greater, firmer and more powerful city, both by the nature of its site and by its brave men, has been overcome by the same enemy. But since we have interpreted Nineveh also as this world, she is commanded to tune and set in order her strings and to prepare herself for a mournful song; for the part of the sons of Ammon, which had been much better than Nineveh and had dwelt upon rivers, because it was caught in error, has paid the penalty of its crime. And first, as must be said according to the history of Alexandria, Ammon means peoples, and the sense according to the laws of allegory is this: Consider the peoples of the Church, which dwells upon the streams of the prophets, and has teachers round about her, out of whose belly rivers flow, and whose beginning is the sea. For from the reading of the law, which without the wood of Christ is bitter, like Marah, one comes to her mystery, and she has Ethiopia for her strength. For Ethiopia indeed will be first to stretch out her hands to God; and she has Egypt, into which the Lord came on a light cloud; and the Libyans, who before dwelt in parched places and afterwards became her help. She too, therefore, if she does not take heed to herself and keep her heart with all diligence, will be led captive and will mourn her sons. Her little ones too, who are still at the beginnings of the ways and have not reached the middle of the journey, will be dashed at their very beginnings, and the most savage enemies will hasten to divide among themselves by lot all her splendid things, and they will drag into captivity her nobles, whom we can understand as princes and overseers, bound with chains and hampered by the heaviest weight of fetters. And you therefore, Nineveh, that is, unbelieving men, men clinging wholly to the world, will feel punishments and will be stupefied by my cup, since those too have drunk who had been of my portion and fell by their own fault. And you will be despised by me; you will seek an end amid the vices and passions that will press upon you, and nonetheless you will not be able to find a resting place and an end of evils; but all your desires and delights, and the powers of the age, and the dogmas you seemed to yourself to hold most firmly, will be devoured by the eater, of whom Samson speaks in a parable: Out of the eater came forth food, and out of the strong came forth sweetness. For then all your strong things, and those that promised sweet fruits to the eyes of onlookers, will tumble at the first shaking of the tree into the mouth of the devouring devil, by whom Nineveh had always been held and possessed. Moreover, what we passed over, And there is no end of your flight, since it is said to Nineveh and is set among the things written about Ammon, and seems to be inserted irregularly in a place not its own, let us refer to Nineveh as if by ὑπέρβατον (hyperbaton), so that the order is: And you will be drunk, and there is no end of your flight, and you will be despised, and the rest that is said to Nineveh. And we will interpret it to mean that there is no end of Nineveh's flight from God, because she always makes progress in fleeing and never wishes to stand still, according to what we said above: And they, fleeing, did not stand, and there was none to look back. And we will say that holy Scripture is woven with these difficulties, and above all the prophets, who are full of riddles, so that the difficulty of the language too may wrap up the difficulty of the meanings, that what is holy may not easily lie open to dogs, nor pearls to swine, nor the holy of holies to the profane. But if we wish to interpret Ammon of the sons of Lot, let us say that Lot had two sons by his two daughters, Moab and Ammon, of whom the elder, Moab, means 'from the father' or 'paternal water', and the younger, Ammon, either 'son of my race' or 'our people'. And I think that, just as to one who had been born of Judah it is said because of his sin: Seed of Canaan, and not of Judah, and in Ezekiel to sinful Jerusalem: Your root and your generation are from the land of Canaan, your father an Amorite, and your mother a Hittite, so whoever were of the former people, that is, of the Jews, and of the younger, that is, of ours, are figuratively called Moabites and Ammonites. And because they turned aside from their father — for Lot means turning aside — they are to be subjected to punishment and will suffer all that we have set out above. But if the severity of God begins with those who were once holy, and she who dwelt among the rivers will be purged by the fire of Gehenna, how much more will Nineveh, which before did not have the law nor receive the yoke of God's commandments, because of her pride at the last fall into the mouth of the devourer.

These things, as I said, have been said by us briefly so that the sense may be grasped; now, returning to the beginning of the section, let us unfold each point as we can. Who would not say that beautiful Nineveh, a soul beautiful by nature, beguiled by the luxury and pleasures of this age, has come to womanish delights, and, her manliness lost, has languished into a woman? For if the soul of the righteous man, coming to the perfect man and keeping the firmness of its creation, and cleaving to God, is made one spirit with him, why should not, on the contrary, the soul that loves the world become one with the world, and, reduced to womanly softness, lose its manly firmness? I think that for this reason in Exodus Pharaoh commands that every male born to the Hebrews be thrown into the river, and every female be kept alive. For the Egyptian king, who says elsewhere: The rivers are mine, and I made them, could command nothing other than this, that whatever is strong and manly in the Hebrews, and in those who pass through this age, be thrown into the waters and carried down by their streams into the sea. And on the contrary, whatever seemed womanly and soft and comely in this age, that should be kept alive, should grow up and beget. And consider at the same time that the Egyptian ruler cannot kill the men of the Hebrews, nor those who have already come out of infancy, but only those whose age is still tender, and their body soft, and their progress just beginning; he knows that the females cannot be reared unless the males have been killed. So he wishes to drown in the whirlpool of his river whatever is strong and manly in the Hebrews, so that what is womanly may grow up alone more freely. As for what follows, To your enemies the gates of your land will be opened with opening, you will be able to understand it by taking testimony from Jeremiah, in whom it is written: Death has come up through your windows. And what in Jeremiah is shown in the windows, here you will declare is shown in the gates, and you will refer these very gates to the senses. For the divine word, knowing that the senses are twofold, says in Proverbs, to distinguish them from the evil senses: You will find a divine sense. But here you should not take sense for the soul and the mind, which in Greek is called νοῦς, but for αἰσθήσει (sense-perception), from which the five senses also are named: sight, smell, taste, touch, hearing. The gates of the land of Nineveh, then, are understood as the bodily senses; but the gates of the heavenly Jerusalem as every divine sense, and one coming from above. These gates of Nineveh her people opens through sight and hearing and all the remaining senses, as through the broad and spacious way that leads to death, seeking to seize the bodily pleasures against which the men of God close their gates, stopping their ears lest they hear the judgment of blood, closing their eyes lest they see iniquity, stopping their nostrils lest they take in by smell the chief ointments to the effeminacy of their soul, closing the mouth to the gullet and to the greedy belly, and drawing back their hands from soft touch, lest the belly, boiling over into lust, drive the burning soul into womanly embraces. But those who are men of God open their senses, that is, the gates of the heavenly Jerusalem, so that the word of God may enter into them. Of the evil gates this is an example: You who lift me up from the gates of death. Of the good, this: That I may announce all your praises in the gates of the daughter of Zion. When you see a lover of pleasures rather than a lover of God, and one given over to luxury, say of him at once: He has opened the gates of his land to his enemies; for it is not to the friends of their soul, but to the enemies, that they open the gates of the land of Nineveh. And if those too who are reckoned princes among the people do the same, you will not fear to say of them also: The leaders of my people have been cast out of the house of their delights. But if you see them hampered by pleasures and by luxury surrounding them on this side and that, having no mercy toward the poor and no concern for the people of God, you will fit to them what follows: Who sleep upon beds of ivory and flow with pleasure on their couches, who eat kids from the flocks and sucking calves from the herds; who drink strained wine and are anointed with the chief ointments; and they suffered nothing over the affliction of Joseph. Moreover, what is said, Fire has eaten your bars, is of this kind: If there seemed to be anything of natural good in your soul which, like bars, could hold back and repel the enemies who were trying to break in through the gates of your senses, this has been burned up by the Babylonian fire. Also, Draw to yourself water of the fortification, is said of the word of God, so that the soul may surround herself with the teaching and reason of the Scriptures as with a most firm wall, lest the enemy be able to break into her inner parts. Hold, he says, your fortifications. Whatever good you have in you by nature, which preserves in you the beginning given by the best Creator, this, unhappy soul, Nineveh, hold for your defense, and do not let it flow out of the ruling part of the heart. As for what is added after this, Walk into the clay, and be trodden in the straw, another perhaps may think it is said of the soul which, fixed in the clay of the body and the straw of this world, empty, that is, and perishable, turning itself about wholly, is trodden down by demons. But to me this seems to be said to her: Bear the temptations, the injuries to which you have been handed over, the punishments which you suffer by your own desert; for if you walk upon the empty and fragile dealings of this world, know that you are bearing them as a remedy, if only you prevail over the brick and subjugate the flesh to the rule of the soul. Then follows: There fire will eat you. If you are not over the brick and do not have dominion over the flesh, but remain in the brick, and love the straw, and live in the flesh according to the flesh, not only will the burning darts of the enemy devour you, but his sword too will kill you, and like locusts it will consume with greedy tooth whatever in you seemed green and sprouting of its own accord from the good of nature, and as the cankerworm, its flight lost and weighed down by its own weight, so you too, weighed down by the burden of sins, will be dragged down to the earth. Therefore, lest you suffer such and so great things, multiply like the cankerworm, and as great as its number is, so many virtues must you have. LXX: The cankerworm rushed in and flew out; your commingled one leapt out like the attelabus, like the locust that goes up upon the hedge on the day of frost; the sun rose, and it leapt out, and did not know its place. It seems to me that the multitude of Nineveh, without a ruler, mingled together from here and there without order, and rushing wherever impulse carries it, is compared to the cankerworm, a small and innumerable creature, which seems to raise itself only a little from the ground. And the attelabus too, which in Greek is called συμμικτὸς and in Latin has been translated as 'commingled one', we can understand as the ignoble rabble and a people gathered from here and there out of diverse nations, that is, not citizens but foreigners. Hence the people of Israel too, as it went out of Egypt, is related to have had πολὺν σύμμικτον (a great mixed multitude), that is, of Egyptians and Ethiopians and various nations. This mixed one of Nineveh, then, so to speak, is compared to the leaping attelabus and to the locust, which on the day of cold, because it cannot fly away, settles on the hedge, and afterwards, when the sun has risen, warmed by its heat leaps out, and flying away to other regions by no means remembers the hedge on which it had sat in the time of cold. Let these things be said παραφραστικῶς (by way of paraphrase), so that the prophet's discourse itself may be more easily understood. However, you will not be able to hesitate to call cankerworm the multitude of men which, going along the broad way, busies itself in the world, when you see them wholly given to the earth, running about hither and thither with fickleness of opinion, and unable to fly to higher things. Look at Rome, and at Constantinople exchanging its former poverty together with its earlier name. See Alexandria, the head of Egypt, and when a riot has been stirred up, either for scarcity of the grain supply or, what is matter for shame and blushing, on account of charioteers and mimes and actors, see the people rush like the cankerworm and, clinging wholly to vices, flit hither and thither through its fickleness and its change of opinion from moment to moment; then truly you will be able to say: The cankerworm went off with a rush and flew away. Moreover, as to what follows, The attelabus leapt out, and your mixed one like the locust, I think the mixed one differs from the cankerworm in this, that the cankerworm is compared to the unskilled and innumerable multitude, but the mixed one is gathered from here and there out of all nations. And just as in cities some are citizens and others foreigners, who are pleased to live in a city not their own, so I think that the mixed one who dwells in Nineveh are those who seem to themselves, in their own opinion, to follow some dogmas of truth, and to be better than the cankerworm in this, that the cankerworm does nothing else but stay always on the ground and, without wings, serve its food and its belly; while the attelabus at least takes on small wings, and though it cannot fly on high, still strives to leap up from the ground, and at length, coming to be a locust, it flies about indeed, but its flight is not lasting; for when its little wings fail and are stiffened by the cold, even the locust settles, and settles not on a fruitful tree and on green leaves, but on a hedge woven of brambles and brushwood, or on a dry-stone wall put together from stones picked up at random here and there. Let us consider the wise men of Greece, and of the Egyptians and Persians, and the Gymnosophists of the Indians, and the Samaritans, and the various opinions among them, and the Jews and their Pharisees, and the Sadducees, and the manifold heresies of the Church, and we shall see the attelabus lifting itself a little from the ground, and the locust flying indeed, but not at full course, and, because it does not have the heat of the sun of righteousness, its love toward God growing cold, sitting in thorny hedges. For all their dogmas, since they are cold and cannot fly, find a seat and rest for themselves among the thickets of Aristotle and Chrysippus. From there Eunomius brings forth: What was born did not exist before it was born. From there Manichaeus, to free God from the creation of evils, introduces another author of evil. From there Novatus withdraws pardon, so as to take away repentance. And to sum up everything at once in a brief word, all the dogmas draw the streams of their argumentations from those springs, so that they have even entitled the very places from which arguments are taken τοπικὰ (Topics). This locust, then, which now sits in the hedges, when the time of judgment has come and the world has grown warm at the rising of the sun, will leave its seat and its places to which it clung in the time of cold, and, turned to better things, will not remember its former seat. But what we have said generally of the time of judgment can be understood in part even now: that through learned and erudite men the light of the sun of righteousness may rise for locusts of this kind, and that, leaving their thorns, they may pass into a pure and free flight.
