Commentary on the Epistle to the Ephesians

Latin source: Jerome's commentary on the Epistle to the Ephesians, in three books with a prologue and a preface to each later book (written at Bethlehem about 386–388 for Paula and Eustochium), from Vallarsi's text as printed in J.-P. Migne, Patrologia Latina 26 (Paris, 1845), cols. 439–553, verified against the page images and collated with Vallarsi's edition (Venice, 1769). Scripture is translated as Jerome quotes it, from his own Latin; Psalms keep the Latin (LXX) numbering. The Scripture references are the editors', not Jerome's, and are given corrected where Migne misprinted them. The words of Ephesians that Jerome expounds and the other Scripture he quotes are in italics. Greek and Hebrew are kept as printed. The footnotes give the alternative readings printed in Migne and, at the few places where its text is corrupt, the reading followed, with its witnesses (Rufinus, Origen's Greek fragments, and Heine's translation of Pieri's critical text). This English translation is released into the public domain.

Prologue

If there is anything, Paula and Eustochium, that holds a wise man steady in this life and persuades him to remain with an even mind amid the pressures and whirlwinds of the world, I reckon it to be, first of all, the meditation and knowledge of the Scriptures. For since we differ from the other living creatures in this above all, that we are a rational animal and are able to speak, and since all reason and speech is contained in the divine books, through which we both learn God and are not ignorant of why we were created, I am amazed that some have arisen who either give themselves over to idleness and sleep and are unwilling to learn things that are most excellent, or think that others who have this pursuit deserve to be reproved. Though I could answer them more sharply, and briefly send them away, either offended or appeased, by saying that it is much better to read the Scriptures than to gape after increasing and heaping up wealth, I will say this, which I could make good even before the most unjust judge: that my leisure pleases me, and solitude seems to me more delightful than any crowd. And just as I do not reprove, do not condemn what they do, so they ought to grant me my trifles. I am not eloquent enough: what is that to you? Read someone more eloquent. I do not render Greek into Latin worthily: either read the Greeks (if you have knowledge of that language), or, if you are only a Latin, do not pass judgment on a free gift, nor, as the common proverb has it, look a gift horse in the teeth. Do I lay hands on you and drag you into court because you do not write1 our works? Anyone less skilled than I will read me; your sayings, perhaps, if you write them, Tully will admire. Did Tertullian deter the blessed martyr Cyprian from writing, or Cyprian Lactantius, or Lactantius Hilary? I say nothing of the other small fry who chatter along with me in their books. Unless there are small things as well, great things cannot stand out. Nothing is called first unless a second and a third follow. We do not climb to the top unless we step through the lowest places. Therefore I beseech both you who are here at present and the holy Marcella, the unique model of widowhood, not to hand over my little works readily to slanderers and the envious, nor to give what is holy to dogs and cast pearls before swine (Matt. 7). Since they cannot imitate good things, they do the only thing they can: they envy; and they think themselves learned and erudite in this, if they disparage others. I beseech you to answer them that they should take up the pen themselves, join three words together, as the saying goes, sweat a little, put themselves to the test, and learn from their own labor to pardon those who labor. For you yourselves know that you drove me to this work of explanation against my will and holding back. Not that from my youth I ever ceased either to read or to question learned men about what I did not know, or that I have had only myself for a teacher, as most do. Indeed, recently I went to Alexandria for this reason above all, to see Didymus and to ask him about everything I found doubtful in all the Scriptures. But it is one thing to compose books of one's own, for example on avarice and on faith, on virginity, on widows, and on each subject to join worldly eloquence to testimonies of the Scriptures sought out here and there, and to toss about a showy style almost in commonplaces; it is another thing to enter into the mind of a prophet and an apostle, to understand why they wrote, by what reasoning they established their view, what is peculiar in the Old Law to the Edomites, Moabites, Ammonites, Tyrians, Philistines, Egyptians and Assyrians; what, again, in the New Testament to the Romans, Corinthians, Galatians, Philippians, Thessalonians, Hebrews, Colossians, and to the Epistle to the Ephesians that we now have in hand. For they must have differing occasions, arguments and origins, according to the differences of the places and times and of the people to whom they were written. And just as the blessed John in his Apocalypse, writing to the seven Churches, in each of them either reproves particular vices or approves particular virtues, so too the holy apostle Paul heals the wounds inflicted in each several Church, and does not wish, after the manner of an unskilled physician, to cure everyone's eyes with one salve. And since a few days ago, at your request, we set out what we thought on the Epistle to the Galatians, we must now pass on to the Ephesians, the middle epistle of the Apostle, in meaning as in order. I call it the middle, not because, following the first ones, it is greater than the last, but in the way that the heart of a living creature is in the middle: so that from this you may understand with how great difficulties and how deep questions it is entangled. He was writing to Ephesians who worshipped Diana, not this huntress who holds a bow and is girt up, but that many-breasted one whom the Greeks call πολύμαστον, so that, namely, from her very image too they might falsely claim that she is the nurse of all beasts and living things. He was writing, moreover, to the metropolis of Asia, a city in which idolatry and, what always follows idolatry, the deceptions of the magic arts had so flourished that Demetrius said: and the temple of the great goddess Diana will be reckoned as nothing, and her greatness too will be destroyed, which all Asia and the whole world worships (Acts 19:27). In fact the Apostle stayed there three years, preaching the Gospel of God night and day, so that, once the citadel of idolatry was destroyed, the temples of the lesser cities might easily be taken. Scripture itself reports this very thing, how Paul addresses the Ephesians, saying: Therefore watch, remembering that for three years night and day I did not cease with tears to admonish each one of you; and now I commend you to God, and to the word of his grace, who is able to build up and to give you an inheritance among all the sanctified (Acts 20:31, 32). For they needed the Apostle's commendation to God, they whom the error of demons had held for so long; and they knew that there were certain spiritual powers, and had recognized a certain likeness of divinity in entrails and auguries and divinations. Hence he says to them again: Wherefore I testify to you this day that I am clean from the blood of all. For I did not hold back from declaring to you the whole will of God. And in another2 place: How I held back nothing of what was profitable to you, but declared it, and taught publicly and from house to house (ibid., 20). He had read in Ezekiel (ibid., 26, 27) that the watchman who had not forewarned the people of the coming of the enemy would have the blood of the citizens required at his hands; and therefore he recalled that he had declared the whole will of God and all things that would be useful to them, so that he might be free of their blood. And that you may know that those who had believed at Ephesus had been bound by demonic illusions and the arts of magic, it is recounted in the same Acts of the Apostles, where it says: Many also of those who had practiced curious arts brought their books together and burned them before all; and they reckoned up their price and found it fifty thousand pieces of silver: so mightily did the word of the Lord grow and gain strength (Acts 19:19). We have recounted all these things for this reason: to show why the Apostle, in this Epistle above all, gathered together obscure meanings and mysteries unknown to the ages, and taught about the might of the holy and the opposing powers: who the demons are, what they can do, what they were before, and how after the coming of Christ they were overthrown and destroyed. Of them he says: We have no struggle against flesh and blood, but against principalities and powers, against the rulers of this darkness, against the spiritual forces of wickedness in the heavenly places (Eph. 6:12). And in another place: But I will remain at Ephesus until Pentecost. For a great and effective door has been opened to me, but there are many adversaries (1 Cor. 16:8, 9). Now he remained at Ephesus until Pentecost, a time of gladness and victory, in which we do not bend the knee or bow down to the ground, but rising with the Lord are lifted up to the heights of heaven. And he remained because a door was open to him, and not a small door but a great one, so that, having bound and overcome the strong man, he might invade his house, plunder it, overturn it, and lead captivity captive (Matt. 12); and that strong man, gathering the columns of his henchmen, fought against the Apostle with his whole battle line, and was conquered. What sort of men the Ephesians were is proved most of all from this as well: that just as John, who was to relate the mystery of the Lord's ancient birth, reclined on his breast and drew a stream of teachings from the purest spring (John 13 and 21), so these too, falling on the neck of Paul as he departed (Acts 20), showed by their kisses and embraces that they had one treasure of knowledge with him, and bore witness with tears to their longing for their master. This too I point out in the preface, so that you may know that Origen wrote three volumes on this Epistle, and we too have followed him in part; and that Apollinaris and Didymus also published some short commentaries, from which we have culled a few things, it is true, and we have added or taken away some things as seemed good to us, so that the studious reader may recognize at the very outset that this work is partly another's and partly our own.

Book One

1:1

Paul, apostle of Jesus Christ, through the will of God.

If the preposition through (per) shows the ministry of the one through whom a thing is done, this meaning seems to me to accord with that statement: No one comes to me unless my Father has drawn him to me (John 6:44). And so it will come about that this too which is read in John's Gospel: All things were made through him (John 1:3), doubtless through the Son, does not prejudice our Lord Jesus Christ, as though he were less because he obeys the Father's will; just as now too it does not prejudice the Father that Paul is an apostle of Jesus Christ through his will. Others, however, understand this passage so as to think that Paul is shown to be an apostle of Jesus Christ through the will of God, which (they say) is Christ: so that, just as he is called wisdom and Word and power of God, and truth and resurrection and way, so he is also named will. Some think it makes no difference whether he said through (per) the will of God or from (ex) the will of God, because the Apostle uses these prepositions indifferently; and that "through the will of God" signifies the same as if he had said from the will of God.

To all the saints who are at Ephesus. Some, more curiously than is necessary, think, from the fact that it was said to Moses: These things you shall say to the sons of Israel: He who is has sent me (Exod. 3:14), that those too who are saints and faithful at Ephesus are named by the word for essence: so that, just as from the Holy One men are called holy, from the Just One just, from the Wise One wise, so from him who is these are called those who are; and that, according to the same Apostle, God chose the things that were not, to destroy the things that were. The very Scripture adduced in testimony they also expound in such a way that we are to understand the destruction of the things that were from what follows: That no flesh, he says, may glory in the sight of God (1 Cor. 1:29). For if, they say, anyone who is advancing, by God's doing, from what was not into being what is3 does not return thanks to the giver, but reckons that he subsists by his own merit and virtue, at once his being what he is is destroyed, and what he had been begins not to be. Others, however, think it is written simply, not to those who are4, but to those who are saints and faithful at Ephesus.

And to the faithful in Christ Jesus. Since the unbelieving wife is sanctified in the faithful brother, and the unbelieving husband is sanctified in the faithful wife (1 Cor. 7:14); and there are also holy vessels, and dumb beasts that are slain for God in the temple (Num. 4 and 31): therefore those whom he had called saints he also called faithful; because faith comes down from the choice of one's own mind, whereas into sanctification we are sometimes taken up, without our will, by the bounty of the one who sanctifies. And as for his saying to the faithful in Christ Jesus, this too has been set down to mark a distinction of the most careful observance. For there are many faithful, but not in Christ Jesus. If someone returns a deposit, if someone does not deny what was entrusted to him, shows himself a faithful friend, is ready to give up his life rather than betray his faith (which we read of some philosophers, and, lest they should glory too much in it, an Athenian harlot accomplished it too), that man is indeed faithful, but he is not faithful in Christ Jesus.

1:2

Grace to you and peace from God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ.

Either both are to be referred to both, that is, both grace and peace to God the Father as well as to our Lord Jesus Christ; or each is to be referred to each, so that grace is referred to God the Father, and peace to Christ. For there follows at once: To the praise of the glory of his grace, in which he glorified us in the Beloved; so that the grace of the Father lies in this, that he deigned to send the Son for our salvation; and the peace of the Son in this, that through him we have been reconciled to the Father, and that, having destroyed the middle wall and making void the enmities in his flesh, he made both one.

1:3

Blessed be the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, who has blessed us in every spiritual blessing in the heavenly places, in Christ.

Just as God, who is good by substance and nature, has made us good by communion with himself, and says to Israel: Be holy, for I am holy (Lev. 20:7), so he who is blessed grants us to be blessed. For the lawgiver gives blessings, and the blessing of the Lord is upon the head of the righteous (Prov. 10); from this blessing Baruch has his name, which in our language means "blessed." And he has blessed us not in one blessing but in all. Not that we all obtain all things; but while each of us has either single blessings or several out of all of them, we all possess them all through the individual ones. And not in earthly blessings, but in spiritual ones. For these are earthly blessings: for someone to have children, to abound in wealth, to rejoice in honor and health; and this earthly blessing comes down even to the irrational beasts. For it was said of them: The Lord blessed them, and said: Increase and multiply (Gen. 1:22). But spiritual blessings are in the heavenly places, because the earth does not hold a spiritual blessing. Indeed, those blessings too which are promised in Leviticus to those who keep God's precepts (Lev. 26), for example, to lend at interest to foreign nations, to have granaries full of grain, to be blessed in the cities (Deut. 28), blessed in the fields, and the rest like these, we shall not see5 fulfilled in the prophets, men who wandered about in sheepskins and goatskins, in want, in distress, in desert mountains, in caves and caverns of the rocks, avoiding the onslaughts of persecutors (Heb. 11). All things, therefore, are to be taken spiritually, and spiritual things are to be awaited not in earthly places but in heavenly ones. But as for his saying: He has blessed us in every spiritual blessing in the heavenly places, as though it had already been done in the past and not promised for the future, that is, "he has blessed," not "he will bless," the question is how he has blessed us with a heavenly blessing while we are still set upon the earth. Either, then, because our way of life is in heaven, and we are not of this world, but, having laid aside the image of the earthy, we bear the image of the supercelestial; and we do not live in the flesh but in the spirit; and we lay up treasure for ourselves in heaven, where we also have our heart: for this reason we are now said to be blessed with a heavenly blessing; or surely because every spiritual blessing in Christ, although it is on earth, is nevertheless reckoned as belonging to the heavenly places. He has blessed us, he says, in every spiritual blessing in the heavenly places in Christ: in the word of God, and wisdom, and truth, and the other virtues. It is to be read in two ways: Blessed be the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ; either so that it is "Blessed be God," who is the Creator of all things, and the pause comes here, and next is added: who is also the Father of our Lord Jesus Christ; or thus: so that "God and Father" is referred jointly to our Lord. Blessed be the God of the man who was assumed, and the Father of him who in the beginning was God the Word with God. Not that the man assumed is one and the Word that assumed him another; but that one and the same is proclaimed, according to the variety of circumstances, now as lofty, now as lowly.

1:4

Even as he chose us in him before the foundation of the world, that we might be holy and spotless before him.

For "foundation of the world" (constitutio mundi) the Greek has written πρὸ καταβολῆς κόσμου; but καταβολὴ does not mean the same thing that constitutio means. Hence we too, because of the poverty of our language and the novelty of the subject, and because, as someone says, the Greeks have both a broader speech and a happier tongue, shall try not so much to translate word for word, which is impossible, as to unfold the force of the word by a kind of circumlocution. Καταβολὴ is properly said when something is thrown down and sent from on high into a lower place, or when some thing takes its beginning. Hence those too who lay the first foundations of buildings that are to be are said καταβεβληκέναι, that is, to have thrown down the beginnings of the foundations. So Paul, wishing to show that God contrived all things out of nothing, referred to him not a founding, not a creation and a making, but καταβολὴν, that is, the beginning of a foundation: so that it is not, as Manichaeus and the other heresies hold (which posit a maker and matter), that something6 out of which the creatures were made preceded the creatures, but all things came to subsist out of nothing. And that he testified that we were chosen before the fabric of the world, that we might be holy and spotless before him, that is, before God, belongs to the foreknowledge of God, to whom all future things are already done, and all things are known before they come to be. Just as Paul himself, too, is predestined in his mother's womb, and Jeremiah is sanctified in the womb (Jer. 1), is chosen, strengthened, and sent as a prophet to the nations in the type of Christ. But another, who tries to show that God is just, in that he chooses each one not from the prejudgment of his own knowledge but from the merit of the chosen, says that before the visible creatures, heaven, earth, the seas and all things that are within them7, there were other, invisible creatures, among them also souls, which for certain causes known to God alone were cast down into this valley of tears, into the place of our affliction and pilgrimage; set in this place, the saint prayed that he might return to his former seat, saying: Woe is me, that my sojourning has been prolonged! I have dwelt with the inhabitants8 of Kedar; my soul has long been a pilgrim (Ps. 119:5). And in another place: Wretched man that I am: who will free me from the body of this death? And: It is better to return and to be with Christ. And elsewhere: Before I was humbled, I sinned (Ps. 118:67), and other things like these. So, they say, before the souls were cast headlong into the world, and the world came to be out of souls9 together with its inhabitants, itself cast down to the lowest place, God chose Paul and those like him, who were holy and spotless, before himself. But no one is chosen except out of many; and where there are some of lesser worth, there a choice is made. And just as in the Babylonian captivity, when the people was carried off by Nebuchadnezzar into Chaldea, the prophets Ezekiel, Daniel, the three youths, Haggai and Zechariah were sent, not that they too had deserved captivity, but to be a solace to the captives; so too, in that casting down of the world, those who had been chosen by God before the world came to be were sent for the instruction and teaching of sinful souls, so that at their preaching these might return to the place from which they had fallen; and this, they say, is what is said by Moses in the eighty-ninth psalm: Lord, you have become our refuge in generation and generation: before the mountains were made firm, and the earth and the world came to be (Ps. 89:1). That is, before the world came to be and the whole of generation took its beginning, God was a refuge for his saints. But as for his saying that we might be holy and spotless before him, between holy and spotless there is this difference: one who is holy can also be understood to be spotless, but one who is spotless is not at once holy as well. For little children are spotless, because with their body intact they have committed no sin; and yet they are not holy, because holiness is acquired by will and effort. Also, he can be called spotless who has not committed sins, but holy he who is full of virtues, according to what is written in a certain psalm: He who walks without spot and works righteousness (Ps. 14:2). And in the Song of Songs: You are all beautiful, my neighbor, and there is no spot in you (Song 4:7). The question is how anyone can be holy and spotless before God, when the prophet testifies, saying: Every one living shall not be justified in your sight. For either the Ephesians are holy and spotless before God, and what is said is false: Every one living shall not be justified in your sight. Or, if no one is justified in the sight of God, what went before is false, that they are holy and spotless in the sight of God. To this a twofold answer must be given. For Paul does not say: He chose us before the foundation of the world when we were holy and spotless; but: he chose us that we might be holy and spotless, that is, that we who were not holy and spotless before might be so afterwards. This can also be said of sinners converted to better things, and that sentence will stand: Every one living shall not be justified in your sight, that is, in his whole life, in all the time that he has lived10 in this world. Understood this way, it also tells against him who says that the souls were chosen before the world came to be because of their holiness and their having no fault of sins. For, as we have already said above, Paul and those who are like him are not chosen because they were holy and spotless; but they are chosen and predestined so that in their subsequent life, through works and virtues, they may become holy and spotless. Then it must also be understood in this way: that he did not say: Any one living shall not be justified in your sight; but every one living, that is, not all will be justified, but some will be justified.

1:5

In love predestining us to adoption as sons through Jesus Christ unto himself.

It is to be read in two ways, so that "love" is joined either with what precedes or with what follows. With what precedes, thus: that we might be holy and spotless before him in love, and then there follows: predestining us to adoption as sons through Jesus Christ unto himself11. With what follows, however, thus: in love predestining us to adoption as sons through Jesus Christ unto himself. But the Latin language does not bring out the difference between the Greek words προορίσας and ὁρισθέντος. For the former word refers to those who did not exist before, and who were thought of before they came to be, and afterwards came to subsist. But the latter is said of him whom no thought, no will preceded, but who always was, and never received a beginning in order that he might be. Hence it is rightly said now of those who, not having existed before, afterwards came to subsist: προορισθέντες. But of the Son, that is, of our Lord Jesus Christ, it is written in another place ὁρισθέντος, because he was always with the Father, and the Father's will never preceded him in order that he might be. From this it is gathered that the Father always was, that the Son always was, and that in those in whom eternity is coequal the nature is the same. Moreover, this too must be inferred: that although God predestines, or predetermines12, us to adoption as sons through Jesus Christ, yet we cannot13 be sons before we receive the faith and understanding of his Son Jesus Christ. And he indeed is Son by nature, but we by adoption. He was never not Son; we were predestined before we existed, and then received the spirit of adoption when we believed in the Son of God.

According to the pleasure of his will. The word εὐδοκίαν, which the Latin language has rendered "pleasure" (placitum), is among the Greeks compounded of two whole words, ἀπὸ τοῦ Εὖ καὶ τοῦ Δοκεῖν, from "well" and from "pleasing," which we can call "good pleasure" (beneplacitum); because not everything that has pleased can at once also please well, but εὐδοκία, that is, good pleasure, is said only where what has pleased is proved to have pleased rightly. This word the Seventy translators rendered from the Hebrew RESON (רצון), devising new words for new things. Those, therefore, seize an occasion in this passage who think that before the founding of the world souls dwelt with the angels and the other names of the virtues in the heavenly Jerusalem: for, they say, it cannot be understood as either the good pleasure of God or to the praise of his glory and grace that some are born naked, barbarians, slaves, feeble, and others rich, Romans, free, healthy, base-born or noble, in different parts of the world, unless causes have gone before, from whose merits each of the souls has received this lot. And that text written to the Romans, which some think they know, though they do not: Has not the potter power to make from the same lump one vessel for honor, another for dishonor (Rom. 9:21)? they refer to the same sense: that just as a life good or bad, laborious or easy, is lived in vain in this world unless we believe that there is to be a judgment of God, so too the diversity of those born in this world would accuse the justice of God, unless the merits of souls had gone before. For if, they say, we do not take these things thus, it will be neither the good pleasure of God's will, nor to the praise of his glory and grace, that he chose some before the foundation of the world to be holy and spotless and to have adoption through Jesus Christ, and destined others to the lowest condition and to everlasting punishments.

1:6

To the praise of the glory of his grace, in which he graced us in the Beloved.

What praise of the glory of God's grace is it, they say, to grace some in Christ and to prepare others for eternal punishments: to love Jacob before he comes forth from the womb, and to hate Esau before he had committed anything worthy of hatred, unless causes have gone before which prove the righteousness of God? Every grace, therefore, that we obtain is fulfilled unto the glory and praise of him who justified14 us in the Beloved, that is, in our Lord and Savior: because without wisdom, truth, righteousness, peace, redemption and the other virtues no good can be understood. Nor must we suppose that what is found in the Latin manuscripts, in his beloved Son, is what was written, but simply in the Beloved; and if indeed there had been added the beloved of God, or the beloved of the Father, the meaning would be simple and common to everyone's opinion, namely that our Lord Jesus Christ was loved by the Father; but we would grant nothing great to what is proper to the Son, since the Son would be loved in the same way as everything else. For it is said to God15: You love all things, and cast away none of the things that you have made: for you did not create anything while holding it in hatred (Wisd. 11:23). Or if there had been set beside it, in which he graced us in the one beloved above all, every scruple would have been removed, since we know that the patriarchs and prophets and all holy men were loved by God. But now, since he has spoken absolutely, saying in the Beloved, it seems to me that it must be understood with by all supplied. For if Christ, as we have often said already, is wisdom, righteousness, peace, joy, continence and the rest, even those who cannot follow these names of virtues love them, and no one is of such avowed wickedness as not to say that he loves wisdom and righteousness. Just as the opinion of all men agrees concerning the substance of God, that it is divine and is not easily comprehended by anyone's mind, and yet each one errs when he thinks it to be of this kind or of that: so also all love Christ insofar as he denotes the various virtues, although most cannot prove by their deeds what they love. This is the Beloved whom I think is signified in Isaiah too: I will sing to the beloved a song of the beloved of my vineyard (Isa. 5:1); and: A vineyard was made for the beloved; and in the twenty-eighth psalm: The Lord shall crush the cedars of Lebanon, and break them in pieces like the calf of Lebanon, and the beloved is like the son of unicorns (Ps. 28:6).

1:7

In whom we have redemption through his blood, the remission of sins.

He is redeemed who is a captive and who, coming into the power of enemies, has ceased to be free: so also, some say, we are captives in this world and are held under the yoke of slavery beneath the principalities and powers, and cannot free our hands bound with chains and lift our eyes upward until a redeemer has come. But who, they say, is this one, so great and of such a kind, that he could redeem the whole world at his own price? Jesus Christ, the Son of God, gave his own blood, and snatching us from slavery he presented us with freedom. And truly, if we believe the histories of the Gentiles, that Codrus and Curtius and the Decii Mures checked the plagues and famines and wars of cities by their deaths, how much more must this be judged possible in the Son of God, that by his blood he has cleansed not one city but the whole world! The blood and flesh of Christ are understood in two ways: either that spiritual and divine flesh of which he himself said: My flesh is truly food, and my blood is truly drink (John 6:56); and: Unless you eat my flesh and drink my blood, you will not have eternal life (ibid., 54); or the flesh and blood, the flesh which was crucified and the blood which was shed by the soldier's lance. According to this division, a difference of blood and flesh is also understood in his saints, so that one flesh is that which will see the salvation of God, and another the flesh and blood which cannot possess the kingdom of God. Consistently, moreover, after the redemption of the blood of Christ we are written to have received the remission of sins: because unless we have been redeemed, sins are forgiven us in vain. Nor can we receive pardon for our offences and cease to be slaves until the once bloody victor has received the price for us.

1:8

According to the riches of his grace, by which16 he abounded in us.

He who understands what was said: By grace you have been saved, and not of works (Eph. 2), and the debtor of fifty and of five hundred denarii in the Gospel (Luke 7), namely that he to whom more is forgiven loves more: he can know that the grace of God has abounded in us according to its riches; above all in the Church gathered from the Gentiles, which was a stranger to the covenant and promises of Israel, through whose offence we have obtained salvation. Is there not a greatness of graces in Paul and in the other saints, of whom it is said: Do you not know that we shall judge angels (1 Cor. 6:3)? and in another place: Into whom the angels desire to look (1 Pet. 1:12); and again: Father, grant that, as I and you are one, so they too may be one in us (John 17:21). He does not make these riches of graces empty in himself who, as far as human frailty is able, strives, labors and contends, and says with the Apostle: His grace in me was not empty (1 Cor. 15). But he who, not remembering the greatness of the benefit, slips down to worse things: in him the rich grace of God and its opulent bounty are thinned away by poverty.

1:9

In all wisdom and prudence making known to us the mystery of his will.

That wisdom (sapientia) and prudence (prudentia) are different the Stoics also hold, saying: "Wisdom is the knowledge of things divine and human; prudence, however, of mortal things only." According to this division we can take wisdom as concerning things invisible and visible, but prudence as concerning visible things only. It is asked, therefore, how God has made known to us in all wisdom and prudence the mystery of his will. And indeed it is first to be taken simply that the mystery of his will is our redemption through the blood of his Son, and the remission of sins, according to the riches of his grace, by which17 he abounded in us: namely, that we, believing in the Lord's passion (which is foolishness to those who do not believe), possess wisdom and prudence. Next, that through his Scriptures he has made known to us all the mysteries: how he first contrived heaven and earth, and made, ordered and distinguished all things that are within them; how man was formed, and the world was filled up to the passion of Christ; how from visible things those that are invisible are known. Lastly, that even the things that are on earth need wisdom and prudence. For how few of us know what it is that holds birds suspended in the air and fish in the waves, and moves forward the steps of men, and rouses the rage of beasts? But the diligent reader will at once object to us: If Paul knows in part and prophesies in part, and now sees through a mirror in an enigma (1 Cor. 13), how has the mystery of God been revealed either to him or to the Ephesians in all wisdom and prudence? By this necessity, therefore, we are compelled to change the order of the reading and to make it: According to the good pleasure of his will, to the praise of the glory of his grace, in which he graced us in the Beloved; in whom we have redemption through his blood, the remission of sins, according to the riches of his grace, by which he abounded in us in all wisdom and prudence, so that, when we have marked the division up to this point (so, that is, that all wisdom and prudence are joined to what precedes), we then go on: making known to us the mystery of his will according to his good pleasure. But even with this sense remaining, namely that they see in part and prophesy in part, it can be said that the mystery has now been revealed to them in all wisdom and prudence. Not that they themselves learned the mystery in all wisdom and prudence, but that God, in all his wisdom and prudence, revealed the mystery to them as far as they were able to attain it.

According to his good pleasure, which he purposed in it in the dispensation of the fullness of times. Above he had said, according to the good pleasure of his will; now, according to his good pleasure, with the will taken away. There he set down προορισμὸν, that is, predestination unto adoption as sons through Jesus Christ: here, however, πρόθεσιν, that is, purpose (propositionem). Between predestination (prædestinatio) and purpose (propositum), moreover, those who are accustomed to distinguish between words assert that there is this difference: that predestination prefigures, long beforehand, in the mind of the one who destines, what something is going to be; whereas purpose is when the contriving is already near and the effect almost follows the thought. When he says, he purposed in it, it is to be referred to the mystery: since above he had said: that he might make known to us the mystery of his will, which mystery is the dispensation of the fullness of times, that all things may be completed at the appointed time. For just as the heir, as long as he is a little child, although the goods are his own, is nevertheless not yet owed the inheritance (Gal. 4): so also the mystery which had been predestined by God unto the adoption of his sons could not be dispensed beforehand, unless it came in its own time. Of this Paul also speaks elsewhere, in the singular number: But when the fullness of time came, God sent his Son (ibid., 4:4): who could not come before, unless the mystery of time were fulfilled.

1:10

To recapitulate all things in Christ, the things in the heavens and the things on earth18, in him.

For recapitulate (recapitulare) the Latin manuscripts have written restore (instaurare)19. And I wonder why the translators did not use the Greek word itself, since by a license of this kind the words dialectic and philosophy have been taken over just as they are found in Greek. For orators too, in their epilogues, or before20 the epilogues, at the end of their cases, for the sake of the memory of the judges and of those who have heard the business, are accustomed to make a recollection, that is, ἀνακεφαλαίωσιν, so that what they had earlier argued at greater length they may afterwards comprise in a brief speech, and each one may begin to recall what he has heard. The sense in the present place, then, is this: The whole dispensation which began to be both before the world and afterwards in the world, of creatures both invisible and visible, was promising the coming of the Son of God. That Adam, who was cast out of paradise, was to be called back through the Savior (Gen. 3). That in the building of the tower the unity of tongues was split (Gen. 11) foreshadowed the gifts of tongues in the Acts of the Apostles (Acts 2). That Isaac, as a type of the Lord, himself carried his own cross (Gen. 22). That Samson, nourishing his holy hair, loved the embraces of poor Delilah, and confessed all the secrets of his heart (Judg. 16), signified the mystery of the Savior and of the Church from the Gentiles, truly poor and needy (John 19). In the cross of the Lord, therefore, and in his passion all things have been recapitulated, that is, all things have been summed up in this ἀνακεφαλαιώσει. To make this clearer, let us set down an example from everyday usage. For instance: I paid out twenty denarii, then five, and another fifteen; thirty also, and three times ten, I gave at different times. If I wish to sum these up into one, the total of a hundred grows up for me, and in one number I shall have everything that I set down before. So, therefore, all the mysteries and the whole dispensation of old, not only that which was carried out on earth but also that in the heavens, is completed in the passion of Christ. For once Christ has suffered for me, and been buried, and risen again, and ascended as victor to the Father, I have no need of the old count, because I hold all things in one. Pay careful attention: not only all the histories of the Scriptures, which the Holy Spirit recounts as done on earth, but also the things in the heavenly places which are hidden from us, are held recapitulated in the passion of Christ.

1:11

In whom we also have been called by lot: predestined according to the purpose of him who works all things according to the counsel of his will.

The word of inheritance and of lot (sortis), through which we have come into the portion of Christ, shows that we have passed over from one power to another: and, according to what is written in Deuteronomy: When the Most High divided the nations, when he scattered the sons of Adam (Deut. 32:8), that we have been transferred from the dominion of the angels to the portion of the Lord. And they indeed held what belonged to another, either entrusted to them or usurped. But he received back his own: and ascending on high he led captivity captive (Ps. 67), that is, those who had previously been taken captive for perdition he himself took captive for life, to bring them back on high: and in a certain way captivity is taken captive, while through a second captivity those who had earlier been taken captive are set free. It is to be considered that here too προορισμὸς and πρόθεσις, that is, predestination and purpose (propositum), are set together, according to which God works all things according to the counsel of his will. Not that all things that happen in the world are carried out by the will and counsel of God: otherwise evils too could be imputed to God; but that all things which he does, he does by counsel and will, namely because they are full both of reason and of the power of the one who does them. We men wish to do many things by counsel, but the effect by no means follows the will. But no one can resist him, so as to keep him from doing all that he has willed. And he wills whatever things are full of reason and counsel; he wills all to be saved and to come to the knowledge of the truth (1 Tim. 2). But since no one is saved without his own will (for we are of free will), he wills us to will the good, so that, when we have willed it, he too may will to fulfil his own counsel in us.

1:12

That we may be to the praise of his glory, we who hoped beforehand in Christ.

If he had said only21 we hoped, in Christ, and had not put first we hoped beforehand, which in Greek is called προηλπικότες, the sense would be plainer: that those who hoped in Christ were called by lot22 and predestined according to the purpose of him who works all things according to the counsel of his will. But as it is, the addition of the preposition draws us to that understanding which we discussed above, in expounding what is written: Who blessed us with every spiritual blessing in the heavenly places in Christ, as he chose us in him before the foundation of the world, that we should be holy and spotless before him: namely, that just as he has already blessed us with every spiritual blessing in the heavenly places, and chose us before the foundation of the world, so also we may now be said to have hoped beforehand in Christ, from that time when we were chosen and predestined and blessed in the heavenly places. But another, who does not hold this doctrine, that we existed and hoped in Christ before we lived in this body, will transfer23 the understanding to that other sense, so as to say: At the coming of the Lord and Savior, when in his name every knee shall bow, of things in heaven, on earth and under the earth, and every tongue shall confess that the Lord Jesus Christ is in the glory of God the Father (Phil. 2), when all things have been subjected to him, some to be subjected by will, others by necessity, then those who hoped in him before the presence of his majesty will be to the praise of his glory and will be called προηλπικότας, "those who hoped beforehand"24; but those who are found believing by necessity, at that time when not even the devil and his angels will be able to deny him as he reigns, will be hoping, but not to the praise of his glory. And indeed we see this partly fulfilled even now, in that the reward of him who follows God by will is one thing, and that of him who follows by necessity another. Nevertheless, whether in pretense or in truth, only let Christ be proclaimed, provided that both the one who hopes and the one who hopes beforehand know that, according to the difference of their hope, they will receive different rewards.

1:13

In whom you also, having heard the word of truth, the Gospel of your salvation, in whom also, believing, you were sealed with the Holy Spirit of promise.

In whom: no doubt, in Christ. Among the others who have heard, you too, Ephesians, have heard the word of truth, the Gospel of your salvation, believing which you were sealed with the holy spirit of promise. Now according to the order of the reading the sentence seems not to stand, nor is anything given back to answer what was put first, In whom you also, having heard the word of truth, the Gospel of your salvation, because in whom is immediately put a second time; and since it is superfluous, if it is taken out of the middle it can restore the connection to the reading. Paul suffered this from his excessive love of Christ, so that he always named him whom he loved, even superfluously and beyond the order of the sentence. This the diligent reader can find without our help in all his Epistles. Hence here too the in whom put a second time is found to have disturbed the order of the sentence and to have left the earlier proposition hanging. Nor is it small praise of the Ephesians that they heard not preaching but the word of truth. For that there is a great distance between preaching and the word of truth we read in another Epistle as well: And my word, he says, and my preaching were not in persuasive words of wisdom, but in demonstration of the spirit and of power (1 Cor. 2:4). Let us inquire more diligently where else it is written that someone has heard the word of truth, so that by comparing those passages Paul's sense may be clearly opened; and also where the Gospel is named, whether with an addition or absolutely. With an addition, for example: my Gospel (Rom. 16:25), and the Gospel of Jesus Christ (Mark 1:1), and the eternal Gospel (Rev. 14:6); or what is now said to the Ephesians, the Gospel of your salvation. Absolutely, as when he speaks of Luke: Whose praise is in the Gospel throughout all the Churches (2 Cor. 8:18). Now he who has heard the word of truth and has believed in it is sealed with the holy spirit of promise. For there are many who have heard, and, remaining unbelieving, have by no means obtained the seal of the Holy Spirit. It is said also to the prince of Tyre: You are the seal of the likeness of God (Ezek. 28:12). And the seal of God is this: that just as the first man was created after the image and likeness of God, so in the second regeneration whoever has obtained the Holy Spirit is sealed by him and receives the figure of the Creator. We must also inquire where else the holy spirit of promise is written, or what it means. For I think that just as the Holy Spirit makes holy the one into whom he has been poured, and the spirit of wisdom makes him wise, and that of understanding, understanding, and that of counsel, cautious and well-advised, and that of fortitude, brave, and that of knowledge, knowing, and that of piety, pious, and that of fear, fearful and trembling with the fear of God, so also the spirit of promise, or the spirit of God, makes the one in whom he has dwelt a promiser and a god. Just as, on the contrary, an unclean spirit makes a man unclean, and the foul inhabitant prepares a foul house for himself; the spirit of fornication too makes fornicators, of whom the prophet also speaks: They have been led astray by the spirit of fornication (Hos. 4:12); and a wicked spirit makes men wicked and perverse; and a demonic spirit makes them demons; and whatever liquid has been poured into a new jar, the jar long keeps both its smell and its taste.

1:14

Who is the pledge of our inheritance, unto the redemption of adoption, to the praise of his glory.

Pledge (pignus) the Latin translator has put for earnest (arrhabo). But earnest does not mean the same thing as pledge. For an earnest is given as a kind of testimony and binding toward a future purchase. A pledge, that is, ἐνέχυρον, is put down for money lent, so that when the money has been repaid, the pledge is given back by the creditor to the one who repays the debt. Again, where he says unto the redemption of adoption, the Greek does not have υἱοθεσίαν but περιποίησιν, which we can call acquisition (acquisitio) or possession (possessio); and yet we have not expressed the force of the word. For there are many words that cannot be carried over from Greek into Latin, nor from Hebrew into Greek; and conversely, neither from Latin into Greek nor from Greek into Hebrew. Whoever, then, has received not only the Holy Spirit but the Holy Spirit of promise, will at the same time obtain also the earnest of the inheritance, and that inheritance is everlasting life. And just as from the earnest one judges what the purchase will be like and what the property (for example, from ten solidi, a country estate worth a hundred solidi, and from a hundred solidi, a property worth a thousand solidi), so from the difference in the earnest the greatness of the inheritance that is to follow afterwards is also known. And however holy and perfect someone may be, and though he be thought worthy of blessedness in the judgment of all, yet for the inheritance to come he has now obtained the Spirit as an earnest. But if the earnest is so great, how great will the possession itself be? And just as the earnest that is given to us is not outside us but within us, so the inheritance itself, that is, the kingdom of God which is within us, dwells inwardly in us. For what greater inheritance can there be than to contemplate and to see with the understanding the beauty of Wisdom, and of the Word, and of Truth, and of Light, and to consider the ineffable and magnificent nature of God himself, and to behold the substance of all things which25 were created26 after the likeness of God? Now this holy spirit of promise, which is the earnest of our inheritance, is given now to the saints for this reason: that they may be redeemed and joined to God, to the praise of his glory. Not that God needs anyone's praise, but so that the praise of God may profit those who praise him, and that, while through his several works they come to know his majesty and greatness, they may burst out in wondering amazement to praise him.

1:15-17

Therefore I also, hearing of your faith in the Lord Jesus, and toward all the saints, do not cease giving thanks for you, making remembrance of you in my prayers: that the God of our Lord Jesus Christ, the Father of glory, may give you the spirit of wisdom and of revelation in the knowledge of him, the eyes of your heart enlightened.

Although a translation rendered word for word could excuse the Apostle to the diligent reader, in that the whole connection of the sentence seems to teem with faults and a solecism results, yet in the Greek too it sounds like a fault. Hence we also, answering rather the Greek detractors, will try to adjust the order of the reading according to the sense, so that we say: Hearing of your faith in the Lord Jesus, and toward all his saints, and seeing the difference of your faith toward the Lord and toward all his saints, I do not cease to give thanks and to make remembrance of you in my prayers, that the God of our Lord Jesus Christ, but the Father of glory, may give you the spirit of wisdom and of revelation. And what follows, the eyes of your heart enlightened, can be rendered thus by hyperbaton: Therefore I also, hearing of your faith in the Lord Jesus in the knowledge of him, the eyes of your heart enlightened, and toward all the saints, do not cease, giving thanks for you, to make remembrance of you in my prayers, that the God of our Lord Jesus Christ, the Father of glory, may give you the spirit of wisdom and of revelation unto knowledge; that you may know what is the hope of your calling, etc. Further, the God of our Lord Jesus Christ, but the Father of glory, is to be understood thus: that just as our Lord Jesus Christ is himself the word, wisdom, truth, peace, righteousness, fortitude, so he is also glory, according to what is said elsewhere: The glory of God shall appear (Lev. 9:23); and in nearly the whole of the old Law it is written that the glory of God was seen above the tabernacle of the testimony (Num. 9). And the Psalmist sings: Arise, my glory, arise (Ps. 107:3). This glory, enlightening the world with its brightness, built itself a temple from the virginal womb. Of that temple the Father of glory becomes the God. And where Christ is glory, there he is called only Father; where he is Jesus, there the Father is named his God, without any addition. This, then, the God of the man assumed, but the Father of glory, of wisdom, of truth, gives to those who believe in his Son the spirit of wisdom and of revelation, that they may become wise, and with unveiled face may contemplate the glory of the Lord. And when this wisdom and revelation have made them wise and have opened to them the hidden mysteries, it follows at once that they have the eyes of the heart enlightened. From the present words it is proved that, corresponding to the members of the outer man, members of the inner man are also spoken of. For see, he has plainly named the eyes of the heart, which we cannot understand apart from the sense and the mind. With this agrees also that word of the Psalmist: Enlighten my eyes, that I may never sleep in death (Ps. 12:4). And elsewhere: The eyes of the wise man are in his head (Eccles. 2:14). For if we take them simply as the eyes of the flesh, surely the eyes not only of the wise man but also of the fool are in his head. So the head of the wise man is taken to be the sense, which by another word is called the mind (mens), and the mind (animus), and the ruling part of the heart. And as for what he says, in the knowledge of him, that is, ἐπιγνώσει αὐτοῦ, some understand it thus, that between γνῶσιν and ἐπίγνωσιν, that is, between knowing (notio) and knowledge (agnitio), the difference is this: that knowing (notio) is of things which we did not know before and afterwards began to know; but knowledge (agnitio) is of things which, having known them before, we then ceased to know, and afterwards recall; and they surmise a certain former life in the heavenly places, in that, having been cast down into these bodies and having forgotten God the Father, we have now come to know him through revelation, according to that word: All the ends of the earth shall remember and shall turn to the Lord (Ps. 21:28), unfolding27 other things like these.

1:18-20

That you may know what is the hope of his calling, and what the riches of the glory of his inheritance in the saints, and what the sublime greatness of his power toward us who believe28, according to the working of the might of his power, which he wrought in Christ, raising him from the dead.

He to whom, according to Paul's prayer, the spirit of wisdom and of revelation has once been given, so that he looks with the eyes of the heart opened, will know what things are laid up for those who are called, and what the saints of God are to hope for, things which he will give abundantly and generously to those who await his inheritance. For this knowledge the greatness of the power of God is necessary in those who, after the likeness of Paul, are called believers, the power through which God wrought in Christ Jesus, whom he raised again from the dead. Now the inheritance is to be understood in two ways: either that the Firstborn of all creation is the inheritance of the soul of our Lord Jesus Christ, and of the man who was assumed from body and soul, and that we, together with the inheritance of the soul, inherit God the Word; or that our inheritance in Christ is the one divinity of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit; so that, just as the very things which are possessed by heirs are called an inheritance of houses and country estates, so God himself is the inheritance of believers and of the saints. It is written also in the Old Testament: But to the sons of Levi you shall not give an inheritance in the midst of their brothers, for I am their portion (Num. 18:20); and elsewhere: The Lord is their inheritance (Deut. 18:2); and the saint who knows that he has nothing outside God boldly says: My portion is the Lord (Ps. 72:26); and: You are the portion of my inheritance and of my cup (Ps. 15:5). It is therefore a matter of no small study that we should know the hope of the calling and the riches of the glory of God's inheritance in the saints; for to come to know these things we need that power which God also used in raising his Son: whom he raised from the dead not once but always, and made him free among the dead, stained by no contagion of death. Daily Christ rises from the dead; daily he is raised again in those who repent. Not that he did not have, even according to the flesh, the power to lay down his soul and to take it up again (for no one takes it away, unless he lays it down of himself), but in the sense that, according to the dispensation of the flesh and of the son, the man and Son is said to have been raised by God and the Father.

1:21

And making him sit at his right hand in the heavenly places, above every principality, and power, and virtue, and dominion, and every name that is named not only in this age but also in the age to come.

Through a human likeness he has shown the might of God: not that a throne is set up, and God the Father sits on it and has the Son sitting with him; but because we cannot understand him as judging and reigning otherwise than through our own words. To this belongs also what is written in the hundred and ninth psalm: The Lord said to my Lord: Sit at my right hand, until I make your enemies the footstool of your feet (Ps. 109:1). For if the Son sits at the Father's right hand in the manner of one who reigns, it necessarily follows, according to the same likeness, that he is greater than the one who has sat down on the left side. That we may know this is to be understood otherwise than the letter sounds, the same psalm teaches in what follows, saying: You are a priest for ever according to the order of Melchizedek: the Lord at your right hand. For how, when the Son sits at the right hand of the Father, is the Father in turn said to be at his right hand? Or by what reasoning is the earth the footstool of his feet and heaven his throne, when according to Isaiah he is said both to hold the earth in his fist and to measure heaven with the span of his palm (Isa. 40)? For he cannot be within that which is contained by him, nor hold enclosed in his hand that by which he himself, in the posture of one sitting, would be surrounded. Therefore, just as being near to God or withdrawing far from him is to be taken not according to distances of place but according to merits, in that the saints are near him, while sinners, of whom the prophet says: Behold, those who remove themselves far from you shall perish (Ps. 72:27), are kept away from all nearness to him: so also being at the right hand or at the left hand of God is to be understood: that the saints are at his right hand, but sinners at his left, as the Savior also confirms this very thing in the Gospel, when he relates that the sheep are at the right hand and the goats at the left (Matt. 25). But the very word sit also signifies the power of a kingdom, through which God bestows a benefit on those over whom he deigns to sit: namely, that he rules them, and has them in his chariot, and turns at his own nod necks that before were wandering and free. After this it is asked how what follows can be understood: above every principality, and power, and virtue, and dominion, and every name that is named, not only in this age but also in the age to come. And indeed concerning the right and left hands, and concerning the sitting, we have already spoken. Now we must ask where the Apostle found these four names written, I mean principality, and power, virtue, and dominion, and from where he brought them forth into the open. For it is not right to think that one who had been instructed in the divine reading said anything that is not found in the holy books. I think, therefore, that either he brought forth into the open from the traditions of the Hebrews things that are secret, or at least, since he understood that the law is spiritual, he took in a loftier sense the things that are written as though according to history, and recognized in what is related in Numbers and in the books of Kingdoms about kings and princes, and also leaders, tribunes and centurions, an image of other princes and kingdoms: namely, that in the heavenly places there are principalities, there are powers, there are dominions and virtues, and the other names of ministries, which neither we can name, nor, I think, was Paul himself able to enumerate, set as he was in a heavy little body. Now if there are principalities, and powers, and virtues, and dominions, they must also have those who are subject to them, and who fear them, and who serve them, and who are strengthened by their fortitude. These distributions of offices will exist not only at present but also in the age to come, so that through the several advances, and honors, and ascents, and also descents, someone may either grow or decrease, and come to be under one power, virtue, principality and dominion after another. We little men, soon to be dissolved into ash and dust, if by the consent of men we are raised up to be kings, have such diversities and multitudes of ministers as we can more easily perceive than describe: for example, that the Prefect on the civil side has judges, provinces and his own staff; again, that the military is divided into so many counts, generals, tribunes and a manifold army; and do we think that God, the Lord of lords and King of those who reign, is content with a single ministry only?

1:22-23

And he has subjected all things under his feet, and has given him as head over all things to the Church, which is his body: the fullness of him who is filled all in all.

This seems to be contradicted by what is written elsewhere: For we do not yet see all things subjected to him. And also by this: For he must reign until he puts all enemies under his feet (1 Cor. 15:25). For if all things are not yet subjected to him, and he must reign until all things are subjected to him, how has God now subjected the universe under his feet? Especially since Paul himself also testifies in another place: But when all things have been subjected to him, then the Son himself also will be subjected to him who subjected all things to himself, that God may be all in all (1 Cor. 15:28)? Therefore either, according to foreknowledge29, he mentions what is going to be as though it had already been done, in the sense we expounded above, where he says: He has blessed us with every spiritual blessing in the heavenly places in Christ. Or certainly, if it is to be understood of the past, we ought to take it thus: that even those things which are not subject to him by will serve him by the condition of their nature, for example, demons, Jews and Gentiles. For they do not serve Christ, nor are they subjected under his feet; and yet, because they were created by him for good, they are subject to his power against their will, even though they resist him by the will of free choice. To this sense is also fitted what follows: And has given him as head over all things to the Church, which is his body. For just as a head has very many members subjected to it, some of which are faulty and weak, so also our Lord Jesus Christ, since he is the head of the Church, has as his members all those who are gathered in the Church, namely saints as well as sinners: but the saints subjected to him by will, the sinners by necessity. And so it comes about that even enemies are subjected under his feet. Wherefore, in saying all things, he seems to raise a question. For that which was said: Sit at my right hand, until I put your enemies under your feet (Ps. 109:1), and elsewhere: For he must reign until he puts all enemies under his feet (1 Cor. 15:25), does not greatly require an interpreter, namely that the things which are hostile, once they have been overcome, are subjected under his feet and pass into the power of the victor. But why all things, that is, angels, thrones, dominions, powers and the other virtues, which were never opposed to God, should be subjected under his feet, seems obscure. It can therefore be answered that no one is without sin, and that the very stars are not clean before God, and that every creature trembles at the coming of the Creator (Job 15). Hence also the cross of the Savior is said to have cleansed not only the things that were on earth but also those that were in the heavens. Another, however, refers all things not to the whole universe, but only to those things about which the discussion was held; or in this way: "the whole city cried out", not that there was no one in the city who kept silent, but that from the greatest part even the lesser parts are named. And the apostle Paul himself says: All, he says, seek their own, not the things that are God's (Phil. 2:21); and: All have forsaken me. Not that Timothy and the other disciples who were with him at the time when the Epistles were being written had forsaken him; but because he was deserted by many, he complains that he has been deserted by all, that is, by the greatest part. Something similar to this sounds in the psalm too: All have turned aside, together they have become useless: there is none who does good, there is not even one (Ps. 13:3). For if all have turned aside, then he too who speaks has turned aside. And elsewhere: I said in the ecstasy of my mind, every man is a liar (Ps. 115:2). For what he said is either true or false. If every man is a liar, then he too who speaks is a liar. But if he too who speaks is a liar, then not even this which he says is true, that every man is a liar. Further, if the statement is true, "all" must be taken, as we said above, to mean that the great part of men lie. The Apostle also writes elsewhere: Teaching every man (Col. 3:16); and again, admonishing every man: not that he taught all men (for how many there are who to this day have heard neither the Apostle's teaching nor his name!), but that he teaches and admonishes all those who are in the Church and desire to know the things that are God's. There follows: The fullness of him who is filled all in all. This is indeed to be taken in the same way as that other saying: Then he will be subjected to him who subjected all things to himself, that God may be all in all (1 Cor. 15:28). For now God is in individuals in parts: in one as righteousness, in another as chastity, in another as temperance, in another as wisdom, in another as fortitude; and it is difficult, even in holy and perfect men, for all the virtues to be present equally. But when, at the end of things and the consummation of the world, the universe has been subjected to him, he will be filled all in all: so that, in accordance with the fact that God is full of all virtues, he may be filled all in all, and all may have all the things which before they possessed one by one, each his own. But this too which he says, And has given him as head over all things to the Church, which is his body: the fullness of him who is filled all in all, can be understood of a Church not of men only, but also of angels and of all the virtues and rational creatures. Moreover this, Who is filled all in all, is by no means to be taken as it sounds; for he does not say, "Who fills all in all", but, who is filled all in all. For to fill is one thing, to be filled another; because in the one the verb belongs to the one acting, in the other to the one acted upon. Just as, then, an emperor is filled if his army is increased daily, and new provinces are made, and the multitude of his peoples grows, so also our Lord Jesus Christ, in that all things believe in him and come day by day to faith in him, is himself filled in all: yet in such a way that he is filled all in all, that is, that those who believe in him are full of all virtues, and, according to the Gospel, make him advance in age, wisdom and grace, not only with God but also with men (Luke 2).

2:1-5

And you, when you were dead in your trespasses and sins, in which you once walked according to the age of this world, according to the prince of the power of the air, the spirit that now works in the sons of unbelief, in which we also all once lived in the desires of our flesh, doing the wills of the flesh and of the thoughts, and were by nature sons of wrath, even as the rest. But God, who is rich in mercy, because of his great love with which he loved us, even when we were dead in sins, made us alive together with Christ.

Before we discuss the senses of the individual words, it seems to us that the order of the reading should be rendered thus: And you, when you were dead in your trespasses and sins, God, who is rich in mercy, made alive together with Christ, because of his great love with which he loved us; and when we were dead in trespasses, in which we once walked according to the age of this world, according to the prince of the power of the air, the spirit that now works in the sons of unbelief, in which we also all once lived in the desires of our flesh, doing the wills of the flesh and of the minds, and were by nature sons of wrath, he made us alive together with Christ; so that, ἀπὸ κοινοῦ (in common), and he made us alive together with Christ is understood as though said twice. As for the causal conjunction in the place where he says, But God, who is rich in mercy, we judge that it was either added by unlearned scribes, and the fault gradually took root, or else was used superfluously by Paul himself, who was unskilled in speech but not in knowledge (2 Cor. 11). Now, that sin is called the death of the soul is plain from his saying, And you, when you were dead in your trespasses and sins, in accordance also with what is written in Ezekiel: The soul that sins, it shall die (Ezek. 18:4). And since trespasses (delicta), which in Greek are called παραπτώματα, is properly, according to the etymology of that same language, a word of the Scriptures (although delicta would more correctly be translated πλημμέλιαι, "offences"), we ask what they signify, and in what they differ from sins, that is, what difference there is between παραπτώματα and ἁμαρτίας. For they say that παραπτώματα are, as it were, the beginnings of sins, when a thought creeps in silently and, though we connive30 in some measure, has not yet driven us to ruin. Hence also in the eighteenth psalm (v. 13) it is written: Trespasses, that is, παραπτώματα, who understands?, namely because it is difficult to understand the roots and beginnings of sins. But sin, they say, is when something, completed in deed, has come to its end. We also ask what is meant by in which you once walked according to the age of this world: whether there is also another age, which does not belong to this world but to other worlds, of which Clement too writes in his Epistle: "Ocean, and the worlds which are beyond it"? Or whether this is the one world, which rolls on from the beginning of the age in which Adam was made until its end, and passes away; or whether, surely, the prince of this air, who now works in the sons of unbelief, is called by the other name "world"? It is also written to the Galatians: That he might deliver us from the present evil age (Gal. 1:4). And in this same Epistle: Redeeming the time, because the days are evil (below, 5:16). And the days of Jacob are called few and evil (Gen. 47), whether because the time of this life, through which we are held shut in the age, is heavy and laborious, or because Satan himself, as we said above, is called by the name of this world and of the age. Of whom he says immediately in what follows: According to the prince, he says, of the power of the air, the spirit that now works in the sons of unbelief. For by the prince of the air, and the spirit of the power which is in this air, the devil is understood, who now works in the sons of unbelief. For in those who believe the Lord he cannot work. Of him he also says below: We do not wrestle against flesh and blood, but against principalities and powers, against the rulers of this darkness, against the spiritual forces of wickedness in the heavenly places. Not that the devil and his attendants, who, wandering through this world, insinuate sins into men, are able to dwell in heaven, from which they fell for their deserts; but this air which is above us is called heaven, according to the Savior's saying: Consider the birds of heaven (Matt. 6:26), and the rest. For it is plain that birds do not fly through heaven, but through the air. There follows: In which we also all once lived in the desires of our flesh, doing the wills of the flesh and of the minds. What he says, in which (in quibus), is to be referred to the trespasses. For above, since he had set down two things together, And you, when you were dead in your trespasses and sins, and had referred to the sins by saying, in which you once walked, while nothing of the kind seemed to have followed for the trespasses that could complete the sense, he now says: in which trespasses we also all lived; and at the same time, lest in having said, and your sins, in which you once walked, he should seem through pride to exempt himself from sin, he added, in which we also all lived. Now he who says that he has lived so is confessing past trespasses, not present ones. We lived once, he says, not in one desire, but in the desires of our flesh. For the flesh desires against the spirit, and the spirit against the flesh (Gal. 5:17). Doing not one will of the flesh, but many; and not only the wills of the flesh, but also of the minds (mentium), for which in the Latin manuscripts there is of the thoughts (cogitationum). Between the sin of the flesh and that of the minds I think there is this difference: that the sin of the flesh is unchastity and lust, and the things done through its service which are fulfilled in lusts. The trespass of the minds, on the other hand, pertains to dogmas contrary to the truth and to heretical depravity; so that we may say that most heretics (although this is rare) do the wills of the minds and not the wills of the flesh, and that many, on the contrary, among churchmen do the wills of the flesh and not of the minds, and that there are many who do the wills of the flesh and of the minds alike. Now we have said this, not that heretics also do not do the wills of the flesh (for there are more vices of the body among them than among our own), but so that, an example having been set down, what we wished might be more easily understood. And we were, he says, by nature sons of wrath, even as the rest. Let the heretics who contend that there are different natures answer how Paul, who without doubt is of a spiritual nature, was by nature a son of wrath, even as the rest who are still placed in error. We, however, say that all men are at first by nature sons of wrath, either because of the body of humiliation and the body of death, and because from youth the mind of men is set upon wickedness (Gen. 8) (whence Solomon also says: There is no righteous man on earth who does good and does not sin (Eccles. 7:21)); or because from the time when we are able to have knowledge of God and come to puberty, we all sin either in deed, or in tongue, or in thought. We were therefore by nature sons of wrath, even as the rest, and all the Saints, who have been redeemed from wrath by the blood of Christ. For if Paul, who according to the righteousness which was in the Law lived blamelessly, says that he was by nature a son of wrath, why should we fear to testify that holy men of former times also were sons of wrath? Of all of whom it can truly be said: But when the commandment came, sin revived, and they died. Now son of wrath is to be taken in the same way as son of perdition, son of iniquity, son of death. Not that some wrath, death, iniquity and perdition subsists in its own nature, so as to have sons; but that they are called sons of that thing which works in each of them: just as those are called sons of Gehenna who are to be consumed by the fires of Gehenna. Another thinks that they are called sons of wrath as being sons of the devil. For the devil is perdition, and wrath, and death. Death, growing strong, has devoured (Isa. 25:8, LXX). And: Last31 (1 Cor. 15) the enemy, death, shall be destroyed (1 Cor. 15:26); and to the devil himself: You have become perdition (Ezek. 28, LXX). Become, he says, by his own will, not created so from the beginning. As, then, he is called death, from the fact that by the envy of the devil death entered into the world (Wisd. 2), and through him those died who before were living; and perdition, because he destroys whomever he has deceived: so too he is called wrath on account of the savagery which he exercises against man. There are some who think that in the books of Kingdoms, when David numbered the people of Israel, provoking the wrath of God against himself, as Scripture says: And the wrath of God was again kindled against Israel, and it incited David, saying (2 Sam. 24:1), the wrath of the Lord signifies the devil; for, according to the idiom of the Greek language, it did not say, in the feminine gender, the wrath of God λέγουσα, that is, "she who said", but the wrath of God λέγων, that is, "he who said", in the masculine gender: since indeed the Lord sends his wrath and his fury through the worst angels. God therefore, who is rich in mercy, and rich because of his love with which he loved the human race (and a love not simple but great), when we were dead because of our trespasses, made us alive; and not only made us alive (for this was too little for his goodness and greatness), but made us alive with Christ Jesus, granting us to have one and the same life with Christ. Some, in place of what we have now expounded, And we were by nature sons of wrath, have translated, in place of by nature, "utterly" or "altogether", since the word φύσει is ambiguous. And even if it sounds so, it must be expounded according to what we have said.

By grace you have been saved. If the sufferings of this time are not worthy to be compared with the glory to come which shall be revealed in us (Rom. 8), we have been saved by grace rather than by work. For we can render nothing to the Lord for all the things that he has rendered to us.

2:6

And he raised us up together, and at the same time made us sit in the heavenly places in Christ Jesus.

He had said above that God raised Christ from the dead and made him sit at his right hand in the heavenly places, above every principality, and power, and virtue, and dominion, and every name that is named, not only in this age but also in that to come. But now he has added: that he has also raised us with him, and made us sit in the heavenly places at his right hand. The question is therefore asked how God, who saved us and raised us, has made us sit together with Christ. And indeed one who means to answer more simply asserts this: that, according to the foreknowledge of God, he has spoken of what is going to be as though it had already been done; and that this is the custom of the Scriptures, that future things are sometimes inflected in the past tense. For example, of the cross of the Lord: They dug my hands and feet (Ps. 21:17). And elsewhere, of his passion: He was led as a sheep to the slaughter (Isa. 53:7). And further, of the injuries of the scourges: By his bruise we have been healed (ibid., 5). And elsewhere32: Because of the iniquities of my people he was led to death (ibid., 8, LXX). Now this is done so that, because future things are always uncertain, men's hope may not waver and totter: the things which God has known to be future (with whom nothing is doubtful) are related as though already done; so that, since past things, according to the philosophers too, cannot be made undone, those who hear may hold the things that are to come as though already done. Another, however, who understands the resurrection and the kingdom of Christ spiritually, has not hesitated to say that the saints already sit and reign with Christ: for just as the saint is by no means in the flesh though he lives in the flesh, and has his way of life in the heavenly places though he walks on the earth, and, ceasing to be flesh, is wholly turned into spirit: so he sits in the heavenly places with Christ; for the kingdom of God is within us (Luke 17:21), and where our treasure is, there will our heart be also (Matt. 6:21); and firm and steadfast we sit with Christ, with wisdom, the Word, righteousness, truth. But this too can be said: that, just as we have received the earnest of the Holy Spirit without yet having attained his whole fullness, so we also sit and reign with Christ without yet having obtained the perfect session in the heavenly places.

2:7

That he might show in the ages to come the abundant riches of his grace in kindness upon us in Christ Jesus.

How great is the magnitude of the benefit, and how manifold the grace by which the Lord, having freed us from the disturbances of this age, has made us sit and reign with Christ, is proved most of all from this: that in the ages to come, not in one but in all, he is going to show his glory upon us to all rational creatures, and to display his riches. That we, who were once held by the law of hell, and who, because of our vices and sins, were destined for punishments as we were for the works of the flesh, now reign in Christ and sit with him. And we sit not in some lowly place, but above every principality, and power, and virtue, and dominion, and every name that is named, not only in this age but also in that to come. For if Christ, raised from the dead, sits at the right hand of God in the heavenly places, above every principality, and power, and virtue, and the rest, and we sit and reign with Christ, it is necessary that we sit above the things above which he sits. But one who is a careful reader immediately asks and says: What then, is man greater than the angels and all the powers in heaven? Since it is dangerous to answer this, he will refer the principalities and powers, and virtues, and dominions, and every name that is named, not only in this age but also in that to come (especially since all things are subjected under the feet of Christ), not to the good side but to the contrary one: so as to say that they are the renegade angels, and the prince of this world, and Lucifer who rose in the morning, above whom the saints are to sit with Christ at the end, conferring a benefit on them too, who now, unbridled and badly abusing their freedom, wander33 everywhere and rush headlong34 over the precipices of sins. But when they have such riders, they will begin to be steered according to the will of those who sit upon them. Another, however, will transfer what he says, that he might show in the ages to come the abundant riches of his grace in kindness over us, in Christ Jesus, to this understanding: that we have been saved not by our own merit but by his grace, and that it is a sign of greater kindness to die for sinners rather than for the righteous: For perhaps for a good man someone might dare to die; and that he is going to give us the things which eye has not seen, nor ear heard, nor have they entered into the heart of man (1 Cor. 2). All of which he has already given in part in Christ Jesus: because no good can be spoken of apart from Christ.

2:8-9

For by grace you have been made safe through faith, and this not of yourselves: for it is the gift of God; not of works, lest anyone should glory.

Therefore, he says, he is going to show the abundant riches of his grace in kindness in the ages to come, because you have been made safe by grace through faith, not through works. And this very faith is not of you, but of him who called you. Now this is said lest perhaps a hidden thought should creep in upon us: if we have not been saved through our works, surely we have at least been saved through faith, and so in another way it is our own doing that we are saved. He therefore added and said that faith itself, too, is not of our will but of God's gift. Not that free choice is taken away from man, and that, according to that saying of the Apostle to the Romans, it is not of him who runs nor of him who wills, but of God who has mercy (Rom. 9); but that the very freedom of choice has God as its author, and all things are to be referred to his benefit, since he himself has permitted us even to will the good. And all this is so that no one may glory that he has been saved by himself and not by God.

2:10

For we are his workmanship, created in Christ Jesus in good works, which God has prepared, that we should walk in them.

He has given the reasons why we have been saved by grace through faith, and this very thing not of ourselves but of the gift of God, saying: For we are his workmanship; that is, that we live, that we breathe, that we understand and are able to believe, is his, because he himself is our Creator. And observe carefully that he did not say, we are his shaping (figuratio) and moulding (plasmatio): but, we are his workmanship (factura). For moulding draws its origin from the mud of the earth, while workmanship took its beginning according to the likeness and image of God. And these, set down together in the hundred and eighteenth psalm as well, signify different things: Your hands made me and moulded me (Ps. 118:73). Workmanship holds the first place; then moulding. And since the name of creation and of founding is always wont to be joined to great works (for example: that city was founded, and the world was created from the beginning; and each one of the saints, through various doctrines and virtues, is a whole world in himself), therefore we are now said to be created in Christ, and created in good works, whether those which we ourselves have done or are going to do, or in other creatures, to which our way of life is to be transferred, so that we may walk in the things which God has prepared; great hope having already been given us, while we are going to walk in those things which God has prepared so greatly. And since we have once come to the name of creature, and Wisdom in the Proverbs of Solomon says that she was created the beginning of the ways of God (Prov. 8), and many, for fear lest they be compelled to call Christ a creature, deny the whole mystery of Christ, so as to say that it is not Christ who is signified in this wisdom but the wisdom of the world: we freely proclaim that there is no danger in calling him a creature whom, with the whole confidence of our hope, we profess to be a worm, and a man, and crucified, and a curse; especially since, two little verses before, Wisdom herself promises that she will speak of the things which are after the ages (Prov. 8:21). But since Christ made the ages, and what she says next are the things which he promised he would speak of after the ages, the things that follow are to be referred to the mystery of the incarnation, not to the nature of God; although in the Hebrew manuscripts it does not read: The Lord created me the beginning of his ways, but, The Lord possessed me. Now between possession and creation there is a great difference: because he who is possessed certainly exists and subsists, and he who is possessed is one's own. But he is created35 who did not exist before he came to be; or certainly he is transferred from what he was into something else, just as we too are now said to be created in Christ Jesus. Created, certainly, not because we did not exist before, but created in good works. This David too prays for in the fiftieth psalm, saying: Create a clean heart in me, O God (Ps. 50:11). And certainly he had had a clean heart before his sin, when the Lord said of him: I have found David the son of Jesse after my own heart (Acts 13:22); but as there creation means renewal, so also in us and in Christ, through individual works and advances, creation and founding can be understood: so that daily in believers (who, variously according to their different merits, are called mountains, and valleys, and hills, and plains) Christ is created, born and founded.

2:11-12

Wherefore be mindful that once you, Gentiles in the flesh, who were called uncircumcision by that which is called circumcision in the flesh, made by hand: that you were at that time without Christ, alienated from the way of life of Israel, and strangers to the covenants of the promise36, having no hope, and without God in the world.

In calling the Ephesians Gentiles in the flesh, he shows that in the spirit they are not Gentiles: just as, on the contrary, the Jews are Gentiles in the spirit and Israelites in the flesh. Jews and Gentiles, then, are divided in four ways. Some are circumcised in the flesh and in the spirit, such as were Moses and Aaron, the Apostles and Nathanael, whose hidden Judaism the Lord beheld when he said: Behold an Israelite indeed, in whom there is no guile (John 1:47). Others are circumcised neither in the flesh nor in the spirit, such as were Nebuchadnezzar and Pharaoh, and today the multitude of the barbarian and Roman nations, which do not believe in God. Third, those who are circumcised only in the flesh and have an uncircumcised spirit, to whom the prophet says: Be circumcised to your God, and do not circumcise the flesh of your foreskin (Jer. 4:4). And elsewhere: All the nations are uncircumcised37 in the flesh: but the house of Israel are uncircumcised in heart (Jer. 9:26). Last, those of whom it is now said: That once you, Gentiles in the flesh, who were called uncircumcision by that which is called circumcision in the flesh, made by hand, such as is today the whole throng of believers, and the whole world is full of those from the Gentiles. To distinguish them, then, from the Jews, who are Gentiles in the spirit, the Ephesians are called Gentiles in the flesh, because according to the spirit they are Israelites. For in another place too Scripture, mentioning a fleshly Israel, says: Behold Israel according to the flesh (1 Cor. 10): because in the spirit it was not Israel. And he has also tempered his words beautifully: who were called uncircumcision. You were called uncircumcision, he says, and you were not, by that which is called circumcision in the flesh, made by hand. Not that it is circumcision, but that it takes this name to itself, and is truly a circumcision made by hand, not by the spirit. At the same time this too must be noted: that we, whom he recalled as having once been without Christ, alienated from the way of life of Israel, and strangers to the promises and to the Covenant of God, now, after we have believed in Christ, just as we have received his promises and covenants, so may be said to have the way of life of Israel as well: inasmuch as the whole way of life of the Law is fulfilled in us, since, of course, the Law is spiritual, and it is rather we who are circumcised, and keep the sabbath in the spirit, offering spiritual victims: their temple and altar having been destroyed, it is we who offer to God the tithes of our fruits, it is we who sacrifice the spotless lamb, and, girded about the loins and ready for the road, eat the Passover. For just as circumcision is said to be in the flesh, made by hand, so, by distinction from it, another circumcision is understood to exist, which is not38, as we have said, made by hand, but by the spirit. And as to his saying: Having no hope, and without God in the world: it is not that the Ephesians, before they believed in Christ, did not have many gods and did not worship them; but that whoever is without the true God has no god. And without God in the world has been added significantly. For indeed they had God, since God had known beforehand that they would have him, and in the foreknowledge of God they were not without God, but in the world they were without God.

2:13-14

But now in Christ Jesus you who once were far off have been made near in the blood of Christ; for he is our peace.

God is everywhere, and is everywhere whole: from whom can anyone be separated, since all things are in him, and he himself says through the prophet: I am a God drawing near, and not from afar (Jer. 23:23)? And the Psalmist testifies: If I ascend into heaven, you are there: if I descend into hell, you are present (Ps. 138:8). Although, then, all things are in him, yet he is said to be far from the wicked, according to that saying: The Lord is far from the wicked (Prov. 15:29). He from whom the wicked are far is near to the saints. Accordingly, though he was far from the Ephesians too, in the blood of Jesus he was made near to them. And it must be observed more carefully that without the blood of the Lord Jesus no one draws near to God, because he is our peace, saying: My peace I give to you: my peace I leave to you (John 14:27). For just as wisdom makes men wise, and righteousness righteous, and sanctification holy, and life living, so he too, who is peace, makes us peaceable, so that we may say: With those who hated peace I was peaceable (Ps. 119:7). But if Christ is the peace of believers, whoever is without peace consequently does not have Christ.

2:15-18

Who made both one, and, breaking down the middle wall of the partition, the enmity in his flesh, making void the law of commandments in decrees: that he might create the two in himself into one new man, making peace, that he might reconcile both in one body to God through the cross, slaying the enmity in it; and coming, he preached peace to you who were far off, and peace to those who were near; for through him we both have access in one spirit to the Father.

This is the middle wall and partition which divided each people from the other. Of it the midwife also speaks in Genesis at the birth of the twins: Why has the hedge been broken through on your account? (Gen. 38:29). After, then, the Savior destroyed in his own flesh the wisdom of the flesh, which is hostile to God (Rom. 8), and exchanged the precepts of the Law for the decrees of the Gospel, so as to make of Jew and Gentile one Christian people, preaching peace and concord to us who were far off and to the remnant of the Jews who believed from Israel through the apostles: then we drew near through him to God, and came to know that we have one Father in the spirit; and that was fulfilled which the Lord spoke in the Gospel, saying: And there shall be one shepherd and one flock (John 10:16); and again: And other sheep I have which are not of this fold, meaning us, who were to be gathered from the Gentiles. Now the enmity which was destroyed in the Savior's flesh was also slain through the cross. For it is written: that he might reconcile both in one body to God through the cross, slaying the enmity in it. Not, as is found in the Latin manuscripts, in himself: on account of the ambiguity of the Greek pronoun; for ἐν αὐτῷ can be understood both as in himself and as in it, that is, in the cross, because the cross, that is, σταυρὸς, is of the masculine gender among the Greeks. The law of commandments too has been overturned in decrees: after circumcision, and the sabbath rest which remains for the people of God, and the Passover, and Pentecost, and not appearing empty in the sight of God, came to be understood more deeply than they sound, and we, withdrawing from the letter that kills, began to follow the spirit that gives life. And as to his saying: Coming, he preached peace to you who were far off, that is, to the Gentiles, and peace to those who were near, that is, to the Jews, he seems to me to have brought forward a testimony from Isaiah in other39 words, and to have used the sense of Scripture as though it were his own. For it is written in him: I have seen his ways, and I have healed him, and I have comforted him, and I have given him true comfort: peace upon peace to those who were far off, and to those who were near (Isa. 57:18). And let this much be said according to the common interpretation. But whoever reads that saying of the Apostle, where he speaks of Christ: Making peace through the blood of his cross, for the things which were on earth and the things which are in the heavens, and the rest that is said in the same place, will not think that we, who are called Israel according to the spirit, were far off, and that the Jews, who are named Israel only in the flesh, were near. And he will adjust this whole understanding to the angels and the heavenly virtues, and to human souls: that Christ in his blood has joined together earthly and heavenly things which were formerly at variance with one another; and that the good shepherd, carrying the sick sheep back to the mountains, has made it be with the rest; and has joined the drachma which had been lost to the drachmas which had been safe (Luke 15); and has drawn the commandments of the Law back to those decrees after whose type and image Moses beheld the things that were to be made in the tabernacle (Exod. 25). And so it comes about that the cross of the Lord has profited not only earth but heaven too; not only men but angels as well; and that every creature has been cleansed by the blood of its Lord. And as to his saying: That he might create the two in himself into one new man, which seems rather to fit the earlier sense concerning Jews and Gentiles: he would fit it to his own understanding thus, saying that man, made according to the image and likeness of God, will after the reconciliation receive back the same form which the angels have even now and which he himself lost. And he says that the new man is the one who is renewed daily and is to dwell in a new world, when there will be a new heaven and a new earth (Isa. 65), and is to drink the new cup of Jesus in the kingdom of God (Mark 14), and to sing a new song (Ps. 32:3 and 97:1), and will rejoice in the reading of the Old and the New Testament. That too which is written elsewhere: Behold, I make new things, which shall now spring forth (Isa. 43:19), he will take in such a way as to judge that the beginning of the renewal takes place in the present age; but he will think that the perfect and consummated newness cannot yet be fulfilled in this age. And just as now we see through a mirror in an enigma, but then face to face (1 Cor. 13), so too he will think that the restoration of the new man is to be fulfilled fully and perfectly then, when heavenly and earthly things have been joined together and we have drawn near to the Father in one spirit, and mind, and the same judgment. Something like this Paul hints to every prudent reader in another Epistle too (if anyone, that is, accepts it), saying: All these, receiving the testimony of faith, did not obtain the promise, God providing something better for us: that they should not be made perfect without us (Heb. 11:39, 40). For this reason also the whole creation groans together with us who groan in this tabernacle, and suffers pain together with us who from the fear of God have conceived in the womb and are in pain, and we await40 the revelation of the sons of God: that it may be set free from the vanity of the servitude to which it is now subjected, and there may be one shepherd and one flock; and the Prayer of the Lord may be fulfilled, who says: Your will be done as in heaven and on earth (Matt. 6:10).

2:19-22

Therefore you are no longer strangers and sojourners: but fellow citizens of the saints, and members of the household of God, built upon the foundation of the apostles and prophets, Christ Jesus himself being the chief cornerstone. In whom the whole building, fitted together, grows into a holy temple in the Lord. In whom you also are built together into a dwelling place of God, in the spirit.

To what he had said above: strangers to the covenants and the promise, he now responds: therefore you are no longer strangers and sojourners. And to what he had put earlier, alienated from the way of life of Israel, he now returns: You are fellow citizens of the saints, and members of the household of God. This passage tells most of all against those who strive to introduce diverse natures. For how have strangers become fellow citizens of the saints, and how have those who were once alienated from the way of life of Israel become members of the household of God, if nature cannot be changed either for the better or for the worse? And what follows: Built upon the foundation of the apostles and prophets, fits the first interpretation, the one in which we said above that before the passion of Christ Israel was near, and the Gentiles were far off, better than the second. We can use this testimony also against Marcion and the other ancient heretics who preach one God of the Law and another of the Gospel. For if those who are no longer strangers and sojourners, but fellow citizens of the saints and members of the household of God, have been built upon the foundation of the apostles and prophets, Christ Jesus himself being the chief cornerstone, in whom the whole building, fitted together, grows into a holy temple in the Lord, in whom the Ephesians too are built together into a tabernacle of God in the spirit, then there is one God, of one building and temple, which has been built upon the foundation of the apostles and prophets. But if the whole building, fitted together, grows into a holy temple in the Lord, we must strive with all our labor to become those stones of which it is written: Holy stones are rolled upon the earth (Zech. 9:16). And when we have become living stones, hewn on every side, smooth41, polished, having no roughness, let us be built into a temple and become a dwelling place for God. And let there be set up within us the ark of the covenant, keeper of the Law of the Lord, and the Cherubim, the multitude of knowledge; and let the inner parts of our breast pass over to a new name, and let us be called DABIR (דביר), which we can call "oracle" or "response", or, to render the word more strictly word for word, call λαλητήριον, that is, "speaking-place": so that we may break out with the Apostle into the words: Or do you seek a proof of Christ who speaks in me? (2 Cor. 13:3). But the whole building constructed upon the foundation of the apostles and prophets can signify not only us but also the heavenly virtues, so that all alike may become a dwelling place of God in the spirit: for they say it is incongruous that a building of men, compacted and harmonious, should grow into a holy temple in the Lord and become a dwelling place of God in the spirit, while the angels and all the blessed virtues that serve God in the heavenly places should be thought strangers to this happiness. Now the chief cornerstone, which holds both peoples together (or, according to the second interpretation, joins heavenly and earthly things), is Christ our Lord, the stone cut from the mountain without hands, of whom the Psalmist also testifies, saying: The stone which the builders rejected, this has become the head of the corner. This has been done by the Lord (Ps. 117:22). For he was rejected by the Pharisees, who seemed to have knowledge of the Law and to be building the temple of God with the commandments of the Law; and he was taken up by God as the cornerstone, so that he himself, in the middle, might hold the two walls together, and through him both peoples might have access to God. This cornerstone is, to those who do not believe, a stone of stumbling and a rock of offence: whoever falls upon it will be shattered; but on whomever it falls, it will crush him (Luke 20).

Book Two

With the help of your prayers, O Paula and Eustochium, we take up the second book on the Epistle to the Ephesians, intending also to send new little gifts to Rome. Not that the senate of the learned deigns to read these things and to enrol them in the libraries of the ancients, but because the holy Marcella demands by her letters that this very thing be done. As often as I recall her studies, her talent, her labor, so often do I condemn myself for sloth, I who, settled in the solitude of a monastery and looking out upon that manger in which the shepherds in haste adored the crying infant (Luke 2), cannot do what a noble woman accomplishes in her spare hours amid a bustling household and the management of her house. Wherefore I beg her and you together, and any others who may perhaps read it, to know that I am not bringing forth a discourse long thought out and polished, but that, to disclose the mysteries of the Scriptures, I use words almost from the street corner, and sometimes in a single day reach as many as a thousand lines, so that the explanation begun on the Apostle may be completed by the prayers of Paul himself, whose Epistles we are striving to expound.

3:1-4

For the sake of this thing, I Paul, the prisoner of Jesus Christ for you Gentiles: if indeed you have heard of the dispensation of the grace of God which was given to me in you. For according to revelation the mystery was made known to me, as I wrote before in brief; as you are able, reading, to understand my sense in the mystery of Christ.

As far as the sequence of the discourse and the texture of the language are concerned, though we searched most diligently, we have been able to find nothing that he gave in answer to what he put first: For the sake of this thing, I Paul, the prisoner of Jesus Christ for you Gentiles. For he did not say: for the sake of this thing I Paul did this or that, or taught this and that; but, leaving the sentence hanging, he passed on to other things. Unless perhaps, pardoning him for what he himself also confessed, saying: Even if unskilled in speech, yet not in knowledge (2 Cor. 11:6), we should look in him for the order of the meanings rather than of the words. It can be supplied thus: for the sake of this thing, I Paul, the prisoner of Christ Jesus, and a prisoner for you who are from the Gentiles, came to know the mystery, so that I might hand it on to you as well, as I said a little before in this same Epistle. And you ought to hear of the dispensation of the grace of God which was given to me in you who are from the Gentiles, for whom I am also the prisoner of Jesus Christ. That Paul is the prisoner of Jesus Christ for the Gentiles can also be understood of martyrdom: that, cast into chains at Rome, he sent this Epistle at the time when, as we have shown in another place, the Epistles to Philemon, to the Colossians and to the Philippians were written. Or surely, because we have read in many places that this body is called the bond of the soul, by which it is held as though shut up in a prison, we say that Paul is held in by the ties of the body, and does not return and be with Christ, for this reason: that the preaching to the Gentiles may be fully completed through him. Some, however, introduce another sense into this: that Paul, predestined and sanctified from his mother's womb for the preaching to the Gentiles before he was born, afterward received the bonds of the flesh. But I think that the expression in this place is also faulty. For instead of what he ought to have said: for the sake of this thing, I Paul, the prisoner of Jesus Christ for you Gentiles, came to know the mystery, as I also wrote before in brief; as you are able, reading, to understand, he says: for the sake of this thing I Paul the prisoner, according to revelation the mystery was made known to me, and the rest. But if anyone can show, even according to the context of the discourse and the language, that the Apostle was perfect and did not fall into the faults of the grammatical art, he is rather the one to be listened to. As for us, whenever we have noted solecisms or anything of the kind, we are not striking at the Apostle, as the malevolent charge, but are rather the Apostle's champions, because a Hebrew of the Hebrews, without the polish of rhetorical speech, the arrangement of words and the charm of eloquence, would never have been able to bring the whole world over to the faith of Christ had he not evangelized it not in wisdom of word but in the power of God. For he himself says to the Corinthians: And I, when I came to you, brethren, came not in eminence of word or of wisdom, announcing to you the testimony of God (1 Cor. 2:1). And again: And my word and my preaching were not in persuasive words of wisdom, but in demonstration of the spirit and of power, that your faith might not be in the wisdom of men, but in the power of God (ibid., 4 and 5). This man, then, who makes solecisms in his words, who cannot complete a hyperbaton and close a sentence, boldly claims wisdom for himself and says: For according to revelation the mystery is known to me, as I wrote before in brief. For truly, if anyone considers the earlier parts of this epistle, he will see mysteries revealed to the Apostle, some small part of which he touched on in his discourse: not so much bringing forth all that he knew as showing from the little what he kept silent. As you are able, he says, reading, to understand my sense, or wisdom, in the mystery of Christ. This is what we said in the preface: that none of Paul's Epistles has so many mysteries, or is wrapped in such recondite meanings, which the Apostle both boasts that he knows and shows to us, indicated briefly, so that we may reread more attentively what has been written.

3:5-7

And42 in other generations it was not made known to the sons of men, as it has now been revealed to his holy apostles and prophets in the Spirit: that the Gentiles are fellow heirs, and fellow members of the body, and fellow partakers of the promise in Christ through the Gospel, of which I was made a minister according to the gift of the grace of God, which was given to me according to the working of his power.

The mystery of Christ, which the Apostle set out in part above, how it was unknown to other generations, seems to call for fuller treatment: whether Abraham, Jacob and Moses, Isaiah and the other prophets were ignorant of it, or not. For Scripture records that the coming of Christ and the calling of the Gentiles were foretold by them. Abraham indeed saw his day, and was glad (John 8:56), and it is said to him: In your seed shall all nations be blessed (Gen. 22:18). And Jacob speaks prophetically of him who was to spring from Judah: And he shall be the expectation of the nations (ibid., 49:10). Moses too esteemed the reproach of Christ greater riches than the treasures of Egypt. And Isaiah: Behold, he says, a virgin shall conceive in the womb, and shall bear (Isa. 7:14). And elsewhere: There shall be the rod of Jesse, and he who shall rise to reign over the nations: in him shall the nations hope (ibid., 11:10). And, not to be long, let it suffice to have cited a few things from the Psalms: And all the families of the nations shall worship in his sight (Ps. 21:28). And again: All nations shall serve him (Ps. 71:11). And still more plainly, concerning the people of Israel and concerning the Lord the Savior: Visit this vine, and perfect that which your right hand has planted, and upon the son whom you have strengthened for yourself (Ps. 79:15, 16). The dispensation of his body, too, is indicated in what follows: Let your hand be upon the man of your right hand, and upon the son of man whom you have strengthened for yourself (ibid., 18). Either, then, we must take it, with Montanus, that the patriarchs and prophets spoke in ecstasy and did not know what they said; or, if this is impious (for the spirit of the prophets is subject to the prophets), they certainly understood what they spoke. And if they understood, the question is how Paul now says that what was not made known to other generations has been revealed to the apostles of Christ. Either, then, we must answer that Paul cautiously and pointedly testified that the mystery was unknown to the sons of men, not to the sons of God, to whom he says: I said: You are gods, and all sons of the Most High (Ps. 81:6); namely, that those who had received the spirit of adoption, of whom the patriarchs and prophets were, knew the mystery of God. Or, if this is not accepted, and seems too violent and forced, the argument will pass over to this, that it says Paul did not state definitely and generally that the mystery of the Lord was altogether unknown to other generations, but that the patriarchs of old and the prophets did not know it in the way in which it has now been revealed to his saints and apostles. For it is one thing to know in the spirit the things to come, another to see them accomplished in deed. Hence John too is said to be greater than all the prophets (Luke 7) for this reason: because he himself beheld the one whom the others prophesied, and pointed him out with his finger, saying: Behold the Lamb of God: behold him who takes away the sins of the world (John 1:29). In accordance with this sense that saying too can be explained: Many prophets and righteous men desired to see what you see, and did not see it, and to hear what you hear, and did not hear it (Luke 10:24). They certainly desired to see and hear these things, which they had known were to come. But if they did not know the things to come, how could they desire what they were wholly ignorant of? We read in the books of Kings that the Lord, in a dream, made a promise to Solomon, saying: Because you have asked this thing of me, and have not asked for yourself many days, nor requested riches, nor the lives of your enemies, but have asked that you might understand and hear judgment: behold, I have done your word, and have given you a prudent and wise heart, so that one like you43 has not been before you, and after you there shall not arise one like you (1 Kings 3:11, 12). And how do some think that a mystery was revealed to the apostles of Christ which was unknown to Solomon, when the divine voice promised that Solomon was wiser than all the apostles and than the patriarchs before him? He also speaks confidently of himself: God has taught me wisdom, and I have known the understanding of the saints (Prov. 30:3). David too glories in the knowledge of the hidden mystery, saying: The uncertain and hidden things of your wisdom you have made manifest to me (Ps. 50:8). And to him who applauded himself for his wisdom God speaks through the prophet: Are you wiser than Daniel (Ezek. 28:3)? Either, then, the sense which we have already discussed above must be held: that the patriarchs and prophets were ignorant of the mystery of Christ in the way in which it has now been revealed to the apostles, because it is one thing to hold something in one's hands, another to foresee in the spirit what is to come. Or it must be said that, as faces are not like faces, so neither are hearts like hearts; and that, according to the Apostle, the gifts of grace are diverse: one has prophecy, another kinds of tongues, another gifts of healings, another helps and governments; this one is wise, that one faithful, another strong in the knowledge of secrets, that one content with simple faith alone (1 Cor. 12). For not all have spoken alike, after the example of Solomon, of the natures of beasts and birds and plants, so as to discourse from the cedar of Lebanon to the hyssop that comes out through the wall. On the other hand Solomon perhaps did not see, as Moses did, all the kinds of victims and of the worship of God; nor did the Lord speak with him face to face; nor did he either possess or understand so great an array of vessels, whose pattern the Lord showed to Moses on the mountain (1 Kings 4; Exod. 33, 25). And as the patriarchs and prophets had some things which we must believe the apostles did not have, so, on the other hand, in keeping with the fitness of the time and for the preaching of the Gospel, the apostles had the mystery of Christ better known. The holy men of old indeed knew it too, but not as the apostles did, on whom the necessity of preaching lay. But what is this mystery which was not revealed to other generations as it is now? Surely that which follows: that the Gentiles are fellow heirs, fellow members of the body, and fellow partakers of the promise in Christ Jesus through the Gospel, of which Paul was made a minister, according to the gift of the grace of God which was given to him according to the working of his power. I know that the addition of that conjunction, by which it says fellow heirs, and fellow members of the body, and fellow partakers (cohæredes, concorporales, comparticipes), makes the sentence unseemly in the Latin language. But because it is so in the Greek, and in the divine Scriptures the single words, syllables, accents and points are full of meanings, for that reason we would rather run a risk in the composition and structure of the words than in the understanding. The Gentiles, then, are fellow heirs with Israel, or, as we think better, they are fellow heirs with Christ, so that God is our inheritance, and we are fellow heirs of Christ. This is also written in another place: Heirs of God, and fellow heirs of Christ (Rom. 8:17). Not that some possession is divided among us; but that the Lord himself is our inheritance and possession. The Lord, he says, is your inheritance (Deut. 18:2); and elsewhere: The Lord is my portion and my inheritance (Ps. 15:5). Fellow members of the body, moreover, signifies this: that just as in one body there are many members, for example eyes, hands, ears, feet, belly and knees, and though they are in one body they have their own differences, and rejoice and grieve for one another; so, though they have different graces, those who have believed in Christ are nevertheless gathered together in the one body of the Church. And in the two former words, that is, in his saying fellow heirs and fellow members of the body, one could suspect something different within the one body. But from his having added and fellow partakers of the promise in Christ Jesus, all difference whatever has been cut away. For where there is one common partaking, all things are common. Beautifully too he added through the Gospel, of which I was made a minister according to the gift of the grace which was given to me according to the working of his power, so as to show the grace of God, not his own merit. For he knew that he had been a persecutor and had laid waste the Church of Christ; by this humility the charge of arrogance is wholly excluded. On this point some think he should be rebuked, because he said that a mystery was revealed to him which had been unknown to the patriarchs and prophets. For a disciple of humility would never swell with words of arrogance, he who says that the Gospel of which he is a minister came not of his own merit, but of the grace of God. Those who hold that the prophets did not understand what they said, and44 spoke as if in ecstasy, along with the present testimony also drag in, to confirm their dogma, the passage found in many manuscripts of the Epistle to the Romans, reading: Now to him who is able to strengthen you according to my Gospel and the preaching of Jesus Christ, according to the revelation of the mystery kept silent in eternal times, but now made manifest through the prophetic Scriptures and the coming of our Lord Jesus Christ, and the rest. To them we must answer briefly that in past times the mystery of Christ was kept silent, not among those who promised that it would come, but among all the nations, to which it was afterwards made manifest. And we must likewise note that the mystery of our faith cannot be revealed except through the prophetic Scriptures and the coming of Christ. Let those, then, who do not understand the prophets and do not wish to know them, claiming that they are content with the Gospel alone, know that they are ignorant of the mystery of Christ, which in eternal times was unknown to all the nations.

3:8-9

To me, the least of all the saints, this grace was given: to preach among the Gentiles the unsearchable riches of Christ, and to enlighten all as to what is the dispensation of the mystery hidden from the ages in God, who created all things.

I do not think that the apostle Paul, in agreement with the secret of his own mind, truly called himself lower than all45 the Saints: for example, than those who were in Ephesus, in Corinth, in Thessalonica, or who had believed in the whole world. For although it is a sign of humility to call oneself lower than all the Saints, it is the guilt of a lie to keep one thing shut up in the breast and to bring forth another on the tongue. An argument must therefore be found by which Paul both truly was lower than all the saints46, and yet did not fall from the apostolic dignity. The Lord speaks in the Gospel to the disciples: Whoever among you wishes to be greater, let him become the least of all, and whoever wishes to be first, let him be the last of all (Matt. 20:26, 27). This Paul fulfilled in deed, saying: For I think that God has shown us apostles as the last, as if destined to death (1 Cor. 4:9). Than all, therefore, who desired to be weak for Christ's sake, the apostle Paul was weaker, and for that reason greater. For I labored more, he says, than all of them; yet not I, but the grace of God which is with me. Because of this humility, while he is the least of all the saints, this grace was given to him among the Gentiles, that he should preach the unsearchable riches of Christ, and teach the dispensation of the mystery hidden from the ages in God, who created all things. If the riches of Christ are unsearchable, how are they preached among the people? If the mystery was hidden from the ages in God, on what principle is it brought forth through Paul to the Gentiles? But "unsearchable" and "hidden" must be understood in two ways: either that the riches were unsearchable before, and now, after the Lord's passion, have been opened; or else that the things which by their nature were unsearchable to man have become known by God's revealing them. For it is one thing to reach a secret by one's own curiosity, which, once it has been found, ceases to be unsearchable; another thing to be wholly unable to grasp it by one's own diligence, but to know it through the grace of God; and this, when you have come to know it and have shown it to others as well, nonetheless remains unsearchable, since for you, as far as lay in you, it was hidden before it was shown. The Psalmist too, speaking to Christ himself, testifies that the riches of Christ are unsearchable, saying: How infinite the multitude of your goodness, which you have hidden for those who fear you (Ps. 30:20). These riches of his goodness were hidden through all the past ages in God, who is the Creator of all. Where are Marcion and Valentinus, and all the heretics, who assert one Creator of the world, that is, of the things visible, and another of the things invisible? They call the one just, the other some unknown being, ever unknown, only good, who is the Father of Christ. Behold, God, in whom the mystery of Christ was hidden through all the past ages, is proclaimed the Creator of all. From this it is shown that the God of the New and of the Old Testament is the same. The mystery hidden from the ages can, however, also be understood otherwise: that the ages themselves were ignorant of it, that is, all the spiritual and rational creatures that were in the ages. For "age" is frequently taken for those things that dwell in the age. As Paul speaks to the Galatians, saying: That he might deliver us from the present evil age (Gal. 1:4). And in another place: That he might show in the ages to come (Eph. 2:7), in place of: to all those who were to be in the ages coming after.

3:10-11

That the manifold wisdom of God might now be made known to the principalities and powers in the heavenly places through the Church, according to the purpose of the ages, which he made in Christ Jesus our Lord.

According to the gift of the grace of God which was given to the Apostle, that he should preach the unsearchable riches of Christ and teach among the Gentiles the mystery which had been unknown from the ages, for this reason that very mystery was revealed to him: that not only to the Gentiles, but also to the principalities and powers, the manifold wisdom of God might be made manifest through the Church, which, destined long ago according to the purpose of the ancient ages, is now seen to be accomplished in Christ. But if the manifold wisdom of God, which has now been revealed to them through the Church, was unknown to the principalities and powers in the heavens, which we ought to take as holy and as ministers of God (although some interpret them as the prince of this air and his angels), how much more was it unknown to the patriarchs and prophets, who, as we showed above, were not ignorant of the mystery of Christ, but did not know it as the apostles did? For the manifold (multiplex) wisdom of God, which in the Greek language is called πολυποίκιλος, and, so to speak, multifarious, has now been revealed through the Church of God to the principalities and powers as well. God had long ago decreed in his mind that it should come to be, and from what we see we recognize that it is now perfected. The cross of Christ, therefore, has profited not only us, but also the angels and all the virtues in heaven, and has opened a mystery which they did not know before. Accordingly they marvel at God returning to heaven with his body, and say: Who is this who comes up from Edom, his garments tawny from Bozrah, so47 beautiful in a white robe (Isa. 63:1). And in another place: Who is this king of glory? The Lord of virtues, he is the king of glory (Ps. 23:10). Let us not think, then, that in the Church there is a simple faith, but that the wisdom is manifold and multifarious, so that it is not only varied, but marked out by much variety. You look upon the cradle of Christ: look at the heaven as well. You behold an infant crying in the manger: but listen at the same time to the angels praising. Herod persecutes, but the Magi worship (Matt. 2). The Pharisees do not know him, but the star points him out. He is baptized by a servant: but the voice of God thundering from above is heard (Matt. 3). He is plunged in the waters: but the dove descends, or rather the Spirit in the dove (ibid., 16). He comes to his passion, and fears to suffer; he wishes the cup to pass, and he rebukes Peter because he feared the cup. What is more prudent than this foolishness, more distinct than this variety, more obscure than this wisdom, which God made in Christ Jesus our Lord? For although, following the order and connection of the sentence, many could understand that the Church was made, and also the πρόθεσις, that is, the propositio (proposition), which in the Latin language we have expressed in the neuter gender as propositum (purpose), nevertheless "wisdom made in Christ" can ring truer than the rest. For the foolishness of God is wiser than men (1 Cor. 1:25). But if, according to the mystical understandings, wisdom is said to have been made in Christ (Sir. 24), let the Arians understand that they boast in vain of that testimony in which Wisdom says that she was created and begotten and founded at the beginning of the ways of God. And certainly, according to them, she was created; she could not have been born. But if she was born, how is she said to have been both founded and created?

3:12

In whom we have boldness, and access in confidence through faith in him.

Nothing can so give us boldness toward God, and purity of conscience, of which it is said: If our conscience does not reproach us, we have confidence toward God (1 John 3:21), as the Word, truth, wisdom, righteousness, all of which are understood in Christ. As these, then, give us boldness toward God, so too they give access, so that our sense may approach him and contemplate him more attentively, and take and claim for itself something of his majesty, and, as if from the rays of the sun, borrow a certain warmth and splendor of light, and say: The light of your countenance has been signed upon us, O Lord (Ps. 4:7). It is a great thing, then, not only for us to have boldness, but also to have it in confidence; and not only to have access, but, that the access itself may be firmer, to have it in confidence; and the beginning and origin of this boldness and access is faith in Christ. He, then, who does all things with reason and order believes in Christ the Word and Reason; he who has been able to grasp wisdom believes in Christ the Wisdom; he who has understood truth believes in Christ the Truth; he who has lived righteously believes in him as Righteousness. Nor is there need to say more, since the necessity has often been laid on me of discoursing about Christ in a similar way.

3:13

Wherefore I ask that you may not faint in my tribulations for you, which is your glory.

What the Latin translator has now rendered that you may not faint can, owing to the ambiguity of the Greek language, also be read thus: that I may not faint. So that the sense is: Wherefore I ask that I may not faint in my tribulations for you, etc. This, then, is what the Apostle asks and earnestly entreats of the Lord: that he may not faint in his afflictions. For he saw that he had preached the Gospel from Jerusalem as far as Illyricum, had gone to Rome, and had either proceeded to Spain or was planning to go there (Rom. 15). The whole world, at his teaching, was being snatched from the prince's power: idols were being abandoned, and the temples, deserted by their worshippers, were filling with squalor and filth. For this reason the whole army of demons, the whole throng of hostile powers, formed a wedge and fought against him, so that through tribulations and straits he might cease to preach the Gospel of Christ and, wearied, at length take his rest. This, therefore, is what he says: I ask that I may not faint in my tribulations for you. For all that I suffer, that I am afflicted, that I am hemmed in, is for the sake of your salvation, while I long to proclaim the Gospel to you: and these tribulations of mine are your glory. Moreover, he spoke as if unskilled, in joining a singular "glory" to the plural number of "tribulations", and saying: In my tribulations for you, which is your glory, in place of which tribulations are your glory: unless perhaps someone, more profoundly and by hyperbaton, thinks that this should be referred either to the boldness, or to the access, or to the faith, and says: in whom we have boldness, and access, and faith; which boldness, access and faith is your glory. But the former sense is rather to be followed. However, following what the Latin has rendered, Wherefore I ask that you may not faint in my tribulations for you, which is your glory, it can be explained that he asks on behalf of the Ephesians, that they may not grow weary and faint in the afflictions of the apostle. Indeed, what follows also fits this sense better: For this reason I bend my knees to the Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, that he may grant you, according to the riches of his glory, to be strengthened with power. For they needed strength, so as not to be overcome in tribulations. Someone may say: If the Ephesians had been placed in straits, Paul could rightly have prayed for them that they might not faint and grow weary; but as it is, it is absurd, when Paul is placed in tribulations, for him to ask that the Ephesians may not faint in his toil. But to resolve this little question too, this example will be taken: Your temptation in my flesh you did not despise nor reject. For it is shown in this saying that in the flesh of Paul a temptation arose for others. Let us consider the men of the whole world (and why do I speak in common of all? let us examine those who are thought to believe in Christ), and we shall see that they are tempted above all by this question: why many adversities befall holy men who serve God, while on the contrary the wicked, the impious, parricides flourish, thrive, are rich and powerful. Expressing their words in his own person, David says: How good is God to Israel, to the upright of heart. But my feet were almost moved; my steps were almost poured out. Because I was envious over the wicked, seeing the peace of sinners (Ps. 72:1 ff.), and the rest. Upon which he adds: But I said: Then in vain have I justified my heart, and washed my hands among the innocent, and I have been scourged all the day. It is no wonder, then, if, while Paul was hemmed in by many straits in Ephesus and in Asia, the Ephesians were being tempted, and needed the help of his prayers, so as not to faint in his afflictions. That the Apostle suffered many things in Ephesus and in Asia is indeed written in the Acts of the Apostles as well; but we learn it more from the Epistles of Paul himself, in which he says: If, after the manner of men, I fought with beasts at Ephesus, what does it profit me, if the dead do not rise (1 Cor. 15:32)? And in the second to the Corinthians: I do not want you to be ignorant, brethren, of our tribulation which came upon us in Asia; that we were weighed down beyond measure, beyond our strength, so that we despaired even of living; but we ourselves had in ourselves the answer of death, that we might not trust in ourselves, but in God who raises the dead, who has delivered us from such deaths, and delivers us; and we hope that he will also deliver us (2 Cor. 1:8 ff.). How great a weight of tribulations, do you think, pressed upon him, that he should say: who has delivered us from so many and such deaths? Rightly, therefore, he asks that the faith of the Ephesians may not fail in his afflictions and straits, which he suffers for this reason, that he was preaching the Gospel to the Ephesians. These afflictions are indeed punishments in the eyes of unbelievers, but among the faithful they are glory and victory, because to have conquered is not to have yielded to one's adversaries in straits.

3:14-15

Therefore I bow my knees to the Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, from whom all fatherhood in the heavens and on earth is named.

Just as it was shown above that there are spiritual eyes after the likeness of the bodily lights, so now it must be understood that the knees of the outer man are one thing and those of the inner man another. For that which is said in Isaiah, Be strengthened, you slack hands and loosened knees (Isa. 35:3), and in another place by the Apostle himself, That in the name, he says, of Jesus every knee should bow, of things in heaven, of things on earth, and of things below (Phil. 2:10), pertains not to the knees of the body but to the subjection of the mind and the bowing of the soul and the obedience of the heart, which levels itself with the ground, as the Psalmist also sings, saying: My soul has cleaved to the pavement (Ps. 118:25). For how could the soul, a spiritual and incorporeal thing, cleave to the pavement, a bodily thing? Granted, indeed, that earthly beings bow the knee in the name of Jesus: can we also affirm that the angels and the other virtues, which abide in the heavenly places, have a bodily form, so that they bow the knee in his name? or that the souls in the underworld, freed from the bonds of the body, will bend a knee which they do not have? Therefore, just as whoever is subject to the Savior is said to bend the knee to him, so he who is the slave of sin, and has the spirit of slavery again in fear, is written to have bent the knee to sin. I have left for myself48, says the Lord, seven thousand men who have not bowed their knees before Baal (1 Kings 19:18), that is, before an idol and before sin. He who is a fornicator, and runs about through brothels and harlots, bends his knee to lust. He who is furious worships anger with bent knee. He who looks up to mammon, and whose god is his belly, bends the knees to avarice and luxury. And what need is there for me to go through them one by one? We bend the knee to the devil as often as we sin. Yet in expounding these things spiritually we do not at once do away with the custom of praying according to the letter, by which we worship God in supplication with the knee set down, and, with the knee fixed on the ground, the more surely obtain what we ask of him. For we read both that Paul prayed thus on the shore and that kneelings in prayer are prescribed (Acts 21:5). But just as that edifies the simple, so we teach that the true kneeling is in the mind; for many who bend the bodily knee have by no means bowed the knee of the soul. And on the contrary, others, praying to God with the body upright, have bowed themselves the more in mind. Further, what follows, to the Father from whom all fatherhood in the heavens and on earth is named, is to be read not as it has been added in the Latin manuscripts, to the Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, but simply to the Father, so that the name of God the Father may be fitted not to our Lord Jesus Christ but to all rational creatures. We must ask, therefore, how all fatherhood in the heavens and on earth has been named from God the Father. And at the same time, before anything is discussed, it must be noted that he did not say, from whom all fatherhood in the heavens and on earth was born, or was created, but, from whom all fatherhood in the heavens and on earth is named. For it is one thing to merit the title of fatherhood, another to have a share in the nature. Fatherhood (paternitas), which in Greek is called πατριὰ, in Hebrew MESPHATH (משפחת), that is, kindred or family, we read also in Numbers: Take, he says, the sum of the whole synagogue of Israel, according to kindreds and peoples, according to the houses of the fatherhoods, according to the number of their names (Num. 1:2). And a little after (v. 16): These are the chosen ones of the synagogue, the princes of the tribes, according to their fatherhoods, the leaders of Israel. And again concerning the tribe of Levi: And the Lord spoke to Moses in the desert of Sinai, saying: Number the sons of Levi according to the houses of their fatherhoods, according to their peoples, according to their kindreds: every male from one month and upward, consider them (Num. 3:15). These things concern the people of Israel and the priestly and Levitical tribe. But, turning it over by myself to find where I may have read the word fatherhoods used of the Gentiles, for the present I find nothing else but the testimony of the twenty-first psalm, in which it is written: And all the families (patriæ) of the nations shall worship in his sight (Ps. 21:28); and of the twenty-eighth: Bring to the Lord, you families (patriæ) of the nations; bring to the Lord the sons of rams (Ps. 28:1). Just as, then, God imparts the name of his essence and substance to the other elements as well, so that they too are said to be, not because they are according to nature (for there was a time when all things were not, and again, if he wills, they will return into nothing), but they have it as a gift of God's goodness that they are said to be: so too he has bestowed the name of fatherhood from himself on all. That this may become clearer, I shall bring forward49 the testimony of the Scriptures. The Lord speaks in Exodus: I am who am (Exod. 3:14); and: Thus you shall say to the sons of Israel: He who is has sent me to you. Was God alone, and were the other things not? Surely the angels, heaven, earth or the seas, and Moses himself, to whom the Lord was speaking, and Israel, and the Egyptians, to whom and against whom he was being sent as prince and adversary, also were. And how does God claim for himself as his own a name common to substance? For the reason we have stated: because the other things received their being by the kindness of God. But God, who always is, and has no beginning from elsewhere, and is himself his own origin and the cause of his own substance, cannot be understood to have from elsewhere that by which he subsists. For in fire too, the hot is one thing and the heated another. Fire cannot be understood without heat. The other things that are heated by fire borrow its heat, and if the fire withdraws, their heat gradually lessens, they return to their own nature and are no longer called hot. In this sense it is said also in the Gospel to him who thought the Savior to be not the Son of God but a good teacher: Why do you call me good? No one is good but the one God (Luke 18:19). And certainly we read of good ground (Luke 8), and of a good man (Matt. 12), and of a good shepherd (John 10); but no one is good according to nature but God alone. The other things obtain being called good through his goodness. Just as, therefore, he who alone is good makes others good, and he who alone is immortal bestows immortality, and he who alone is true imparts the name of truth, so too he who alone is Father, because he is the Creator of all and the cause of the substance of all things, grants to the others that they be called fathers. Let us contemplate heavenly things from earthly ones. Adam, whom God first formed, and whose Creator and Father God was, surely knows that he owes to God the Father that by which he subsists. Again, those who were begotten of Adam understand him as the father from whom they sprang. Hence also in the Gospel according to Luke, when the generations had been reckoned backward step by step from Christ to David and Abraham, at the end Scripture says: sons of Seth, sons of Adam, sons of God (Luke 3:38), to show that the word fatherhood on earth first arose from God. But it is asked for what reason all fatherhood in the heavens is also named from him. Just as we, who are not of the race of Abraham, are called sons of Abraham if we have his faith, and call the patriarchs also and the prophets our fathers (provided only that our sins do not separate us from them), so I think that the angels too and the other virtues have princes of their own kind in the heavenly places, whom they rejoice to call fathers. For one can be called archangel only as archangel of angels; and dominion, principality and power are not so called unless they have inferiors subject to them. This too, therefore, can be said: since God the Father of our Lord Jesus Christ is Father according to substance, and the Only-begotten is Son not by adoption but by nature, the other creatures have merited the name of fatherhood by adoption. And whatever we say of the Father and the Son, let us know that it is said also of the Holy Spirit. For our Savior too knew himself to be a Father, saying: Son, your sins are forgiven you (Mark 2:5); and: Daughter, your faith has made you whole (Matt. 9:22); and: My little children, yet a little while I am with you (John 13:33); and through the Holy Spirit all the righteous are adopted as sons. Valentinus thinks that the emanations and marriages of his ages are confirmed above all from this passage, by no means understanding, as we said above, that fatherhoods in heaven and on earth are not made after the likeness of God the Father, but are so called.

3:16-19

That he may grant you, according to the riches of his glory, to be strengthened with power through his spirit in the inner man; that Christ may dwell through faith in your hearts; rooted and grounded in love; that you may be able to comprehend with all the saints what is the breadth, and length, and depth, and height; to know also the surpassing love of knowledge of Christ, that you may be filled in all the fullness50 of God.

Therefore, he says, I bow my knees to the Father, after whose likeness all fatherhood in heaven and on earth is named, praying and beseeching him to grant you the power of his glory, that is, to have a share in his majesty; and to strengthen and confirm you through the Holy Spirit, because there is no strength without the Holy Spirit; and to strengthen and confirm you in the inner man. For we seek not the strength of the body but that of the soul, and we desire that not the outer but the inner man be strengthened, so that after Christ has dwelt in the inner man, he may dwell in the ruling part of that inner man itself, that is, in our hearts: by no means running about through all its members, but dwelling in its rational part, and setting there his dwelling and his seat. All this, however, will come about through faith, if we believe in him. Therefore he says: that Christ may dwell through faith in your hearts. And this dwelling, which is built through the beginning of faith, has its roots and foundation in love, so that, since we are God's husbandry, God's building (1 Cor. 3), all things may grow up and be built up in love. And when, rooted and grounded in love (Eph. 3), we know with the full confidence of the mind that Christ dwells in the inner man, then we shall begin, with the other saints, to stretch ourselves toward those things too, so that we may comprehend with a keen mind what is the breadth, and length, depth and height; and not only this, but we shall also desire to know the surpassing love of knowledge of Christ; so that after all these things have been fulfilled in us in order and reason, then we may be filled in all things with the fullness of God. Let us first learn breadth and length, depth and height from bodily things, so that through them we may be able to pass over to spiritual things. For example, let the breadth of this heaven and earth, that is, of the whole world, be from East to West; the length, from South to North; the depth, in the abysses and in hell; the height, what is raised above the heavenly things. But since by very many, according to Ecclesiastes (ch. 1), heaven is affirmed to be round and to revolve in the manner of a sphere; and since no roundness has breadth and length, nor height and depth either, but it is equal on all sides; we are compelled by necessity to understand by the height the angels and the upper51 virtues; by the depth the underworld and the things that are beneath it; and by the length and breadth the middle things which stand between those above and those below. And since it follows that each thing is near either to those above or to those below, whatever things begin to advance toward better things and to rise to the heavenly and the high, let them be called length; but to the things that are nearest to the lower part and slide down into vices, let the name of breadth be given. For broad and spacious is the way that leads to death (Matt. 7:13). All these things can also be understood in the cross of our Lord Jesus Christ. For ascending on high, he led captivity captive (Ps. 67:19), and he descended into the lower parts of the earth. And after the height and the depth, the preaching of the cross went out into all the earth. And so it holds the height, and the depth, and the length, and the breadth. Nor is it any wonder if the cross of Christ possesses all things, since even anyone who has been crucified with Christ has the same power. For he will know first the breadth, beginning from the lower things and coming to know the lesser; then the length, those who, placed on earth, hasten toward the sublime and lofty; after these the depth, the adverse and opposing powers, which wage war against us in this world; and at the last the height: because after we have knowledge of them and have made them to be under our feet, then we shall merit to climb to the high and lofty places. Nor must we think that the end of our labour lies in this, that, rooted and grounded in love, we may be able to comprehend with all the saints what is the breadth, and length and depth, and height, unless we also desire with all zeal to know the eminent love of knowledge of Christ, so that not a simple love but a surpassing one may be known to us; nor is this the limit, to have knowledge of the surpassing love of Christ, unless we add something more, that we may attain the surpassing love of knowledge. From this it is to be observed that Christ has a great and immense love of knowledge, that is, of those who desire to know, who meditate in his law day and night, who turn words into works, and accomplish with the hand what they meditate with the mouth. But he who is such that he is worthy through his knowledge to have the surpassing love of Christ ought to think of nothing else but knowledge. And so he will be filled in all the fullness of God, not only in the present age but also in the one to come, so that he who has now begun to be full in study through reading may afterward be filled more perfectly with God, who is the fullness of all things, as he fills him with himself.

3:20-21

Now to him who is able to do above all things more abundantly than we ask or understand, according to the power that works in us, to him be glory in the Church and in Christ Jesus, in all generations52 of the age of ages. Amen.

To what he had said above, Therefore I bow my knees to the Father, from whom all fatherhood in the heavens and on earth is named, that he may grant you according to his riches to be strengthened with power, and the rest, he now adds: Now to him who is able to do above all things more abundantly than we ask or understand, showing that he himself had asked, according to the weakness of man, for the things that seemed advantageous to them; but that, as far as the truth of the matter itself is concerned, God is able to grant more than he is asked, and our hopes are surpassed by what is accomplished: because we do not know what we ought to pray for, and we often ask for things against ourselves, thinking them to be for us. For how much better would it have been for the fornicator to fall sick and be tormented with infirmity than to make the temple of Christ the members of a harlot? God, then, is able to grant not only beyond what we ask but also beyond what we understand. It happens at times that we do not express our thought in speech, and words do not unfold the mind, and in silent thought, with unutterable groanings, as the Apostle himself says, we pray for something or other which we cannot say. He will therefore bestow beyond what we ask or understand, according to that power which works in us: so that, just as he now gives us, not according to our merit but beyond our prayers, certain things which we neither dared to ask for nor, had we asked, thought that we deserved, so he may also grant the rest, which neither the mind can conceive nor the tongue utter. To God himself, therefore, be glory: first in the Church, which is pure, having no spot or wrinkle, and which can receive the glory of God for this reason, that it is the body of Christ. Then in Christ Jesus, because in the body of the man he assumed, whose members are all the believers, the whole divinity dwells bodily. And this glory is not extended only through the present time and ended in the ages to come, but continues, grows and increases unto all generations, and the age of ages, in ineffable eternity.

4:1

I therefore, a prisoner in the Lord, beseech you to walk worthily of the calling with which you have been called.

He could be writing these things both in the chains of Christ and while placed in prison for martyrdom; but it is better if we call him the prisoner of the Lord53 in the love of Christ. Of this Clement too is a witness, writing to the Corinthians: Who will be able to describe the bond of the love of God (1 Cor. 13)? And in the first book of Kingdoms we read: It came to pass after these words, the soul of Jonathan was bound to the soul of David (1 Sam. 18:1). And the prophet, of the apostles: After you, he says, they shall follow with hands bound in iron (Isa. 45:14). For those who love Christ follow him bound with the bonds of love. There is also another exposition; whether it should be accepted or not will be in the reader's power: that this body is called the bond of the soul, and that, because Paul received this body for the ministry of the Gospel, he is consequently called the prisoner of Christ. That this body is called a bond, and that those who are bound in the body are called the prisoners of the earth, Jeremiah too testifies in the second alphabet, saying: To humble under his feet all the prisoners of the earth (Lam. 3:34). And elsewhere we read this very thing in the person of Christ, saying: To those who were in bonds: Come out; and to those who were in darkness: Be revealed (Isa. 49:9). Here, indeed, the prophet may be showing that the bond of sin and the darkness of ignorance were dissolved by the coming and preaching of Christ. But the former sense also has its place: that the bond is the body, and the darkness is this earthly dwelling, where the rulers of darkness are, and the dark mountains, against which we are forbidden to strike our feet. As for his saying: To walk worthily of the calling with which you have been called: he must be believed to walk worthily of his calling who goes forward through him who says, I am the way (John 14:6; and Isa. 30), and does not turn aside either to the right or to the left, turns his foot away from every evil way (Prov. 4), and in whom this is fulfilled: The steps of a man are directed by the Lord (Ps. 36:22).

4:2

With all humility and meekness, with patience.

He who knows that he is earth and ashes, and is soon to be dissolved into dust, will never be lifted up by pride. And he who, having beheld the eternity of God, considers the span of human life, brief and almost like a point, will always have his death before his eyes, and will be humble and lowly. For the corruptible body weighs down the soul, and this earthly tabernacle oppresses the mind that is anxious about many things (Wisd. 9:15). Wherefore let us say with all humility: Lord, my heart is not exalted, nor are my eyes lifted up (Ps. 130:1). But all humility lies not so much in speech as in the mind, so that our conscience may know that we are humble, and we may never think that we either know or understand or are anything. Meekness too is that which is disturbed by no passion, and above all is not broken by anger and rage. Whoever has it will attain the beatitude which was promised by the voice of the Lord, that he may possess the land, that is, rule his own body and be master of it as of a subject; and let this be his first inheritance, to live in the flesh not carnally. Some, with wrinkled brow, lowered eyebrow and words weighed on the scales, claim for themselves the authority of teachers and judges; not because they themselves know anything worthy of their arrogance, but because they see that all the simpler of the brethren are ignorant of certain things in comparison with themselves.

Bearing with one another in love. If anyone understands what bearing with one another in love means, he will not think that this command fits holy men, but those who are placed at the beginnings of the virtues. For the saints will not have54 anything to bear with in one another, but those will who, being men, are still overcome by some passion. Nor is it any wonder if the Ephesians hear these things, since in a multitude of believers there are some who still must bear with one another. This very thing also seems to me to be signified by what is written to the Galatians: Bear one another's burdens (Gal. 6:2). We can, then, also interpret both testimonies in another way, so as to say that those fulfil both the bearing of one another's burdens and the bearing with one another in love who are rich and relieve the want of the poor. If anyone renders service to a sick brother, he bears with him in love. If anyone, passing a blessed life in celibacy, has helped another who has both a wife and children and can scarcely feed himself, and has comforted him in whatever way he can, he will be praised for having carried another's burden. There is someone who, seeing his mother or his widowed sister wasting away with want, cannot help: if anyone has stretched out a hand to him, he has sustained him in love. But whether we follow the former sense or the latter, he who does not have love, and despises the words of the Apostle admonishing, But we who are stronger ought to bear the infirmities of the weaker, and not to please ourselves (Rom. 15:1), consoles neither the sinning brother nor the needy one.

4:3-4

Careful to keep the unity of the spirit in the bond of peace. One body and one spirit, as you also have been called in one hope of your calling.

To the Ephesians, who had already attained the unity of the Holy Spirit, it is rightly said: careful to keep the unity of the spirit in the bond of peace. For he who has something is enjoined to be careful to keep it; but he who does not have it is commanded to be zealous that he may be able to have it. This passage tells above all against the heretics, who, though the bond of peace has been torn apart and corrupted, think that they hold the unity of the spirit, whereas the unity of the spirit is preserved in the bond of peace. For when we do not all say the same thing, and one says, I am of Paul, I of Apollos, I of Cephas (1 Cor. 1:12), we divide the unity of the spirit and tear it into parts and members. Nor let anyone at once object: How then are the graces different, and the gifts various, if the unity of the spirit is to be guarded? There are indeed varieties of gifts, but in the same spirit; and different ministries, but the same Lord; and manifold operations, but the same God who works all things in all (1 Cor. 12:4 ff.). And as for his saying, One body and one spirit: either the one body of Christ is simply understood, which is the Church, or else the body which he deigned to assume from the Virgin, lest they should think that he was embodied as many times as he appeared in the Old Testament. And one Holy Spirit: for he is the one bestower and sanctifier of all. Or else the one body refers to the life and works which in Greek are called πρακτικὸς βίος (the active life); and the one spirit to knowledge and contemplation, which properly has its place in the heart and is called by them θεωρία. When, therefore, anyone has become a member of the Church and is not separated from its one spirit, he will consequently be in the one hope of the calling. It is asked how there is one hope of the calling when there are different mansions with the Father. To this we shall say55 that the one hope of the calling, the kingdom of heaven, is as it were the one house of God the Father, and that in the one house there are various mansions. For one is the glory of the sun, another of the moon, another of the stars (1 Cor. 15:41). Or else something more subtle is indicated: that at the end and consummation of things all things are to be restored to their original state, when we shall all be made one body and be re-formed into a perfect man, and the Savior's prayer for us will be fulfilled: Father, grant that, as I and you are one, so these too may be one in us (John 17:21). I am not unaware that in what I have just expounded, careful to keep the unity of the spirit in the bond of peace, the Spirit is taken by very many not as the Holy Spirit but as the disposition and judgment of the mind, according to which all believers had one soul and one heart (Acts 4). And the Apostle enjoins the virgin: That she may be holy both in body and in spirit (1 Cor. 7:34), that is, in the work of the flesh and in the disposition of the mind. But the general explanation can also be drawn to a special interpretation: that he keeps the unity of the spirit in the bond of peace who is not carried away by every wind of doctrine, nor changes like the moon after the manner of the fool (Sir. 27), now serving the devil, now God. Let him, moreover, have one body of good works, and always present his flesh to God as a temple; and one spirit, always thinking the same things; in one hope of the calling, so that, by no means doubting the promises, he may trust with a firm mind in the resurrection and restoration of all things.

4:5-6

One Lord, one faith, one baptism, one God and Father of all, who is above all, and through all, and in all.

Just as faith is something other than baptism, God and Lord, so baptism, Lord and God are each something other than the three that are named together with them. I say this, however, on account of Sabellius, who thinks that God the Father and the Son are the same, and confounds the persons, since he finds the same divinity in both. See: most manifestly the Son is called the one Lord, and the Father the one God. This is also written more fully to the Corinthians: But for us there is one God the Father, from whom are all things, and one Lord, our Jesus Christ, through whom are all things, and we through him (1 Cor. 8:6). For just as the Son does not take away from God the Father his being Lord, though he himself alone is called Lord, so too the Father does not take away from the Son his being God, because the Father alone is named God. We also read in another place: And know that the Lord himself is God (Ps. 99:2); and elsewhere: The Lord your God is one God (Deut. 6:4). For if, as the Arians think, God the Father alone is God, by the same logic Jesus Christ alone will be Lord, and neither will the Father be Lord nor the Son God. But far be it that there should not be either deity in the lordship or lordship in the deity. There is one Lord and there is one God, because the lordship of the Father and of the Son is one divinity. For this reason faith too is called one, because we believe alike in the Father, and in the Son, and in the Holy Spirit. And baptism is one, for in the same way we are baptized into the Father, and into the Son, and into the Holy Spirit. And we are immersed three times, so that the one mystery of the Trinity may appear. And we are not baptized in the names of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit, but in one name, which is understood to be God. And I wonder by what logic, in one name, in the same work and in the same mystery, Arius, Macedonius and Eunomius suspect a diversity of nature (their discord agreeing in impiety), and, holding to the muddy spring of a creature in the Son and in the Holy Spirit, have drawn off the various rivulets of heresy. One baptism also tells against Valentinus, who contends that there are two baptisms, and against all heretics: so that they may know that they do not have baptisms, but that the life-giving font is in the one Church of Christ. One baptism can also be said in this sense: that although we are baptized three times on account of the mystery of the Trinity, it is nevertheless reckoned one baptism. Baptism is also one in water, in spirit and in fire. And it is the one of which the Lord speaks: I have a baptism to be baptized with (Luke 12:50); and elsewhere: With my baptism you shall be baptized (Mark 10:39). Now the diversity of the prepositions in the words One God and Father of all, who is above all, and through all, and in all conveys a diverse meaning. For God the Father is above all, because he is the author of all. The Son is through all, because he runs through all things and goes through everything. The Holy Spirit is in all, because nothing exists without him. Nor indeed must we think that the one God is also Father of all in common, so that, that is, the name of Father could be applied to irrational beasts; but just as, if among ten men, five sons and five slaves, we were to say alike, "Of these ten, there is one master and one father," we would surely not call him father of all, nor master of all: so too in what he says, One God and Father of all, it must be understood that he is Father of some and God of others. Zeno too, with his Stoics, supposes something of this kind about creatures and about God. Following him, Virgil says (Aeneid 6): For God goes through all lands and the tracts of the sea, etc.; and: In the beginning, the heaven and the earth and the liquid plains, the shining globe of the moon and the Titanian stars, a spirit within nourishes, and mind, poured through the limbs, moves the whole mass and mingles itself with the great body. Some think that what is written, Above all, and through all, and in all, should be referred to the Father, and the Son, and the Holy Spirit in this way: that above all things is the Father, because he is the author of all; through all, the Son, because all things were created through the Son; in all, the Holy Spirit, for he is given to those who believe, and we are the temple of the Holy Spirit; and the Father and the Son dwell in us.

4:7

But to each one of us grace has been given according to the measure of the gift of Christ.

Although God the Father is above all things, and through all, and in all, nevertheless grace is given to believers according to measure. Not that God bestows the spirit and grace by measure (for of his magnificence there is no end), but that he pours in the liquid according to the measure of the vessels, bestowing as much of the gift as the one to whom it is given is able to receive. For God does not give the spirit by measure, nor can that which is diffused equally everywhere have a measure. To make this plainer, let us take an example, imperfect indeed and not filling out the likeness, yet one through which what is said can be understood. The sea is certainly immeasurable, and its capacity is known to God alone: if anyone wished to hand it out to many men according to what they are able to carry, he would have to give it to each by measure, and its parts would receive a measure, though the whole of it is immeasurable. So too the Holy Spirit is indeed immeasurable and is enclosed by no limit; yet he is given to each according to what is expedient. And at the same time it must be noted that this same grace, which is now said to be bestowed, has been given to us according to the measure of the gift of Christ.

4:8

Therefore he says: Ascending on high, he led captivity captive, he gave gifts among men.

Because he had said above, But to each one of us grace has been given according to the measure of the gift of Christ, in order to confirm that these very gifts, which he also enumerates a little later, saying, And he gave some apostles, and some prophets, others evangelists, others pastors and teachers, and the rest, were bestowed by the Savior, he took up a testimony from the sixty-seventh psalm, so that we may know that these are the spoils distributed to men which Christ the victor won. For ascending on high, he led captivity captive. We who now believe in Christ, gathered from the Gentiles, though we were the creation of God, were taken captive by the devil and distributed among his attendants. Therefore our Lord Jesus Christ came, according to Ezekiel (chs. 9 and 12), carrying with him the vessels of captivity, and with his head covered, so that he might not be recognized by his adversaries, he preached release to those who had been taken captive, and loosing to those who were held in chains, and he freed us from the chains and from the fetters of the enemies, like that woman in the Gospel of whom he himself makes mention: But this daughter of Abraham, whom Satan has bound now eighteen years, ought she not to have been loosed from this bond on the sabbath day (Luke 13:16)? And having freed us, and rescued us through a new captivity from the old captivity, he led us with him into heaven; and on these very ones whom as victor he snatched from the hand of their enemies he bestowed diverse gifts of graces. And he has put it elegantly here, he gave gifts among men, whereas in the Psalter it is written: he received gifts among men (Ps. 67:19). But there, because it had not yet been done but was promised as future, he is for that reason said to have received. Here, however, when the Apostle writes, because he had already given, and Churches had been founded throughout the whole world, it is therefore written not that he received, but that he gave. Others expound this passage thus: that our Lord Jesus Christ ascended as victor to the heavens so that from there he might send angels and the other virtues to guard his Churches. And since this earthly place is unworthy to have the presence of the sublime powers, those powers have in a certain way endured captivity. For this is why (they say) he ascended on high: so that, taking captivity captive, he might bestow gifts on men. Now the Apostle goes back over all this for this reason: because he had said above, Bearing with one another in love, eager to keep the unity of the spirit in the bond of peace, he wished to teach that the Church is in concord amid diverse charisms, and that no occasion for schisms and dissensions is at once given because each one of us has received gifts according to the measure of the gift of Christ: not the same gifts, but that all have been called into one body and into one spirit; that is, that just as there is one Lord, and one faith, and one baptism, and one God the Father, so we too may be the very same in love, keeping the unity of the spirit in the bond of peace.

4:9

But that he ascended, what is it, except that he also descended into the lower parts of the earth?

He who is now said to have ascended ascended for this reason, because he had first descended. For this is what But that he ascended, what is it, except that he also descended means. And so we must ask, concerning what is written elsewhere, No one has ascended into heaven except he who descended from heaven, the Son of man (John 3:13), how he ascended before descending? For when he was saying these things, after his descent from the heavens, he was showing that he had once ascended to the heavens. And this too must likewise be considered: how the descent and the ascent themselves are to be understood, whether locally, according to the body, or spiritually, above the body, or indeed in both ways. Now the lower parts of the earth are taken to be hell, to which our Lord and Savior descended, so that as victor he might lead away with him to the heavens the souls of the saints which were held shut up there. Hence also, after his resurrection, very many bodies of the righteous were seen in the holy city (Matt. 27). And that hell is in the lower part of the earth the Psalmist also testifies, saying: The earth opened and swallowed Dathan, and covered over the congregation of Abiram (Ps. 105:17). And this is explained more fully in the book of Numbers (ch. 16). In another place too we read: Let death come upon them, and let them go down alive into hell (Ps. 54:16).

4:10

He who descended is the same who ascended above all the heavens, that he might fill all things.

Did he then, bodily passing through and transcending all the heavens, and all the heights, and the circles of the heavens which the philosophers call spheres, take his stand at the highest vault of heaven and, to use the very word, its apse? Or rather, scorning and looking down on all bodily things and contemplating the eternal, is he to be believed to have taken his stand above the heavens, that is, above the invisible things? This I think the better view. So the Son of God descended into the lower parts of the earth and ascended above all the heavens, so that he might fulfil not only the law and the prophets, but also certain other hidden dispensations, which he alone knows with the Father. For we cannot know how the blood of Christ benefited both the angels and those who were in hell, and yet we cannot fail to know that it did benefit them. He also descended to the underworld and ascended to the heavens so that he might fill those who were in those regions, according to what they were able to receive. From this we must know that before Christ descended and ascended, all things were empty and stood in need of his fullness. This passage tells most of all against Ebion and Photinus. For if he who ascends into the heavens is the same who had previously descended from the heavens, how is our Lord Jesus Christ not before Mary, but after Mary? And it tells also against those who with insane error invent two sons, namely the Son of God and the son of man. See: here it is most openly said that he himself is the one ascending and descending. Nor, in saying these things, do we at once give room to another heresy, which asserts a halved dispensation of Christ; but we confess one Son of both God and man in such a way that we do not, by believing only in part in the dispensation of the assumed man, by which we have been saved, mutilate it in part.

4:11-12

And he himself gave some apostles, and some prophets, and others evangelists, and others pastors and teachers, for the instruction of the saints, for the work of the ministry, for the building up of the body of Christ.

And from this passage too the one divinity of the Father and the Son is most manifestly proved. For what Christ is now described as having bestowed, God the Father is related in the first epistle to the Corinthians to have given: For some, he says, God has placed in the Church: first apostles, second prophets, third teachers; then powers, and gifts of healings, helps, governments, kinds of tongues (1 Cor. 12:28). Sabellius, not understanding this, confounds the Father and the Son, since he does not think that the same operation can belong to different persons. And at the same time it must be noted that here Christ is recorded to have given apostles, prophets, evangelists, pastors and teachers, but there the Father to have placed the same. So the Father and the Son placed, or gave, first apostles, second prophets: not those who foretell things to come, such as we read of in the Old Testament, but those who convict and judge the unbelieving and the unlearned. For such, he lays down in another epistle, are the prophets of the New Testament (1 Cor. 14:24). Third evangelists, whose feet are beautiful for announcing peace (Rom. 10). Nor indeed must we think that, just as in the three above he said that some are apostles, others prophets, others evangelists, so too in the case of pastors and teachers he set down different offices. For he does not say, "and others pastors, and others teachers," but "others pastors and teachers," so that he who is a pastor ought also to be a teacher, and that no one in the Churches, however holy he may be, should take to himself the name of pastor unless he can teach those whom he feeds. Or indeed it may be taken otherwise: that one and the same president of the Church is pastor and teacher, pastor of the sheep, teacher of men. For men and beasts you will save, O Lord (Ps. 35:7). I think that today too in the Churches, just as a prophet and an evangelist, and also a pastor and teacher, are found, so an apostle too can be found, in whom the signs and marks of apostleship are fulfilled; and that, on the other side, there are very many, outside as well as inside, in heresies as well as in the Church, who are pseudo-apostles, and pseudo-prophets, and pseudo-evangelists, and pseudo-pastors, and pseudo-teachers. And as for the heresies, indeed, there is no doubt that, in keeping with their false faith, everything they possess is false. But in the Churches, do they not seem to you to be false pastors who do not feed the sheep with discipline, but like hirelings give no thought at all to the safety of the flock, not turning back what has strayed and not seeking what has perished, but only grasping at milk and wool from the sheep, that is, at food and clothing? Nor indeed let us think that the Apostle burst out into these words suddenly and without order; but because he had stated beforehand that God is above all things, and through all things, and in all, and that grace has been given to each of the saints according to the measure of the gift of Christ, he now adds that some apostles, others prophets, others evangelists, others pastors and teachers have been distributed in the Church, who are necessary for the perfecting and instruction of the saints, for the work of the ministry, for the building up of the body of Christ: because, since the Church is the body of the Lord, and the Church is built of living stones, those whom we mentioned above as appointed in the Church have this as their work, that according to the dispensation and the offices entrusted to them they build up the Church of Christ, that is, his body. If anyone, then, does not build up the Church of Christ, nor instruct the people subject to him (so that the Church of Christ may be built from the subject people), he is to be called neither apostle, nor prophet, nor evangelist, nor pastor, nor teacher.

4:13-15

Until we all meet in the unity of the faith and of the knowledge of the Son of God, unto a perfect man, unto the measure of the age of the fullness of Christ. That we may no longer be children tossed to and fro, and carried about by every wind of doctrine, in the deceit of men and in craftiness, for the circumvention of error. But doing the truth in love, let us increase all things in him who is the head, Christ.

We must ask whom he means by the "all" who are to meet in the unity of the faith: all men, or all the saints, or at any rate all who are capable of reason? It seems to me, however, that he is speaking of all men, since there are many winds of doctrines; and when the waves have been stirred up by their blast, men are carried hither and thither on an uncertain course and by varied error. Hence we must labor with all zeal, first to meet in the unity of the faith, and then, in that same unity, to have the knowledge of the Son of God. When our possession of these is secure, ceasing to be children and receiving the measure of the inner man (which measure of fullness is the measure of Christ), we shall obtain the name of a perfect man: yet in such a way that56 the whole multitude of believers may arrive at the consummate age of the fullness of Christ, and, by no means wavering in doctrines, may not be carried like children on the uncertain flood of unbelief, while winds blowing from this side and that, namely the diverse and conflicting arguments of heretics or of the wise of this age, either bring shipwreck upon the hearers or threaten it; while some, without any will to deceive, nevertheless preach things that are false, and others, contriving the snares of error with every cunning, hasten to deceive and conquer us. But when the saints have met and arrived at the measure of the aforesaid man, then, understanding the love of Christ, they will increase in him all the seeds of truth which they had received, having the Lord Jesus as the head of the body of the Church. But since he says: That we may no longer be children tossed to and fro, and the rest, testifying that he too is tossed to and fro and a child, it seems we must reconsider whether he said this out of humility, or whether, seeing in part and knowing in part, he understood how far he was from perfect knowledge and burst out into words true to his own conscience. If anyone, then, holds that he said this out of humility, he will use this example: When I was a child, I spoke as a child, I thought as a child, I understood as a child. But when I became a man, I put away the things that were of a child (1 Cor. 13:11). Another, however, may reply to this that, by comparison with many, the Apostle had arrived at the age of the perfect man, but with respect to the things laid up for the saints he is still called a child. For indeed the prophet speaks of all the apostles who believe in Christ as children, saying: Behold, I and the children whom God has given me (Isa. 8:18). After this we must discuss more attentively whether perhaps the Apostle, truly out of humility, said not only that he was a child, but also tossed to and fro, and swept in different directions by every blast of doctrines, in the deceit of men, in wickedness, for the circumvention of error. But he who holds that he uttered this not out of humility but out of his conscience says: Paul the apostle was a man of sharp and keen intellect, one who at the very first attempts of disputants foresaw with a shrewd mind what was going to be inferred. He saw, therefore, that things were often argued on both sides in such a way, and such plausible claims were made for contrary positions, that they made the hearer doubt; and so, as a man still placed in a frail little body, he was indeed carried about by every wind of doctrine, but he was not dashed upon the rocks, nor was his ship filled by the floods: for he stood at the stern holding the helm, and broke the foaming waves of heresy by the security of faith. Yet not fearless and secure, nor quiet and calm, did he look out upon the winds blowing from this side and that, and with an anxious ear he caught them, and he overcame what was contrary: but his victory was not secure. Since, then, he saw that the words and arguments of his adversaries, by which they strove to overturn the truth, could not easily be overcome, being full of every cunning and woven together by the art of dialectic, nay rather of the devil, he hoped for God's help, that he might drive every deliberation from his mind, and believe without any trouble the sure and fixed truth, and increase it in the love of Christ, whom he knew to be the head both of himself and of the whole body of the Church. We should know that this passage is clearer in the Greek; but when it is translated into Latin word for word, the meanings, wrapped up in the words, have made57 what is said obscure.

4:16

From whom the whole body, compacted and glued together through every joint of supply, according to the working in the measure of each member, makes the increase of the body unto the building up of itself in love.

At the end of things, when we have begun to see God face to face, and have arrived at the measure of the age of the fullness of Christ (of whose fullness we have now all received (John 1), so that Christ is not in us in part but wholly; and when, having left behind the beginnings of children, we have grown up into that man of whom the Prophet says: Behold a man, the Dawn is his name (Zech. 6:12). And John the Baptist mentions him (John 1:30): After me comes58 a man who was made before me, because he was before me), then, in the meeting of one faith and of one knowledge of the Son of God, whom we now, because of the variety of minds, do not know by one and the same faith and knowledge, the whole body, which had previously been scattered and torn in different directions, will be brought back into its own framework and joining: so that one supply and the same working, and the consummate perfection of one age, may make the whole body grow equally, and all the members may receive the increase of age according to their own measure. And this whole building up, by which the body of the Church is increased part by part, will be completed by mutual love within itself. Let us understand all rational creatures under the example of a single rational living being, and whatever we say of its members and parts, let us know that this must be referred to each rational creature. Let us suppose this living being so torn apart through its limbs, veins and flesh that neither does bone cling to bone nor sinew join to sinew: the eyes lie apart, the nostrils separately, the hands occupy one place, the feet have been flung to another, and the remaining members are in this way scattered and divided from one another. Imagine some physician of such great knowledge coming that, according to the fables of the pagans, he could imitate Aesculapius and raise Virbius into a new form and a new name: he will have to restore each member to its own place, and fit joint to joint, and, the parts being restored, make one body with a kind of glue. Thus far let one comparison carry us; now let another example be drawn into the same comparison toward what we wish to be understood. Let a little child grow and, through unnoticed time, mature to perfect age: his hands will have their own increase, his feet will feel their own growth; his belly is filled while we do not know it; his shoulders broaden while the eyes are deceived; and all the members grow part by part according to their own measure in such a way that they seem to be increased not for themselves but for the body. So therefore also in the restoration of all things, when the true physician, Christ Jesus, comes to heal the body of the whole Church, now scattered and torn, each one, according to the measure of faith and of the knowledge of the Son of God (whom one is said to recognize (agnoscere) precisely because one knew him before and afterward ceased to know him), will receive his own place and will begin to be what he had been. Yet not in such a way that, according to another heresy, all are placed in one age, that is, all are re-formed into angels; but that each member is perfected according to its own measure and office: for example, that the apostate angel begins to be what he was created, and man, who had been cast out of paradise, is restored once more to the tilling of paradise. And all these things will come about in such a way that they are joined to one another in mutual love; and while member rejoices with member, and is glad at the advancement of another, the body of Christ, the Church of the firstborn, may dwell in the heavenly Jerusalem, which in another place the Apostle calls the mother of the saints (Gal. 4). Therefore (as we said above) these things are rather obscure in our language, because they are said μεταφορικῶς (metaphorically) in the Greek. And every metaphor, if it is translated word for word from one language into another, has the sense and the shoots of its speech choked as if by certain thorns.

4:17-19

This therefore I say and testify in the Lord, that you no longer walk as the Gentiles also walk, in the vanity of their mind, darkened in mind, alienated from the way of God through the ignorance that is in them, because of the blindness of their heart. Who, despairing, have given themselves over to unchastity, in the working of all impurity, in covetousness.

This therefore, he says, I say to you, O Ephesians, and I testify to you, that, since you are to meet in the measure of the age of the fullness of Christ, you do not walk as the Gentiles walk, who, serving idols, abuse both sense and mind for depravity. Though they received a soul and an understanding for this very purpose, that they might know God, they have been alienated from his way (and we know no other way apart from Christ), and they walk in the blindness of their own heart. And would that it had been enough to have sinned, and that they might at least late repent and condemn the vices after which they have continually gaped: there would be a remedy in coming to their senses after error. But now, despairing, and plunging themselves, after the manner of irrational beasts, into mire and the abyss, they have given themselves over to unchastity and luxury, working whatever the body wished, the mind desired, lust suggested. And since they have left out nothing whatever that is unclean, they have done all this in covetousness, since they are never sated with indulgence, and their pleasure has no limit. Or else, beyond the union of man with woman that is permitted, they climb to greater things, males working shamefulness with males, and receiving in themselves the recompense of their error. The vanity of the mind and the darkness of the mind are divided in two ways: into the business of this age, and into worldly wisdom: when we are either held fast in the things that belong to this world and quickly pass away, or come to know things that will be of no profit. Does it not seem to you that he walks in the vanity of mind and darkness of mind who is tormented day and night in the art of dialectic; who, as a searcher of nature, lifts his eyes beyond heaven, and plunges59 beyond the depth of the earth and of the abyss into a kind of void; who builds an iambus; who studiously sorts out and heaps up in his heart so great a forest of meters; and (to pass over the other part) who seeks riches by fair means and foul; who flatters kings, hunts for other men's inheritances, and gathers wealth without knowing to whom he will leave it in a moment? But as for his saying who, despairing, themselves, that is, ἀπηλγηκότες ἑαυτοὺς, it means something very different in the Greek from what it means in the Latin: for those despairing are called ἀπηλγηκότες; and ἀπηλγηκότες are those who, after they have sinned, feel no pain; who, in no way sensing their own ruin, are borne headlong, and, like beasts that see the steel, rush into death. Set before me two men caught in one vice: one who understands and laments what he has done; the other who delights in his crime, and not only does not grieve but even glories, and thinks he has won a kind of palm and victory of shamefulness: does it not seem to you that the former feels pain, and the latter feels none at all? Let us render, if we can, word for word, and say for ἀπηλγηκότες "past feeling" (indolentes), or "painless" (indolorios). For one of the philosophers also proclaimed ἀναλγησίαν, that is, painlessness (indoloriam). Let those who introduce different natures know that the Gentiles walk in the vanity of their mind with darkened minds for this reason: because they have given themselves over to ignorance and blindness. For no one is called ignorant and blind except one who is able to know and to see. Nor do we say that a stone is blind, or that a brute animal is ignorant: because it is not required of it, nor is it of its nature, that it should know and see. But if it was in the nature of the Gentiles to understand and see the life of God, then it is not the differing nature of the earthy and the spiritual, but the will. We said above that the working of all impurity in covetousness pertains not to covetousness, as it sounds at first hearing, but to lust and luxury. We ought to confirm this sense by the testimony of another passage. In the First Epistle to the Thessalonians it is written: For this is the will of God, your sanctification, that you abstain from fornication: that each one of you know how to possess his own vessel in sanctification and honor, not in the lust of desire, like the Gentiles also who do not know God: that no one overstep and circumvent his brother in business, because the Lord is the avenger in all these things, as we also told you beforehand and testified. For God has not called us in impurity, but in sanctification (1 Thess. 4:3 ff.). Observe carefully that, in calling us to chastity and wishing us to be content with our wives alone, he said: That no one overstep and circumvent his brother in business, that is, that no one, deserting his own wife, seek to defile the wife of another. Where we have and circumvent his brother in business, in the Greek is read καὶ πλεονεκτεῖν ἐν τῷ πράγματι τὸν ἀδελφὸν αὐτοῦ. And Πλεονεξία is called covetousness, which we, carrying over the force of the word, may express thus in the present passage: that no one overstep and, being covetous, defraud his brother in business. For what sequence is there, whether in that passage which we have now called in as an example, or in this one which we are principally trying to expound to the Ephesians, that between unchastity and impurity, and also chastity and conjugal affection, covetousness should suddenly and out of order be named? Let it not be tiresome to you if we linger long over the more obscure passages: for we pleaded at the beginning that, among all the Epistles of Paul, this one above all is involved both in its words and in its meanings.

4:20

But you did not so learn Christ; if indeed you have heard him and have been taught in him.

If all who seem to hear Christ really heard him, the Apostle would never have said to the Ephesians, and certainly to those to whom he had revealed the mysteries of Christ: If indeed you have heard him. To learn Christ, moreover, is the very same thing as to know virtue; and "hear him" is no different than if he said "to hear wisdom, righteousness, fortitude, temperance", and the other names by which Christ is called. If anyone, then, has heard and learned Christ, he will not walk in the vanity of his mind, nor will he go about darkened in mind, nor will he be alienated from the life of God; he will also have knowledge, ignorance having been dispelled, and, light having been let into the darkness, all blindness will be taken away from the eyes of his heart. And when he has this, he will not give himself over to unchastity, nor will he work all impurity in covetousness, overstepping the permitted bounds of marriage. But if at some time it should happen that he is overcome by some passion, he will grieve over his wound and suffer the torments of conscience, because he has lost a free brow and the purity of an unstained mind. Let us therefore learn Christ and hear him, if there is anyone who can say: Do you seek a proof of Christ who speaks in me (2 Cor. 13:3)? Let us run to him day and night; let us hang upon his mouth and upon his speech. Christ speaks to us: the words he brings forth are of the Holy Spirit. For God has set in the Church first apostles, secondly prophets, thirdly teachers. But neither must we despair that Christ himself may at some time speak in our minds and teach us by himself, and not seek the instrument of another's mouth: only let us not be subject to sin, nor let offenses possess our body, and wisdom will enter into it.

4:21

As the truth is in Jesus.

The name Jesus sometimes signifies that man who was assumed by God the Word from the Virgin, according to this: You shall call his name Jesus: for he will save the people from its sins (Matt. 1:21). And elsewhere: Jesus, therefore, wearied from the journey (John 4:6), and the rest. But sometimes it signifies God the Word: For to us there is one Lord Jesus Christ, through whom are all things. If ever, then, Jesus says: I am the way, and the truth (John 14:6), he says it according to that by which he is the Son of God. But when Paul writes: As the truth is in Jesus, he is speaking of the temple of the body, in which God the Word dwells. The Word indeed was made flesh, and dwelt in us (John 1:14). Unless perhaps both are to be understood of God the Word: that, just as life dwells in him and he himself is life: For as the Father has life in himself, so he has given to the Son also to have life in himself (John 5:26). So too the Son may be called the truth, and the truth itself be shown to dwell in him. We say these things, however, not separating Jesus from Christ, nor again saying that the man whom God the Word assumed is another than he; but, according to the understandings of effects or of places, naming differently, before the virgin and after the virgin, him whom we believe to be one, the Son of Man and the Son of God. It can also be understood otherwise. As the truth is in Jesus. In none of the patriarchs, in none of the prophets, in none of the apostles was the truth, except in Jesus alone. For the others knew in part, and prophesied in part, and saw through a mirror in an enigma (1 Cor. 13). In Jesus alone the truth of God appeared, which says confidently: I am the truth (John 14:6); which grants freedom to those who believe in it according to the analogy of faith. For he who knows the truth is presented with freedom by the truth. And the truth itself for this reason took the form of a slave, and humbled itself, becoming obedient to the Father unto death (Phil. 2), that it might make the slave free. For where the spirit of God is, there is freedom. If we wish to know the form of the free man in Jesus, and the face of the truth, let us go up with him onto the mountain, let us see him transfigured: where his garments too, that is, the Scriptures which announce him, are changed, and Moses also and Elijah, that is, the Law and the Prophets, are seen in glory (Matt. 17). As long as the Lord does not change the form of a slave and does not go up onto the mountain, the Law remains in filth and the Prophets in squalor. When he has climbed to the higher places, and has willed to change his garments, and the understanding, from being a slave, has become bright and free, then the faces and the garments of Moses and Elijah too are changed.

4:22

To put off, according to your former way of life, the old man, who is corrupted according to the desires of error.

Since, according to the order and texture of the language, the sentence seems to be in disorder, it must be read thus: But you did not so learn Christ (if indeed you have heard him and have been taught in him), to put off, according to your former way of life, the old man, who is corrupted according to the desires of error; as the truth is in Jesus; so that the sense is: As the truth is in Jesus, so will it be in you also who have learned Christ and have heard him; and you have been taught to put off, according to your former way of life, the old man, who is corrupted according to the desires of error. Now the old man, whom he commands to be put off, I think is so called as having grown old in malice. For he, according to his former way of life and the desires of error, ever erring and gorging himself in the work of corruption, is corrupted and violated. And because he is ceaselessly engaged in the desires of error and never ceases from vice, he is by no means said to have been corrupted, but to be corrupted: because through each single day, hour, point and moment he is corrupted, cut off, violated. But the word of God, which slays precisely in order to give life to the dead man, so that, once given life, he may seek the Lord whom before his destruction he did not know, does not corrupt the old man but slays him: For I kill, he says, and I make alive (Deut. 32:39); and: When he slew them, then they sought him (Ps. 77:34). Nor let us think that what he now says, Who is corrupted according to the desires of error, is contrary to what is written in another place: And though our outer man is corrupted, yet he who is inner is renewed from day to day (2 Cor. 4:16); because the corruption of the outer man is the renewal of the inner man, and conversely the corruption of the inner man is the restoration of the outer. Someone may suppose that Paul is speaking simply: If anyone corrupts the temple of God, God will corrupt him: For the temple of God is holy, which you are. But perhaps there too something lies hidden which seems concealed from us. If we are the temple of God, according to what is written: For the temple of God is holy, which you are (1 Cor. 3:17), then he who corrupts the temple of God is one, and that which is corrupted is another. We must ask who he is who corrupts the temple of God. If you find the enemies of Jerusalem corrupting and violating the temple built of stones, you will see likewise everyone who corrupts and violates the temple of God, whom God will corrupt and violate, avenging the corruption of his temple. Nevertheless, the temple too which offered itself (it is indeed living and capable of sensation) to the snares of the corrupter will pay the penalty, by this very fact that, having been corrupted and violated, it has lost the spirit of incorruption.

4:23-24

But be renewed in the spirit of your mind: and put on the new man, who was created according to God, in righteousness and holiness of truth.

We are neither renewed in the mind without the spirit, nor in the spirit without the mind; but we are renewed jointly in the spirit of our mind: so that, just as we sing psalms with the spirit and sing psalms with the mind as well, and pray with the spirit and pray with the mind as well, so we may be renewed in the spirit of our mind: so that when the mind has been made clean and purged, and is a stranger to every stain of filthy accretion, then the spirit too may be joined to it, and they may be so coupled together by a certain glue of unity that it is no longer called simply spirit, but the spirit of the mind. But when we have been renewed in the spirit, which is the spirit of our mind, then we shall put on the new man, who was created according to God. And this, I think, is in other words the very same thing that is said in another place: Put on Christ Jesus (Rom. 13:14). For this is the new man, with whom all of us who believe ought to be clothed and arrayed. For what in the man who was assumed by our Savior was not new? His conception, his nativity, his birth, his infancy, his teaching, his life, his mighty works: and at the last, the cross and the passion, in which he despoiled the principalities and made a show of the opposing powers: and the resurrection too, and the ascent into heaven. This man, then, was truly created in righteousness and holiness of truth: because he was true God, the Son of the true God, and in him all religion and righteousness was fulfilled by the truth of God. Whoever, therefore, is able to imitate his way of life and to express in himself all the virtues, and is meek, as he was meek and humble of heart, and lays down his life for his friends, as he laid down his for his sheep; who, when beaten, does not answer back, when cursed, does not curse in return, but overcomes pride by humility: this man has put on the new man, and can say with the Apostle: But I live, now not I, but Christ lives in me (Gal. 2:20). And: Be imitators of me, as I also am of Christ (1 Cor. 11:1). He can also take up the words of John: He who says that he believes in Christ ought himself also to walk just as he walked (1 John 2:6). But what he says, Who was created according to God, does not sound the same in the Latin tongue as in the Greek. For with us, "creation" (creatio) is used of generation or birth; but among the Greeks, under the name of creation, the word for making and founding is understood. And what among us is "founding" (conditio), this among the Greeks sounds as "creation." Hence also the heresy that slanders the nativity of Christ wrests to its use the example of Solomon: The Lord created me the beginning of his ways (Prov. 8:22). It must be considered, therefore, that "creation" and "founding" are never named except in the case of great works. For example, the world was created; a city was founded; but a house, however great it may be, is rather said to have been built than founded or created. For in great works and makings the word "creation" is taken up. From this it is to be noted that this new man, who was created according to God in Christ, is a great work of God and stands out beyond the other creatures: since he is said to have been founded in such a way as the world was, and to have been created as the beginning of the ways of God and at the outset of all the elements.

4:25

Wherefore, putting away lying, speak the truth each one with his neighbor: since we are members one of another.

It is not simply, as some think, nor in a moral sense that the Apostle commanded that, lying being abandoned, the truth must be spoken with our neighbors. Otherwise, if we speak the truth only with our neighbors, whoever is not a neighbor ought to hear a lie. This indeed is commanded in the Law as well: You shall not commit adultery with your neighbor's wife (Exod. 20:17). If "neighbor" is taken to mean only a kinsman or a friend, it rightly permits adultery against strangers: but it calls "neighbor" every man who was begotten from the same parent as we. This indeed is what that parable also signifies, about the man going down from Jerusalem to Jericho, who fell among robbers: and when the priest and the Levite passed by, he was cared for by a Samaritan and carried back to the innkeeper (Luke 10). After this the Lord affirms that he was the neighbor who showed him mercy, wishing to show that all men are neighbors to all. And this, understood in this way, edifies the hearers. But what follows, Since we are members one of another, seems to me rather to signify a mystery, and to speak of those who are neighbors to us in faith and virtue. For none are members one of another except the faithful of the faithful, and Christians of Christians, and the perfect of those who are of full and consummate virtue. Wherefore Paul, being himself perfect, said in another Epistle: But we speak wisdom among the perfect (1 Cor. 2:6). This, then, is what he commands: that each one should speak with his neighbor all things mystical and secret, and those things which are full of the truth of God, and that day should pour forth speech to day, and night show knowledge to night (Ps. 18), that is, that he should show all things clear and shining to those who deserve to hear: You are the light of the world (Matt. 5:14). But things dark and involved, and veiled in all the night of the mysteries, he should relate to those who are themselves night, darkness, or thick cloud, of whom it is said: And thick cloud was under his feet (Ps. 17:10): no doubt, God's feet. For on Mount Sinai too, Moses enters into the whirlwind and the thick cloud where God was (Exod. 19), and of God himself it is written: He made darkness his hiding place (Ps. 17:12). Let each one, therefore, speak truth and mystery with his neighbor, and not give what is holy to dogs, nor cast his pearls before swine (Matt. 7): but whoever have the oil of truth, those let him lead into the bridegroom's chamber and the king's storeroom. Further, as to what he says, Speak the truth each one with his neighbor, let us know that it is taken from the prophet Zechariah (Zech. 8:16).

4:26

Be angry, and do not sin.

That this has been borrowed from the fourth psalm no one doubts, and what is said elsewhere seems to be contrary to it: But now put away, all of you as well, anger, and indignation, and malice, and blasphemy, and foul speech from your mouth (Col. 3:8). And besides, understood simply, it does harm, since the reins of anger are thought to be loosened. The name of anger, however, is taken in a double sense, not only among us but among the philosophers as well: either when, provoked by an injury, we are stirred by natural goads; or when, the impulse having subsided and the rage having been quenched, the mind is able to exercise judgment, and nonetheless desires vengeance on him who is thought to have done the harm. I think, therefore, that it is the former anger that is spoken of here, and that it has been granted to us as men that we should be moved at the sight of something unworthy, and that the tranquillity of the mind should be troubled as if by some gentle breeze: but that we should by no means be lifted up by the impulse of rage into swelling whirlpools. Our Firmianus wrote a book On the Anger of God in language at once learned and eloquent, and I think that for whoever reads it, it can suffice amply and abundantly for an understanding of anger.

Let not the sun set upon your wrath. If we understand simply this sun, which is seen with the eyes of the flesh, we sin when we are angry and, as the sun sets, the wrath persists. But we do not sin when (for example), being angry from the first hour until the eleventh, we do what indignation, rage and anger have prompted. Than this sense nothing seems to me more absurd: as if anyone could not, from the rising of the sun to its setting, rave into such great crimes as he could not expiate with tears in his whole life? Or would not anger rather have its place in the day, since the night is surely the rest of rage, and, when sleep comes on, even if we are angry, we put off our wrath until the day? Since, then, the true sun, just as it sets upon the evil prophets, according to what is written, The sun has set upon your prophets at midday (Amos 8:9), so also sets upon all sinners, by no means granting them the light of its rising: the Apostle now commands that we, overcome by rage, should not do such things through which the sun may set for us, and the ruling part of the heart be wrapped in darkness. Some think that this is to be taken simply, in the same way as that verse of the fourth psalm, from which this very thing seems to have been taken: What you say in your hearts, be pricked with compunction for it on your beds as well (Ps. 4:5), that is: whatever sins you commit in the day, whether in deed, or word, or thought, purge them through the night by repentance coming after: and let anger be brief, and not be put off to the following day.

4:27

Nor give place to the devil.

Diabolus is a Greek word, which in Latin is called "accuser" (criminator): but in the Hebrew tongue he is called Satan, that is, "adversary" or "opponent": and by the Apostle, Belial (2 Cor. 6), that is, "without a yoke," because he has cast off from his neck the servitude of God: whom Aquila translated "apostate." And it should be known that wherever in the old Law "sons of pestilence" are written of, as in that place, But the sons of Eli, sons of pestilence (1 Sam. 2), there in the Hebrew volumes Belial, that is, the devil, is named in place of "pestilence": although very many, in place of Belial, read Beliar in the Apostle, corruptly. Do not, therefore, he says, give place to the devil, who, like a roaring lion, seeks an entrance through which he may break in. For just as the Father and the Son stand at the door and knock, that they may come in and sup with him who has received them (Rev. 3): so also the adversary is always ready to break in upon us, and when we have given him place, he enters. But before he comes, he is accustomed to send ahead certain darts, and to make a thought the forerunner of his coming: if we receive this thought in our heart and nourish it within, and make it grow, when he sees his offspring increased in us, he too will dare to enter. Thus into the heart of Judas Iscariot he first cast the arrow, that he should betray the Savior; and if that wretched man had not received it and cherished it, Satan would never have entered into him after the bread was dipped in the dish. And at the same time pay careful attention to this as well: that the devil did not find a place for entering into Judas, whose breast he had already struck before, except at the Savior's banquet (John 13). For we are given into his power most of all then, when we are not overcome by the kindness, nor the clemency, nor the meekness of him whom we hate undeservedly. With this, which he now commands, nor give place to the devil, is compared that saying from Ecclesiasticus: If the spirit of one who has power rises up over you, do not give up your place to him (Eccles. 10:4). He, proud and reckless, wishes to ascend, wishes to come up: but even if he thinks you have been crushed, and has exalted himself, do not you give place. For the power of the devil lies not in his recklessness and boasting, but in your will.

4:28

He who used to steal, let him steal no longer: but rather let him labor, working with his own hands what is good: that he may have something to share with him who is in need.

Because those who are occupied in the business of this life are compelled, for the sake of food and necessary uses, either to buy or to sell certain things, and to pursue profits from trading: and it is difficult even for those who have been freed from the other passions, namely fornication, idolatry, adultery and murder, not to be held by this vice. Therefore he now warns the Ephesians not to incur the crime of theft under the pretext of gain, calling "theft" everything that is sought to another's loss; and he says that it is just that each one, working with his own hands and acquiring his livelihood by labor, should share with those who do not have. Nor indeed does he say: "But rather let him labor, working with his own hands what is good, that he may not be in need, and may have his livelihood, and may be a burden to no one": but, "Let him labor," he says, "with his own hands what is good, that he may have the means to share with the needy." He, therefore, who labors only to this end, that he himself may not be in want, and draws back his hand from others, although he may applaud himself, has nevertheless not fulfilled the Apostle's precept. But he who used to steal, let him steal no longer, and the rest, can also be understood more deeply, because of what is written about the false prophets: Who steal words, each one from his neighbor (Jer. 23:30). And in the Gospel: All who came before me were thieves and robbers (John 10:8). And to the Romans: You who preach that one must not steal, you steal (Rom. 2:21): meaning that we are forbidden to commit spiritual thefts. For neither can what follows, but rather let him labor, working with his own hands what is good, be fittingly referred to the necessities of this life, so that whatever is going to perish and belongs to the mammon of iniquity should be called good. For although just labor may have wealth acquired without evasion, it will be enough if such wealth is not called evil: but it cannot be called good. He, therefore, works what is good who turns aside from evil and does good, and works in the field of his own soul, so that he may be filled with spiritual loaves and may be able to lend to the hungry and to him who endures need, giving his fellow servants their rations in due season: but if he who works what is good is such a one, then it follows that he who steals steals words and doctrines, living by theft, sewing pillows for himself out of theft, and gathering the rags of the Scriptures from here and there, so as to be able to make a torn tunic, which is from below, not from above. For the tunic of the Church, that is, the body of Christ, is woven from above, and in no part stitched together, a tunic which cannot be torn even by its enemies.

4:29

Let no evil speech proceed out of your mouth. But if any is good, for the edification of opportunity, that it may give grace to the hearers.

Good speech, which is for the edification of opportunity, giving grace to the hearers, is that which teaches that virtues must be followed and vices fled. Evil speech is that which provokes to sins and incites those who are already inclined still more to ruin. But in place of what we have put, for the edification of opportunity (opportunitatis), which is what is said in Greek, τῆς χρείας, the translator in the Latin manuscripts, for the sake of euphony, made a change and put for the edification of faith (fidei). As often as anyone profits from our speech, and we edify the hearers according to the opportuneness of place, time and person, good speech has proceeded out of our mouth. But as often as we speak either out of season, or in an unsuitable place, or not as befits the hearers, so often evil speech proceeds out of our mouth, to the destruction of those who hear. Let us consider, therefore, what we say, since for every idle word we are going to render an account on the day of judgment (Matt. 12). And even if we do no harm, yet do not edify, we must pay the penalty of an evil word.

4:30

And do not grieve the Holy Spirit of God, in whom you were sealed in the day of redemption.

The grief of the Holy Spirit is to be understood in the same way as the anger of God and his sleep, and the other passions spoken of after the likeness of human beings. Not that the Spirit is saddened, or that the Godhead feels any disturbance; but that from our own words we may learn the affections of God: that he grieves as often as we sin, and weeps over sinners. For the Savior too, when he was set in the body, wept over Jerusalem (Luke 19), and in the prophet he laments the whole race of men, saying: Woe is me, O soul, for he who returns60 has perished from the earth. And there is none among men to set things right: all are judged in blood (Mic. 7). And in Ezekiel, recounting the deeds of the once holy city, he says: In all these things you grieved me (Ezek. 16:43). But we have been sealed with the Holy Spirit of God, so that both our spirit and our soul may be stamped with the seal of God, and we may receive again that image and likeness after which we were made in the beginning. This seal of the Holy Spirit, according to the Savior's utterance, is sealed with God doing the stamping. For him, he says, God the Father has sealed (John 6:27). Therefore everyone is sealed by the Father with the Holy Spirit who, from the fact that he has believed God, has set his seal that God is true. And he is sealed for this reason: that he may keep the seal and show it in the day of redemption pure and unalloyed and in no part mutilated, and on that account be able to be numbered with those who have been redeemed.

Book Three

In the preface to the first book, O Paula and Eustochium, I discussed fully and more than fully the argument of Paul's Epistle to the Ephesians; and here and there, wherever occasion was given, though briefly, I have shown that the blessed Apostle wrote to none of the churches so mystically, and to none revealed the mysteries hidden from the ages. Now therefore, since, supported by the help of your prayers and of the holy Marcella, I am dictating the third, that is, the last, book on the same Epistle, it seems right to me to show that the etymology of the name itself also agrees with the meaning I set out above. Ephesus, rendered into the Latin language, means "will", or "my counsel in her", or at any rate "my soul in her". The will and the counsel and the soul of God are in him who can say: For he himself gave me knowledge of all things (Eccles. 1, and 1 John 5); and when the uncertain and hidden things of God's wisdom have been revealed to him, he will obtain the testimony of the one who says: I have found David, the son of Jesse, from Bethlehem, a man after my own heart, who will do61 all my will (Acts 13:22). The prophet Hosea also signifies a man of this kind, saying: Who is wise, and will understand62 these things; prudent, and will recognize them (Hos. 14:10)? Moreover, that you may know that there is a great distance between the simple righteous man and the wise righteous man, observe what glory each will attain in the resurrection of the dead. And many of those who sleep in the mud of the earth shall rise: these to eternal life, and those to reproach and eternal confusion. And those who understand shall shine like the splendor of the firmament, and many of the righteous like the stars for ever (Dan. 12:2). The righteous, he says, shall shine like the stars for ever; and those who understand, that is, who have knowledge of the Scriptures, like the splendor of heaven. Not that the learned man ought not also to be righteous; but that he who is righteous, unless he has been instructed, is as far from the wise righteous man as the gleam of the stars is from the light of the firmament. But if anyone, instructed only by meditation on the law, neglects his life, and does not dare to say: From your commandments I have understood; therefore I was directed to all your commandments (Ps. 118:104), that man, like sounding brass and a tinkling cymbal and salt that has lost its savor, is to be trampled underfoot in the dung (1 Cor. 13, Mark 9, and Luke 14). But if the choice between the two were given (setting aside the man who has both wisdom and righteousness), I for my part would rather have righteous rusticity than learned malice. For in the one case, though the glory is lesser, it is still glory to be made equal to the light of the stars; in the other, the punishments are greater in proportion to the advance in knowledge. The mighty shall suffer torments (Wisd. 6:7); and: The servant who knew his lord's will and did not do it shall be beaten with many stripes (Luke 12:47). I have said these things for this reason: to show why the name of the Ephesians sounds "the soul and counsel and will of God": they who, abandoning the illusions of the magic arts, transferred the zeal of error to the pursuit of truth. For their salvation Paul fought with so much sweat that he wrote to the Corinthians: If according to man I fought with beasts at Ephesus, what does it profit me, if the dead do not rise (1 Cor. 15:32)? What are these beasts? Those, surely, of which the Psalmist prays, saying: Do not hand over to the beasts the soul that confesses to you (Ps. 73:19). And in another place: Rebuke the wild beasts of the reed (Ps. 67:31). For our adversary the devil goes about like a roaring lion (1 Pet. 5:8). When he saw the chief city of Asia being snatched from his jaws at the teaching of Paul, he gathered all the troops of his henchmen and strove to crush him, and, insolently exalting himself, he wished like an eagle to set his nest above him (Isa. 14, Prov. 4). The Apostle perceived this, and keeping his heart with all watchfulness (since indeed he was not ignorant of his wiles), he spoke after the victory, it is true, but after a victory not bloodless: For we do not wish you to be ignorant, brothers, of our tribulation which came upon us in Asia: that we were weighed down beyond measure, beyond our strength, so that we were weary even of living (2 Cor. 1:8). And the fact that the Epistle is sent by Tychicus accords well with the mysteries of the same Epistle (1 Thess. 4). Of these the title of the ninth psalm also gives notice in advance: for the secrets of the son. For Tychicus is interpreted "silent": one who does not cast pearls before swine nor give what is holy to dogs, and who speaks freely to God. In my heart I have hidden your words, that I may not sin against you (Ps. 118:11).

4:31

Let all bitterness, and rage, and anger, and clamor, and blasphemy be put away from you, with all malice.

Bitterness is the contrary of sweetness: hence people are commonly called "bitter" and "sweet". Of it Jeremiah too speaks, saying: And your bitterness has risen up over me (Jer. 15:17). Rage is anger beginning, indignation boiling up in the mind. Anger, on the other hand (of which bitterness and rage are species), is that which, once the rage has died down, desires vengeance, and wishes to injure the one it thinks has done it harm. These things, although they are often said of God, according to that verse: Lord, do not rebuke me in your rage, nor chastise me in your anger (Ps. 6:1), are not to be reckoned perturbations of the mind as they are in us; for in him all things are measured and ordered, and the punishment by which sinners are corrected is called by our words. But we, if we are angry, are disturbed, and, swept away by rage, cease to be ourselves. Therefore all bitterness and rage and anger must be put away from us entirely. For in that saying of the Gospel: Whoever is angry with his brother without cause shall be liable to judgment (Matt. 5:22), the words without cause have been added to no purpose, since we are not permitted to be angry even with cause, as the Apostle now says most plainly: Let bitterness and rage and anger be taken away from you; and as the thirty-sixth psalm removes all agitation of the mind in general: Cease from anger, and let go of rage. For if anger desires vengeance, and every vengeance longs to pay back evil to the one by whom it thinks itself injured, and the Christian ought not to render evil for evil, but to overcome evil with good (1 Pet. 3, and Rom. 12:19), and: Vengeance is mine, and I will repay, says the Lord (Deut. 32:35), then everyone who is angry sins: For the anger of man does not work the righteousness of God (Jas. 1:20). After bitterness, rage and anger, clamor too and blasphemy are rightly forbidden in us. For whoever has once been overcome by rage must needs burst out into clamor, and, roaring turbulently, be tossed this way and that like a leaf, and say: O the iniquity of things! O the unjust judgments of God! and the other things that those are wont to say who through the rage of indignation have lost the judgment of the mind. Moreover, blasphemy is not only the open kind that is born of anger; it is also uttered without anger, with a calm mind: if someone either complains about the government of this world and says: That ought not to have been so, and this ought to have been so; or else, though placed in the Church and believing in God, slips in doctrines of which it is not permitted to be ignorant, thinking otherwise of the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit than the truth of the matter holds, not believing in the resurrection of the dead as the Scriptures teach; or else, envying another's wisdom, declares that a man who is of the catholic faith thinks wrongly, and in turn, for the flattery with which a heretic courts him, attests that the heretic is catholic: he who calls sweet bitter, and bitter sweet. Therefore we must read the Scriptures with all diligence, and meditate on the law of the Lord day and night, so that, like approved money-changers, we may know which coin is genuine and which counterfeit. Further, let us so take away from ourselves bitterness, rage, anger, clamor and blasphemy that they are put away together with all malice. Malice, moreover, is to be understood either as the contrary of virtue, which by another name we call vice, or as malignity and wickedness, which is perceived in evasion and cunning.

4:32-5:1

But be kind to one another, merciful, granting to yourselves, as God also in Christ has granted to you.

Above we had spoken of sweetness as the contrary of bitterness; this the Apostle now calls by another word, χρηστότητα, that is, pleasantness rather than kindness. He commands that, having condemned all bitterness and rage, anger, clamor and blasphemy, and every turbulent agitation, together with a certain severity of brow, we should be gentle and winning, and should of our own accord invite people into familiarity with us, so that no one may be afraid to approach us: a familiarity which is gained above all through mercy. Nor, in furnishing things to others, do we ourselves straightway cease to have what we have given; for there follows: granting to yourselves (donantes); because what is done well toward another is laid up rather for the one who furnished it than for the one to whom it was given. He who has pity on the poor shall himself be filled; and he who gives to him lends at interest to God. Or else it is to be taken in this way: that in being pleasant and merciful, and in having left behind the perturbations that disquieted us and passed over to mildness and self-control, something has been given and granted to ourselves, while we are turned from bad men into good, and we grant to ourselves these very things which God the Father granted in Christ. For if, having abandoned the vices, we pursue the virtues, and all the virtues, wisdom, truth, righteousness, mildness and the rest, are understood and referred to Christ, then when we have these virtues we also acquire them for ourselves by our own effort, and we cause ourselves to possess those which God granted us in Christ. Another, however, will take his words granting to yourselves simply: that as it is said above, But be pleasant to one another, so now too it is said, granting to yourselves, in the sense of "granting to one another": so that, as God in Christ has granted us our sins, so we too may forgive those who have sinned against us. And to prove that the remission of sins is called in the Scriptures a granting (donatio), he will take the example that is written in Luke, where one man owed a creditor five hundred denarii and another fifty (Luke 7), which he remitted to both; and at the Savior's question, the one who had been asked answered that he had the greater gratitude to whom more had been granted. It is said also in the Lord's Prayer: Forgive us our debts, as we also forgive our debtors (Matt. 6:12). Now God has granted to us in Christ, not as placed outside him but as dwelling in him: for the Father is in the Son, and the Son in the Father. Nor is he in whom the granting is made therefore less than he who grants in him; for the Apostle too says: And I, if I have granted anything to you, in the face of Christ and of God (2 Cor. 2:10).

Be therefore imitators of God, as most dear sons. Whoever understands how it was said: Be perfect, as your heavenly Father also is perfect (Matt. 5:48), will know how it is now said too: Be imitators of God (1 Cor. 4:16). And writing to the Corinthians he says: Be imitators of me, as I also am of Christ (1 Cor. 11:1). For they could not at once become imitators of Christ; but it was a great thing for them if they could become imitators of an imitator. To the Ephesians, however, as to those whom he had already taught such great mysteries, he does not say, Be imitators of me, nor, imitators of Christ, but, imitators of God. Not that it is a lesser thing to be an imitator of Christ than of God (for Christ is indeed God), but that it is one thing to imitate according to man, another according to God. For even if we once knew Christ according to the flesh, now we no longer know him according to the flesh. The Savior himself also speaks, showing the humility of the dispensation: Whatever he sees the Father doing, these same things the Son also does likewise (John 5:19). Not that the Father made one heaven and one earth, and that after their likeness another heaven and another earth and the other elements were made by Christ; but that whatever the Father works, these same things the Son also works. In what respect we can become like God he has attested above, saying: granting to you, as God also in Christ has granted to you. For I do not think that man can imitate God also in all the other things whatever that God has done; but, for example, that as he is merciful, and rains upon the good and the evil, and the rest, so we too should pour out our goodness upon all men. When we have done this, we shall be sons63 beloved, whether of Paul himself or, as I think better, of God.

5:2

And walk in love, as Christ also loved us, and gave himself up for us, an offering and a sacrifice to God for an odor of sweetness.

He who fights against sin even to blood for the salvation of others, so that he even gives up his life for them, walks in love, imitating Christ, who loved us so much that he endured the cross for the salvation of all. For as he gave himself up for us, so this man too, willingly laying down his life for those he can, will imitate him who gave himself up to the Father as an offering and a sacrifice for an odor of sweetness, and he himself also will become an offering and a sacrifice of God for an odor of sweetness.

5:3-4

But fornication and all impurity, or covetousness, let it not even be named among you, as befits saints; and obscenity, and foolish talk, or scurrility, which are not to the purpose; but rather thanksgiving.

Had there not arisen among the philosophers a certain Cynic who taught that every titillation of the flesh, and the flow of seed coming from any rubbing and touching whatever, was not to be avoided in its season, and had not some of the wise of this age assented to this shameful and blush-worthy heresy, the holy Apostle, writing to the Ephesians, would never have coupled all impurity as well to fornication, and joined covetousness to impurity: not that covetousness by which we desire to amass money, but the one of which we spoke above: that he not overreach, and as a covetous man defraud his brother in the matter. That is, because, insatiable and never filled, he runs about through every kind of obscenity and wantonness. As befits, he says, saints. From this it follows that no one can be called a saint who, apart from fornication, is found in any impurity and covetousness of the pleasures that have delighted him. But if anyone thinks that covetousness is not to be taken in the sense we have given, let him render the reasons why, among fornication and impurity and obscenity and foolish talk and scurrility, he set covetousness in the middle, out of the regular order. Moreover, I consider foolish talk to belong not only to those who relate shameful things to raise a laugh, and with feigned silliness rather make sport of those whom they wish to please, but also to those who are reckoned the wise of this age and who, disputing about natural things, say that they have clearly comprehended the sands of the shores, the drops of the Ocean, the expanse of the heavens and the point of the earth. There is foolish talk in the Church too: if anyone thinks that heaven is curved like a vault, deceived by the words of Isaiah, whom he does not understand; and also that a throne is set in the heavens, and God sits upon it, and that, after the fashion of an emperor and a judge, angels stand round about, to obey the words of the one who commands and to be sent off on their various duties (Isa. 6). But since scurrility follows foolish talk, foolish talk is rather to be applied to silly and inept tales. Between foolish talk and scurrility there is this difference: foolish talk has in it nothing wise and nothing worthy of a man's heart; scurrility, on the other hand, comes down from a shrewd mind, and deliberately seeks out certain words, whether urbane or rustic or indecent or witty (what we may call by another word jocularity), to raise a laugh among the hearers. But these things too are to be driven far away from holy men, for whom it is more fitting to weep and mourn; as we also read in the Hebrew Gospel, where the Lord speaks to the disciples: And never, he says, be glad, except when you have seen your brother in love. Up to this point he seems to have brought in nothing outside his purpose, nothing outside the logical sequence and the texture of the order. But as to what follows, and is placed at the end, But rather thanksgiving, someone may ask, and say, what does thanksgiving mean after the prohibition of fornication and impurity and wantonness and obscenity and foolish talk and scurrility? For if he was once free to set down any virtue whatever, he could have said: but rather righteousness, truth, love. But just as this is inconsequent, so those too could have been inconsequent, and by the same license would have had no order. Perhaps, then, the thanksgiving (gratiarum actio) named in this place is not the one by which we give thanks to God, but the one by which we are called pleasing or gracious (gratiosus), and witty64, among men. For it does not befit a Christian to be a foolish talker and a buffoon; but it does befit his speech to be seasoned with salt, so that it may have grace with those who hear. And since it is not customary among the Greeks, except among certain learned men, to say εὐχαριστίαν65 as distinct from eucharistia, that is, being gracious as distinct from giving thanks, I therefore think that the Apostle, as a Hebrew of the Hebrews, used the common word, and wished to set out his meaning by the signification of another word; especially since among the Hebrews "gracious" and "giving thanks" are expressed, as they say, by one word. Hence I think that in Proverbs too it is written thus: γυνὴ εὐχάριστος ἐγείρει ἀνδρὶ δόξαν, a grateful woman raises up glory for her husband (Prov. 11:16), in the sense of "gracious". We would seem to be doing violence to Scripture, and boldly taking a thanksgiving woman for a gracious one, did not the other editions also agree with our opinion. For Aquila and Theodotion and Symmachus put it thus: γυνὴ χάριτος, that is, a gracious woman, and not εὐχάριστος, which pertains to the giving of thanks.

5:5

But know this, that every fornicator, or impure man, or covetous man, which is serving idols, has no inheritance in the kingdom of Christ and of God.

It is to be noted that of the six vices forbidden above, fornication, impurity, covetousness, obscenity, foolish talk and scurrility, he has now set down only three, fornication, impurity and covetousness: whoever is guilty of these cannot have an inheritance in the kingdom of Christ and of God. For if the foolish talker and the buffoon were estranged from the kingdom of God in the same way as the three whom he has specially set apart, the sentence would seem cruel, not pardoning the weakness of human frailty, since even words said in jest would condemn us. For he who does not slip in speech is perfect (Jas. 3:2). Nor yet, in saying these things, do we give place to foolish talk and scurrility on the ground that they are not excluded from the kingdom; but just as with the Father there are diverse mansions, and star differs from star in glory (John 14; 1 Cor. 15), so also is the resurrection of the dead: although someone be a stranger to fornication, impurity and wantonness, yet if he has been a foolish talker and a buffoon, he will not hold the place which he was to possess if he did not have these vices. Someone may answer: Granted that foolish talk and scurrility do not carry the same guilt as fornication, impurity and covetousness, ought he not also to have named obscenity together with the three above? To this it must be said that obscenity here signifies a hidden thought, when our sense is inflamed to lust, and the soul, set on fire, is kindled by the ticklings of the flesh, and is nevertheless reined in by the fear of God and the judgment of the mind. Indeed, above too he named the three together, without obscenity, saying: But fornication and all impurity and covetousness, let it not even be named among you: and after that obscenity was counted together with foolish talk and scurrility. And just as foolish talk and scurrility, so also this obscenity does not destroy, nor does it exclude from the kingdom forever. But since in the preceding passages, from what we had read elsewhere, Let him not go beyond, and as a covetous man defraud his brother in business (1 Thess. 4:6), we had said that covetousness was put for adultery, we ask whether what is now said, or covetous man, which is serving idols, agrees with that interpretation or with the common one. We find in very many places of the prophets that idolatry is called fornication. They committed fornication, he says, after their idols (Hos. 4:12). And: They have been led astray by the spirit of fornication. Therefore fornication can also be understood of idolatry. But if that covetous man is taken to be the one who, gathering money by any means at all, longs to have coins by right and by wrong, and delights in a full purse, he is an idolater in this, that he worships the engraving of the coin itself and venerates the idols engraved on the coins. As the belly is the god of gluttons, so too money can most justly be called the god of the greedy, especially since in another place the apostle calls greed idolatry. Besides this, we must see what he meant by saying in the kingdom of Christ and of God (1 Cor. 10): whether the kingdom of Christ is one thing and that of God another, or whether the kingdom of the Father and of the Son is one and the same. And indeed, if he had said, "in the kingdom of the Son and of the Father," we would come through the Son to the Father; and although there would be a diversity of persons, yet there would be one majesty of those who reign. But now, since he has said in the kingdom of Christ and of God, let us understand God himself, and Christ, because even when he has delivered the kingdom to God and the Father, it will not be the Father who is all in all, but God who is all in all. But where God is, both the Father and the Son can be understood. Further, what we say of the Father and of the Son, let us hold the same also of the Holy Spirit.

5:6

Let no one deceive you with empty words: for because of these things the wrath of God comes on the sons of unbelief.

Words that deceive and trip us up are empty and void; but those that build up the hearers are full, heaped up, packed tight. Since, then, there are very many who say that there will be no punishments for sins, nor torments applied from outside, but that sin itself and the consciousness of the offence serve as the penalty, while the worm does not die in the heart and a fire is kindled in the mind, after the likeness of a fever, which does not torment66 the sick man from outside, but, seizing the bodies themselves, punishes what it possesses without the application of tortures from outside: these persuasions, then, and fraudulent snares he has called empty and void words, which seem to have a certain flower of speech and to flatter sinners; but while they grant confidence, they rather carry them off to eternal punishments. For at nothing is God so angered as when a sinner is proud and, upright and stiff, is not bent to weeping, nor asks mercy for his offence. For because of these things the wrath of God comes upon the sons of unbelief, or, of unpersuadability: for ἀπηθεία (disobedience) can be understood as the contrary of persuasion rather than of trust. The sons of unpersuadability, or of unbelief, are so called in the way that there are sons of perdition, and sons of fornication, and sons of death, and sons of gehenna, and other things like these, which it is very easy to find in various places of the Scriptures.

5:7

Do not, therefore, become fellow partakers with them.

He becomes a partaker (particeps), or a fellow partaker (comparticeps), with the sons of unbelief who is found in fornication, and in impurity, and covetousness, because of which the wrath of God comes upon the sons of unbelief. And he is indeed a partaker of them from that in which he shares, and he has fellowship in evil works; but that one is called a fellow partaker who is a partaker together with others: and in a fellow partaker a partaker too is understood, whereas in a partaker a fellow partaker is not at once included. Observe carefully the word comparticeps and the word particeps. For I think that in the Scriptures particeps is always taken in a good sense, comparticeps in a bad one. For example: Therefore God, your God, has anointed you with the oil of exultation above your partakers (Ps. 44:8); and in another place: For we have been made partakers of Christ, if indeed we hold the beginning of the substance firm to the end (Heb. 3:14). Further, I do not remember having read comparticeps anywhere else except in the present passage; and yet it is evident that here it is set not in a good sense but in the contrary one.

5:8

For you were once darkness, but now light in the Lord.

If it is possible for darkness to be turned into light, there is not, as certain heretics hold, a nature that perishes and cannot receive salvation. Let us then ask those who invent these things whether all the ungodly are darkness or not: of whom some, though they were called darkness because of their malice, have been converted to better things and are now called light in the Lord. And just as the righteous are the light of the world, so the ungodly will consequently be called darkness; and the righteous indeed, since they are light, will see light in light; but the unrighteous, since they are darkness, are a people sitting in darkness and seeing nothing. Their difference from one another and the distance between them we understand from their fruits. For everyone who works evil hates the light, and, not coming to the light, is dark, and a son of night and of darkness. But he who works the truth and comes into the light is light, and a son of light and of day (John 3:20, 21). The luminous and the dark are known from the light or the darkness of the heart. Beyond this you will ask whether perhaps it is said of the righteous, but now light in the Lord, to distinguish those who are light but are not light in the Lord. It is fittingly written also to the Ephesians, who had climbed to the summit of knowledge, that they are light in the Lord. Nor is darkness itself turned into light, or light changed into darkness; but those who, from what they are, have earned the name either of virtue or of vice, if they have been converted from the one to the other, will receive the title either of darkness or of light ὁμωνύμως (by a shared name) with the things by which they are possessed.

Walk as sons of light. If God is light, and there is no darkness in him, the sons of God are sons of light. And also, if Christ is the true light, his sons too, to whom he speaks, saying, My little children, yet a little while I am with you (John 13:33), are sons of the true light. From this it is gathered that the same ones are sons of God the Father who are sons of Christ Jesus.

5:9

For the fruit of light is in all goodness and righteousness and truth.

Against Marcion (who separates the righteous God from the good one, and thinks that the Creator is righteous, but that another, I know not who, whose son this Christ who came is, is the God who is only good) let us bring forward this testimony. For indeed the fruit of light is not only in goodness, but in righteousness and in truth. Where, therefore, there is goodness, there is also righteousness; where righteousness, there consequently is truth also. With the good Father of Christ, then, as they themselves also confess, there is truth and goodness. But where there are goodness and truth, there, with him and not with another, as the Apostle now teaches, is righteousness. Let Marcion also understand that Christ himself is called goodness, truth and righteousness: goodness, in that he gives grace to those who believe in him not according to works but according to mercy; righteousness, in that he renders to each one what he deserves; and truth, in that he alone knows the causes of all creatures and of all things.

5:10

Proving what is well pleasing to God.

All things are to be done with counsel, so that, cautious and careful, we may do only those things which we know to please God, after the manner of a most prudent money-changer, who tests an engraved coin not only by the eye but also by its weight and its ring. But since in this place the connection of the discourse seems to be disordered, and the whole sentence to be in turmoil, the passage must be restored to order thus: Do not therefore become fellow partakers with them, proving what is well pleasing to God: for though you were once darkness, yet now you are light in the Lord: walk as sons of light, showing the fruit of the light in all goodness, and righteousness, and truth.

5:11

And do not have fellowship with the unfruitful works of darkness.

In writing to the Galatians also he put the name of fruit in the spirit, but that of work in the flesh, saying: Now the works of the flesh are manifest, which are fornication (Gal. 5:19), and the rest. But the fruit of the spirit is love, joy, peace (ibid., 22), and so on. Now, in the present passage, he has called the works of darkness unfruitful: those who have done them are joined to them in fellowship.

But rather even reprove. Among the other commands, to be able also to reprove those who sin belongs to the greatest freedom. But he can do this who does not deserve to hear: Hypocrite, cast out first the beam from your eye, and then you will be able to cast out the mote from your brother's eye (Luke 6:42). For this reason the prophets too, being themselves polluted by no filth of sins and not having a seared conscience, were able to reprove others who went astray. From this it must be observed that he can reprove who is not himself reproved within himself.

5:12

For the things that are done by them in secret, it is shameful even to speak of.

It does not seem to me that the connection of the discourse holds, such that what is now said, For the things that are done by them in secret, could depend on what precedes, and it could be known to whom above all it should be referred, unless perhaps the hyperbaton is traced further back and it is joined to the sons of unbelief, so that it can sound thus: for the things that are done in secret by the sons of unbelief, it is shameful even to speak of, which are fornication and impurity and all covetousness.

5:13

But all things that are reproved are made manifest by the light: for everything that is made manifest is light.

All things: no doubt those which are done in secret by the sons of unbelief; for these, being reproved, are made manifest by the light. Now the light reproves those things which before were darkness and afterwards became light in the Lord, so that, from the very fact that they are rebuked, they may be changed for the better, and once changed may be made manifest in public, and once made public may be light, because everything that is made manifest is light.

5:14

Wherefore he says: Arise, you who sleep, and rise up from the dead, and Christ will enlighten you (or, Christ will rise upon you).

Since the works of darkness, when reproved by the light, are made manifest into light, the things that had been reproved being changed, it is said to those who sleep and are dead, because they have done the works of darkness: Arise, you who sleep, and rise up from the dead. Someone may ask who this is that says: Arise, you who sleep, and rise up from the dead; or whose testimony the Apostle has made use of? And indeed one who is content with a simple answer will say that he brought these words forward, having read them in the hidden prophets and in those writings called apocrypha (as it is clear he did in other places also), not because he approved of the apocrypha, but in the same way that he made use of the verses of Aratus, and of Epimenides, and of Menander, to prove what he wished at the time. And yet not everything that Aratus, and Epimenides, and Menander wrote is holy because he attested that they said something true. Another, however, will say that the Apostle fashioned, as it were, a προσωποποιΐαν (personification) of the Holy Spirit, and will relate that these words were spoken as an exhortation to repentance. I certainly, according to my poor little capacity, sifting diligently through all the editions of the ancient Scriptures and the very volumes of the Hebrews, have never found this written. Unless perhaps we say this too: that just as the prophets of old used to speak in the assembly of the people, Thus says the Lord, and, For the Lord has spoken, so also the Apostle, full of the Holy Spirit, suddenly broke out into the words which Christ was speaking in him and said: Thus says the Lord. Moreover, this too must be discussed: how to one and the same person it is said, as to one living, Arise, you who sleep, and as to one dead, Rise up from the dead. Therefore, since there is both the spirit of man, which we remember is always written of in a good sense, and the soul, whose infirmities and deaths of sins we read of, let what is now said, Arise, you who sleep, be referred to the spirit, and what follows, Rise up from the dead, be fitted to the soul. For the soul that has sinned, it shall die (Ezek. 18:4). But of the death of the spirit we never read at all. Christ, then, the true light, will rise upon him who has risen from sleep and shall have been raised from the dead. I know that I heard someone discoursing on this passage in the Church who, as a theatrical marvel, displayed to the people a form never seen before, in order to please, saying: This testimony is spoken to Adam, buried in the place of Calvary, where the Lord was crucified. And it was called Calvary for this reason, that the head of the ancient man was buried there. At that time, then, when the Lord was crucified, he was hanging above Adam's tomb, and this prophecy was fulfilled, which says: Arise, Adam, you who sleep, and rise up from the dead; and not, as we read, ἐπιφαύσει σοι Χριστὸς, that is, Christ will rise upon you, but ἐπιψαύσει, that is, Christ will touch you. Namely, because by the touch of his blood and of his hanging body he would be brought to life and rise up; and that then also that type was fulfilled in truth, when the dead Elisha raised a dead man (2 Kings 13). Whether these things are true or not, I leave to the reader's judgment. Certainly at that time, spoken among the people, they pleased, and were received with a kind of applause and dancing for joy. One thing I know, and I say it: this sense does not agree with the interpretation and context of this passage.

5:15

See then how you walk circumspectly: not as the unwise, but as the wise.

Rightly is it said to the Ephesians that they should walk circumspectly, since they had their senses exercised to discern good and evil, and, proving all things, they held fast what they had determined to be good. But he who sees how he walks, and how circumspectly he plants his step, lest perhaps he strike his foot against a stone, and says: Your word, O Lord, is a lamp to my feet (Ps. 118:105), is certainly wise. For I do not think that any ἰδιώτην (unlearned man), even if he wishes to walk circumspectly, can fulfil this precept, since it was commanded to the wise and not to the unwise. From this we understand that even the moral precepts (which most people think are plain, from the fact that in the eighteenth psalm (v. 9) it is said: The commandment of the Lord is bright, enlightening the eyes) need a prudent and circumspect exposition, because that bright precept enlightens the eyes of those who, having abandoned unwisdom, have given themselves over to the pursuits of wisdom.

5:16

Redeeming the time, because the days are evil.

He who is wise, and for that reason walks circumspectly, redeems the time. And he redeems the time because the days are evil. When we spend time in good work, we buy it, and make our own what the malice of men had sold. But no one who seeks the necessities of this life, and who thinks about riches and cares, which the Gospel calls thorns (Mark 4, and Luke 8), can redeem the time for himself. But by redeeming the time, which is in evil days, we in a certain way change it: we turn evil days into good, and make them belong not to the present age but to the age to come. This passage can also be explained otherwise: O you Ephesians, upon whom, as you rise up from the sleep of this age, Christ, the sun of righteousness, has risen, walk circumspectly and prudently; and, having cast off unwisdom, hold fast to wisdom, through which you may be able not to be changed along with the variety of the times, but to make the diversity of the times one time for yourselves. And because there are very many persecutions (for at the beginning of the faith the Churches were harassed almost daily), hold one course, and know that this must be kept: that you not be changed like the moon, after the manner of the fool, but that what you have once begun you hold with a firm mind; lest, namely, if you see a persecuting judge, you too be changed along with the judge's will, and again, if another arrives who praises your teaching, you then confess yourselves Christians; but rather, what you are, keep at all times. Let us set down an example from the Scriptures, so that what we say may become clearer. Joseph had one purpose, to please God. This was changed by no variety of time: not by the envy of his brothers, nor by the condition of slavery, nor by the allurements of youth, nor by the promises of his mistress, nor by the squalor of prison, nor afterward by the swelling pride of Egyptian power; but he was always one and the same, and, redeeming for himself, as we have said, the variety of the times, he turned the evil days into good. The same must be thought of Job too, who, though harassed by various trials, was not changed by riches, nor by losses, nor by bereavement, nor by wounds, nor by the reproach of his friends, nor by solitude, nor afterward by the restoration of all his goods. For he had redeemed the time for himself, and had made the evil days good.

5:17

Wherefore do not become imprudent, but understand what the will of God is.

Because the time is evil and, as we said above, must be redeemed, wisdom must first be sought, so that through it we may be able to understand what the will of God is. For we cannot walk circumspectly unless the will of God has first been understood. In every work, therefore, we must first consider what God wills; and, judgment having been made, we must afterward do what has been proved to be pleasing to him.

5:18

And do not be drunk with wine, in which is debauchery: but be filled with the spirit.

Just as we cannot serve two masters, God and mammon (Matt. 6), so we cannot be filled with the spirit and with wine alike. For he who is filled with the spirit has prudence, and gentleness, modesty, chastity. He who is filled with wine has folly, rage, insolence, lust. This, indeed, I reckon is signified in one word by debauchery (luxuria). If certain people understood this, they would never have accused me of rashness and heresy because I said, concerning the preservation of virginity, that wine must be shunned by young people, and that oil must not be poured upon the flame, nor the natural heat of the flesh increased by the kindling of pleasure. But the wine in which is debauchery can also be taken as that of which it is said in the song of Moses: Their wine is the rage of dragons, and the incurable rage of asps (Deut. 32:33), which all who are drunk with the thought of this age drink, and they rave, and vomit, and fall headlong. And, as in the fable of the Lapiths and Centaurs, they are borne to mutual destruction. Contrary to this wine is that wine which the Lord promises he will drink with us in his kingdom (Mark 14). We have often noted that the name spirit, without any addition, is used in a good sense; and this indeed seems to need observing here as well.

5:19

Speaking to yourselves in psalms, and hymns, and spiritual songs, singing and making melody in your hearts to the Lord.

He who has abstained from drunkenness with wine, in which is debauchery, and has instead been filled with the spirit, can take all things spiritually: psalms, hymns, and songs. What difference there is between a psalm (psalmus), a hymn (hymnus) and a song (canticum) we learn most fully in the Psalter. But now it must briefly be said that hymns are those which proclaim the strength and majesty of God, and always marvel at either his benefits or his deeds. This is what all the psalms contain to which ALLELUIA is either prefixed or appended. Psalms, on the other hand, properly pertain to the ethical part, so that through the instrument of the body we may know what is to be done and what is to be avoided. But he who discourses on higher things, and, as a subtle disputant, sets forth the harmony of the world and the order and concord of all creatures, he sings a spiritual song. Or certainly (so that, for the sake of the simpler, we may express what we mean more plainly) the psalm is referred to the body, the song to the mind. And so we ought to sing and to make melody and to praise the Lord with the mind rather than with the voice. For this is what is said: Singing and making melody in your hearts to the Lord. Let young men hear these things; let those hear them whose office it is to sing psalms in the church: that one must sing to God not with the voice but with the heart, and that the throat and jaws are not to be smeared with a sweet medicine after the manner of tragic actors, so that theatrical melodies and songs are heard in the church, but that one must sing in fear, in work, in the knowledge of the Scriptures. Though someone be, as those people are wont to call it, κακόφωνος (ill-voiced), if he has good works, he is a sweet singer before God. Let the servant of Christ so sing that not the voice of the singer but the words which are read may please, so that the evil spirit which was in Saul (1 Sam. 16) may be cast out of those who are likewise possessed by it, and may not be brought into those who have made the house of God a stage for the people.

5:20

Giving thanks always for all things, in the name of our Lord Jesus Christ, to God and the Father.

Something similar to this is written also in the First Epistle to the Thessalonians: Rejoice always, pray without ceasing, in all things give thanks (1 Thess. 5:16, 17). This precept can be kept only by the one who knows that by the providence of God even the five sparrows that are sold for two pennies are governed: not one of which falls into the snare without the will of the Father (Luke 12). But what he says, Giving thanks, both always and for all things, is to be viewed in two ways, so that we render thanks to God both at every time and for all things that befall us: so that not only for the things we think good, but also for those that hem us in and come against our will, the mind may break forth gladly into the praise of God, and we may say: Naked I came out of my mother's womb, naked also may I return: as it pleased the Lord, so it has been done: blessed be the name of the Lord (Job 1:21). This thanksgiving is observed among prudent men both generally and specially. Generally, so that we give thanks to God because the sun rises for us, the day runs its course, the night is changed into rest, the darkness is tempered by the brightness of the moon, and by the rising and setting of the stars the seasons change and return; because the rains serve us, the earth gives birth, the elements wait upon us; because such great varieties of animals have been given, whether for carrying, or for working, or for eating, or for covering, or for example, or for wonder; and finally, because we were born, because we subsist, because in the world, as in the house of a most powerful householder, we carry on a stewardship, and we understand that everything whatsoever that is in the world was brought forth for our sake. Specially, however, when we rejoice in the benefits of God that come to us. But this the Gentile also does, and the Jew, and the publican, and the heathen. The virtue proper to Christians is to render thanks to the Creator even in the things that are thought adverse. If the house has collapsed, if a most dearly loved wife and children have been cut off by captivity, or by poison, or by shipwreck, if we have lost our riches by proscription, if countless diseases, and the crippling of gout, always to be expected by the wretched, have broken our health. Those who seem to themselves holier are accustomed to render thanks to God because they have been freed from dangers or from miseries. But according to the Apostle this is the greatest virtue, that in the very dangers and miseries thanks be rendered to God, and that we always say: Blessed be God; I know that I endure less than I deserve: these things are small compared with my sins: nothing is paid back to me as I deserve. This is the mind of a Christian; this is the one who, taking up his cross, follows the Savior, whom neither bereavement nor losses weaken. Of him, as Flaccus says in a lyric poem: Should the shattered world fall in upon him, the ruins will strike him unafraid. But he who, as we have said, gives thanks to God and the Father, let him render them in the mediator of God and men, Christ Jesus: because except through him we are not able to come to the Father.

5:21

Being subject to one another in the fear of Christ.

Let the bishops hear this, let the presbyters hear it, let every order of teachers hear it: that they are subject to those subject to them; and let them imitate the Apostle who says: For though I was free from all, I made myself the slave of all, that I might win all (1 Cor. 9:19). And in another place: Through love serve one another (Gal. 5:13). Hence he himself also, by that same love, served all the Churches of the Gentiles. The Savior too took the form of a servant, that he might serve his disciples, and he washed their feet (John 13). This is the difference between the princes of the Gentiles and those of the Christians: that they lord it over their subjects, we serve, and we are greater in this, if we have been the least of all. But this too that he says, In the fear of Christ, is to be taken thus: that the subjection itself be done not for the glory of men, but for the fear of Christ, while we fear to offend him. Another, however, will interpret being subject to one another in the fear of Christ in this way: so as to say that this general statement is divided and distributed in what follows: Let wives be subject to their husbands; and: Children, obey your parents; and: Slaves, obey your masters according to the flesh with fear and trembling: so that not only the wife to the husband, and children to parents, and slaves to masters, but also husbands to wives, according to the duty that is commanded, and fathers to children, that they do not provoke them to anger, and masters to slaves, that they forgo threats and supply them with what they need, are to be subject to one another; and that they do this out of the fear of Christ: so that, as he was subject to his servants, so those too who seem greater may be subject to their lesser ones by rendering the duties that are commanded. Here we can also take "fear" (timor) as standing for εὐλάβεια, that is, reverence, which is nearer to love. For it by no means befits the Ephesians to do anything out of fear, and not out of love of Christ.

5:22-23

Let wives be subject to their husbands as to the Lord: because the husband is the head of the wife, as Christ also is the head of the Church.

This, which has been added in the Latin copies, let them be subject (subditæ sint), is not found in the Greek manuscripts: since it refers back to what precedes, and Being subject to one another in the fear of Christ is understood, so that by ἀπὸ κοινοῦ (in common) "subject" (subjectæ) resounds: and wives to their husbands as to the Lord. But this is understood better in the Greek than in the Latin. As, then, the Church is subject to Christ, so let the wife be subject to her husband. For the same order of rule and subjection that Christ and the Church have binds husband and wife. But we must see to it that, as in Christ and in the Church the union is holy, so also in husband and wife the bond be holy. And as not every congregation of heretics can be called the Church of Christ, nor is Christ their head, so not every marriage that is not joined to its husband according to the precepts of Christ can rightly be called wedlock, but rather adultery. Elsewhere, moreover, the wife is subject to the husband as to a lord: because her turning is toward him, and he shall rule over her (Gen. 3). For Sarah also called Abraham lord (Gen. 18). And this voluntary servitude, the more it has been subject by free will, the more it begins to be co-equal; nay rather, by its compliance, to bring the one who rules into servitude. Some interpret this passage according to anagogy in such a way as to say that the wife is taken to be the body, the husband the mind (animo). And as the Church is subject to Christ, so bodies ought to be subject to the sense (sensui), and to be brought into one spirit, if they have been joined to the Lord. For he who cleaves to the Lord is one spirit (1 Cor. 6:17).

He is the Savior of the body. Since the nature of the Church is nearer to the substance of Christ and lower than it, for this reason I think it has been named the body of Christ: of which body Christ Jesus is the Savior, that is, the Word, wisdom, and the other powers in which the Son of God is understood. Search more carefully whether anywhere in the divine volumes you can find the word "flesh" used for the Church. For here the Church is by no means called flesh, but the body of Christ. Moreover, it is plain that whatever is flesh is consequently also body; but not whatever is body is consequently also flesh.

5:24

But as67 the Church is subject to Christ: so also wives to their husbands in all things.

The Church of Christ is glorious, not having spot or wrinkle or anything of this kind. Whoever, therefore, is a sinner and stained with some filth cannot be called of the Church of Christ, nor be said to be subject to Christ. But it is possible that, as the Church, which formerly had wrinkle and spot, was afterwards restored to youth and cleanness, so the sinner too may run to the physician (because those who are well have no need of a physician (Luke 5), but those who are sick), and his wounds be healed, and he become part of the Church, which is the body of Christ. Quite elegantly, too, and cautiously, speaking to the Ephesians, he says: As the Church is subject to Christ: so also wives to their husbands. For if the wife is to be subject to the husband as the Church is to Christ, between husband and wife there will be a holy union, and they will never be slaves to the passions of the body. But if anyone should set against us what is written to the Corinthians, that the husband should render the debt to the wife, and the wife to the husband (1 Cor. 7), let him observe that there is a great distance between the Corinthians and the Ephesians. To the former it is written as to little ones and sucklings, among whom there were dissensions and schisms, and fornication was heard of such as is not even among the Gentiles: and therefore it is granted to them that after prayer they return to the same thing, lest they be tempted by Satan; although even there, in what follows, he says that he pardons them not according to his will, but according to συγκατάβασιν (condescension). But the Ephesians, among whom he spent three years and to whom he opened all the mysteries of Christ, are taught otherwise: and each one has free power of choice, to follow either the Corinthians or the Ephesians, and to be saved either by the servitude of a Corinthian or by the freedom of an Ephesian. Woe, says the Savior, to those who are pregnant, and to those who are nursing, in that day (Luke 21:23), that is, the day of judgment; and these are, of course, properly the works of marriage. Hence we must strive with all labor to emulate the Ephesians rather than the Corinthians, and not be caught, as in the flood, buying and selling, marrying and joined in matrimony; but with loins girded let us hold lamps in our hands (Matt. 24).

5:25-27

Husbands, love your wives, as Christ also loved the Church, and gave himself up for her, that he might sanctify her, cleansing68 by the washing of water in the word, that he might present to himself a glorious Church, not having spot, or wrinkle, or any such thing, but that she might be holy and without blemish.

Although husband and wife may be held by love for one another (such as that wife of Hasdrubal is recorded to have been, who, when her husband was captured, threw herself into the burning of her country, and the other women who refused to outlive their dead husbands), yet a wise man will never compare that love to the love of Christ and the Church. Here, therefore, a holy love is to be understood, the love with which Isaac too loved his wife Rebekah (Gen. 24), whose name is interpreted "patience": and from the land of Mesopotamia, which is girded on every side by the waves of this age, he brought her into the land of promise, that he might be comforted for the death of his mother. For he tempered the death of the Synagogue by union with the Church. In saying these things we give an occasion to the heretics who think that all marriages without exception are to be rejected; and they use this testimony above all. To them we must answer in brief words69: that here passions, impurity and luxury between husband and wife are forbidden by the Apostle, not a holy union; otherwise, if he was forbidding wedlock altogether, what need was there to say your wives, when he could have said: Husbands, love women, or wives? For your properly signifies marriage. And again in what follows: and husbands ought to love their wives as their own bodies: and more plainly still: He who loves his wife loves himself: For no one ever hated his own flesh; but he nourishes and cherishes it: because according to the edict of the ancient Law and the restoration of the Gospel, husband and wife become one flesh. Therefore, as we have said, the works of begetting children are granted in marriage; but the pleasures that are taken from the embraces of harlots are condemned in a wife. Reading this, let every husband and wife understand that after conception they must devote themselves to prayer rather than to conjugal union. And what in animals and beasts is prescribed by the very law of nature, that when pregnant they do not couple until the birth, let them know that in human beings this has been left to choice, so that there might be a reward from70 abstinence from pleasures. But since according to tropology we had said that husbands are souls and wives bodies, let the soul so love the flesh71, as Christ loved the Church, so as to give itself up for its salvation, and to sanctify it by the word of doctrine, so as to present it to itself not having spot or any wrinkle of oldness: especially since it knows that it is to be saved in the resurrection and to see the salvation of God. Such a husband has Christ as his head: and when, humbling himself for the salvation of the flesh, he has become one flesh with his wife, he draws her back to the spirit, and, joined to the Lord, ceases to be flesh. Beautifully, too, because he was speaking of marriage, he drew "spot or wrinkle" from the example of women to the beauty of the Church. For just as on women's bodies moles are apt to look dirty, or wrinkles to draw the skin together, or freckles to mottle it, and the whole effort of women is to wipe away whatever seems to disfigure, and to present to their husbands the loveliness of their bodies, so also souls must be purged of all the filth of sins, that the wrinkle of the old man may be smoothed out by youth, and be renewed into the new man from day to day.

5:28

So also husbands ought to love their wives as their own bodies.

And in Genesis it is written, in the person of Adam speaking: This now is bone of my bones, and flesh of my flesh (Gen. 2:23). And the same thing is confirmed in the Gospel, when the Lord later says: He who created from the beginning made them male and female, and said: For this reason a man shall leave father and mother, and shall cleave to his wife, and the two shall be in one flesh (Matt. 19:4-5). Since, therefore, husband and wife are one flesh, we must provide for our wives as for our own bodies. But no one loves his body shamefully, or loves himself for the sake of intercourse; but he cherishes and nourishes the body as the little vessel of his soul, lest, the vessel being broken, what it held should flow out and burst forth. Moreover, according to the letter too, as long as a woman is in service to childbirth and children, she has the same difference from the man as the body has from the soul. But if she wishes to serve Christ rather than the world, she will cease to be a woman and will be called a man, because we all desire to come to the perfect man. But if we refer these things also to tropology, we shall love our body and the senses of the body, which are indeed of a meaner condition than the mind, but through which the disciplines of the good arts and of the virtues pass over to the mind.

5:29

He who loves his wife loves himself: for no one ever held his own flesh in hatred, but nourishes and cherishes it, just as Christ also does the Church.

As far as the simple understanding is concerned, now that holy love between husband and wife has been enjoined, we are commanded to nourish and cherish our spouses, that is, to provide them with food and clothing and the things that are necessary. But it can be objected against us that the statement of the Apostle is not true when he says: For no one ever hates his own flesh, since those who suffer from the jaundice, and consumption, and cancer, and catarrhs prefer death to life and hate their own bodies. Let the passage, therefore, rather be referred to a figurative understanding, and let us say that the soul loves that flesh which is to see the salvation of God, and nourishes and cherishes it, instructing it with disciplines, and fattening it with heavenly bread, and watering it with the blood of Christ, so that, refreshed and sleek, it may be able to follow its husband with free course and not be weighed down by any weakness and burden72. Beautifully, too, in the likeness of Christ, who nourishes and cherishes the Church and says to Jerusalem: How often would I have gathered your children, as a hen gathers her chicks under her wings, and you would not? souls also cherish their bodies, so that this corruptible may put on incorruption and, borne up by the lightness of wings, may be raised more easily into the air. Let us therefore cherish, we husbands our wives and we souls our bodies, so that both wives are changed into men and bodies into souls. And let there be no difference of sexes at all: but just as among the angels there is not male and female, so let us too, who are to be like the angels, even now begin to be what has been promised to us in the heavenly places.

5:30

Because we are members of his body, of his flesh and of his bones.

Because we are members of the body of Christ, and Christ nourishes and cherishes the Church, therefore we too nourish and cherish our flesh, which no one ever held in hatred. Now we are members of the body of Christ not according to the nature of his eternal divinity, but according to the man whom he deigned to assume. Although the man himself who was assumed has the nature of our bodies, yet he does not have their origin. For we are formed from human seed; he was born of the Holy Spirit. But it can also be said in another way: since the body of Christ is the Church, and the Church is gathered together from all believers, Paul and the Ephesians are members of the body, that is, of the Church of Christ.

5:31

For this a man shall leave father and mother, and the two shall be in one flesh.

What we have frequently noted, that the apostles and evangelists did not use the same words as the passages of the Old Testament as they are contained in their own volumes, we prove here too: for this testimony is written thus in Genesis: For this a man shall leave his father and his mother, and shall cleave to his wife, and the two shall be in one flesh (Gen. 2:24). But now, instead of what is found there, ἕνεκεν τούτου, that is, for this, the Apostle has put ἀντὶ τούτου, which cannot be said in Latin in other words; then, instead of his father and his mother, he removed the pronouns and put only father and mother; and what is said in the middle, and shall cleave to his wife, he here omitted entirely, and joined to the preceding words only what followed this saying, and put: and the two shall be in one flesh. Now we have observed all this at present for this reason: so that in other places too, wherever testimonies are used by the apostles as if from the prophets and from the Old Testament and are not found in our manuscripts, we may by no means run at once to the absurdities and ravings of the apocrypha, but may know that these things are indeed written in the Old Testament, but were not set forth in that form by the apostles, and that it was rather the sense that was used; and that where they are written cannot easily be found except by the studious. So, to exhort husband and wife to mutual affection, he took the example of Adam and Eve: that just as a rib is taken from Adam and built into a wife, and the wife herself in turn is brought back into the one flesh of the man, because he who loves his wife loves himself, so we too should love our wives. And this very thing he interprets by allegory of Christ and of the Church, so that Adam prefigured Christ, and Eve the Church. For the last Adam was made into a life-giving spirit (1 Cor. 15). And just as the whole race of men is born from Adam and his wife, so from Christ and the Church the whole multitude of believers has been begotten. And it, made into the one body of the Church, is placed again in the side of Christ, and fills the place of the rib, and is made the one body of the man, the Lord himself asking for this in the Gospel: Father, grant that, as I and you are one, so they too may be one in us (John 17:21). Let us ask Marcion by what consistency he can interpret this passage, which is taken from the Old Instrument, of Christ and of the Church, when according to him the Old Scripture does not pertain to Christ at all.

5:32

This mystery is great, but I say in Christ and in the Church.

It is not, as most people think, that the whole history written in Genesis about Adam and Eve can easily be referred to Christ and to the Church, but only what is set down in the present passage, that is: For this a man shall leave his father and his mother, and shall cleave to his wife, and the two shall be in one flesh. For Adam, the first man and the first prophet, prophesied this of Christ and the Church: that our Lord and Savior would leave his Father, God, and his mother, the heavenly Jerusalem, and would come to earth for the sake of his body, the Church, and would fashion her from his own side, and for her sake the Word would become flesh. And because not all mysteries are equal, but there is one mystery greater (sacramentum majus) and another lesser (minus), therefore he now says: This mystery is great; and at the same time it is a sign of his humility that he adds: But I say in Christ and in the Church. Gregory of Nazianzus, a very eloquent man and exceedingly learned in the Scriptures, when he was discussing this passage with me, used to say: See how great the mystery of this chapter is, that the Apostle, interpreting it of Christ and of the Church, does not claim to have expressed it as the dignity of the testimony demanded, but in a way said: I know that this passage is full of ineffable mysteries and requires the divine heart of an interpreter. But I, according to the smallness of my understanding, think that meanwhile it is to be understood of Christ and of the Church: not that anything is greater than Christ and the Church, but because it is difficult to be able to interpret all that is said about Adam and Eve of Christ and of the Church.

5:33

Nevertheless let you also, each one individually, love his own wife as himself.

Someone might suppose that the Apostle enjoins the same love between husband and wife as is commanded toward the neighbor: for so it is written: You shall love your neighbor as yourself (Lev. 19:18), and now it is said: Let each one love his own wife as himself. Therefore the love toward the neighbor and toward the wife will be the same. But if, according to the Savior's interpretation, every man is a neighbor to every man, then there will be no difference of love between a wife and the love of any men whatever, which is very absurd to say. For in the case of the neighbor a likeness is set down, that you should love him just as yourself and desire him to be saved. But in the case of the wife the adverb of comparison, as (sicut), sounds, with a certain weight, not likeness but approval and confirmation. As we say of a man: he acted as a man (quasi vir); and of the Savior it is written: We saw his glory, glory as (quasi) of the only-begotten (John 1:14). Not that the Savior himself had glory by comparison with another only-begotten: for he himself is the Only-begotten. And if there were also another only-begotten, he could not be called only-begotten. Hence he had no need of the example of another only-begotten, but he possessed glory as the Only-begotten, that is, as it befitted him himself to have it. The opening of the seventy-second psalm, though according to the Greeks, sounds this same thing: ὥς ἀγαθὸς ὁ Θεὸς τῷ Ἰσραὴλ τοῖς εὐθέσι τῇ καρδίᾳ, which has been translated by our people: How good is the God of Israel to the upright of heart (Ps. 72:1)! Otherwise, according to the Greeks, ὥς, that is, as (sicut), seems to signify a likeness rather than the firmness of what is said, if you hear it not as a confirmation but as73 an example. At the same time this too must be noted, that the husband is commanded to love his wife, but the woman to fear her husband. For love befits a husband, fear a woman; but to a slave not only dread but also trembling is joined. Hence in what follows he also says: Slaves, obey carnal masters with fear and trembling.

And let the woman fear her husband. If dread toward God, because of fear of punishment, does not allow him who dreads to be perfect, how much more imperfect will a woman be who dreads not only God but also her husband? For this reason it must be asked whether the wife, and the wife's fear, are to be understood carnally, since wives are frequently found to be much better than their husbands, and to command them, and to govern the house, and to bring up the children, and to keep the household in discipline, while the husbands live in luxury and run after harlots. Whether these women ought to rule their husbands or to fear them, I leave to the reader's judgment. But if, according to allegory, as we said above, the wife is taken as the body and the husband as the mind, there is nothing unfitting in her fearing her husband as a handmaid, set in the second rank and in a baser substance. For, as Crispus (Sallust) says, we use rather the command of the mind and the service of the body. But he who follows the simple understanding of the woman and the husband will show that there are two meanings in the word "fear." And he will say: one is that of which John says: He who fears has punishment, and he who fears is not perfect (1 John 4:18). According to it, slaves too have the spirit of slavery in fear, which is required of them by the Lord, who says to them: And if I am a father, where is my glory? And if I am a lord, where is my fear (Mal. 1:6)? The other is that which among the philosophers is named εὐλάβεια, and among us, though it does not render it fully, can be called reverence (reverentia). The prophet too knows a fear of the perfect, and whoever fears with this fear is perfect, when he says in the thirty-third psalm: There is no want to those who fear him (Ps. 33:9). So here dread can be enjoined on the wife, simply understood, that she fear, that is, revere her husband.

6:1-3

Children, obey your parents in the Lord: for this is right. Honor your father and your mother (which is the first commandment in promise), that it may be well with you, and that you may be long-lived upon the land.

It is said ambiguously: whether children ought to obey those who are their parents in the Lord, or rather whether children should obey their parents in the Lord. Both are to be done: that we both obey those parents who begot us in the Lord, such as Paul and the apostles were, and do whatever they have commanded, and also comply in the Lord with our parents from whom we were born according to the flesh, fulfilling those things that are not contrary to the will of the Lord. And at the same time we shall also press hard the heretics, who will not have it that the Old Testament belongs to the good God whose Son Christ is: by what reasoning does the Apostle of Christ, the Son of the good God, use the Scripture of the Creator, and take for granted the obedience of children from the old Law? This testimony, taken from Exodus, is worded thus there: Honor your father and your mother, that it may be well with you, and that you may be long-lived upon the land which the Lord your God will give you (Exod. 20:12); from which he has now removed the last words; and this commandment is the fifth in the Decalogue. Hence it must be asked why he has now said, which is the first commandment, when the first commandment is: You shall have no other gods besides me. For this reason some read it thus: which is the first commandment in promise, as if the four other commandments, which were spoken before, had no promises, and in this one alone a pledge were set down attached to it: that it may be well with you, and that you may be long-lived upon the land which the Lord your God will give you. But they seem to me not to have observed carefully enough that in the second commandment too a promise is joined. For he says: You shall not make for yourself an idol, nor any likeness of the things that are in heaven above, and that are on the earth beneath, and that are in the waters under the earth: you shall not worship them, and you shall not sacrifice to them: for I am the Lord your God, a jealous God, who repay the sins of the fathers upon the children to the third and fourth generation, to those who hate me, and I show mercy to thousands, to those who love me and keep my commandments (Exod. 20:5-6). For observe that these are the words of a pledge: Showing mercy to thousands, to those who love me and keep my commandments. Perhaps, then, because the Decalogue was given as the first Law to the people going out of Egypt, each commandment of the Decalogue is to be called the first commandment, by comparison with those precepts which were afterwards written down in the Law. But he who will try to hold the former exposition, in that he had divided the words, which is the first commandment in promise, will say that the commandment stands separately: Honor your father and your mother; and that afterwards the promise is set in its own place: that you may be long-lived upon the land which the Lord your God will give you. But in this commandment, that is: You shall not make for yourself an idol, nor any likeness, he will say that not separately, but in one context and discourse, it is not so much that a promise was given as that the sentence ended in the praises of God, who shows mercy to thousands, to those who love him and keep his commandments. Again, he who contends that all the commandments of the Decalogue are the first commandment will show that the promise in which it is written: that you may be long-lived upon the land which the Lord your God will give you, pertains not only to those who obey their parents, but also to countless other precepts; and he will have to unroll all the commandments in which this reward and prize is promised, that they may be long-lived upon the land which the Lord their God has given them. From him the interpreter who differs will have to demand that he show that those things which he has brought forward were written as commandments before this commandment. And if he cannot prove this, he will mention in vain that this promise is attached in other precepts too. After this it must be considered again that for honoring father and mother the promise is not a Jewish and carnal one, that the children should be long-lived upon the land which the Lord their God has given them. For we must believe that there have been many who, though obedient to their parents, died quickly, and who, though impious toward their parents, came to extreme old age. For let the Jews, and those like the Jews, answer: if the length of this life is among the things promised, and to remain long in the body is happiness, what does that saying in the Psalms mean: Woe is me, that my sojourning has been prolonged! I have dwelt with the dwellers of Kedar (Ps. 119:5)? And this of Solomon in Ecclesiastes: I praised all the dead who died long ago, above the living who live up to the present; and better than these two is he who is not yet born, and has not seen all the evil work that has been done under the sun (Eccles. 4:2, 3); and a little after: If a man begets a hundred, and lives many years, and many are the days of his years, and his soul is filled with good things, and he has no burial, I said: better than he is the abortive, because it came in vanity, and its name will be covered in darkness; and indeed it has not seen the sun (Eccles. 6:3-5), and the rest. For if the dead are praised above the living (and according to certain people who think that souls dwell in the heavenly places before they descend into these bodies, he who is not yet born is said to be better than both, and this whole life is a temptation; and according to Job: The death of a man74 is rest (Job 3:23); and according to the same man and Jeremiah, Cursed is the day on which we are born (Jer. 20:14)), how is it now promised to those who honor father and mother that they shall be long-lived upon the land which the Lord their God has given them? The land, therefore, must be sought which the Lord promises and grants to those who have left the spiritual Egypt, and with all patience have crossed the great and terrible deserts of this life, and have conquered great kings, whom the Lord strikes; and have crossed over into Judea, which flows with milk and honey, and under the leadership of Jesus, with Jericho falling and Ai laid waste, which is interpreted "precipice," have come to Jerusalem, and a temple is built for them under Solomon, the peaceful king (Josh. 6; 8), and they possess the land which has been prepared for the gentle: for Blessed indeed are the meek, since they shall possess the land (Matt. 5:4), which is truly the land of the living, as the Psalmist also says: I believe that I shall see the good things of the Lord in the region of the living (Ps. 26:13). The length of this life wisdom too holds in her right hand, holding in her left riches and glory.

6:4

And fathers, do not provoke your children to anger, but bring them up in the discipline and conduct of the Lord.

The sin of children is not to obey their parents; and because parents could command something perverse, he added, in the Lord. But the sin of parents is to provoke their little children and infants to anger, or at any rate, when they are already adolescents and of a more mature age, to command them things that are burdensome. Just as, therefore, in the children obedience and the reward of subjection have been set forth, so to the parents a moderate authority is enjoined, that they may know they are set over them not as over slaves but as over sons. Nor is he content with this end of the precept, but he added this as well: Bring them up in the discipline and correction of the Lord. What we read as correction (correptio) is better expressed in the Greek as νουθεσία, which sounds more like admonition (admonitio) and instruction than severity. Let the bishops and presbyters read this who instruct their sons in secular literature and make them read comedies and sing the shameful writings of the mimes, sons educated perhaps at the Church's expense; and what a virgin or a widow, or some poor man pouring out his whole substance, had offered into the corban for sin, this the Grammarian and the Orator turns into a New Year's gift for the Kalends, a Saturnalian dole and a Minerval fee, or into household expenses, or into temple offerings, or into filthy harlots. Eli the priest was himself holy, but because he did not instruct his sons in all discipline and correction, he fell backward and died (1 Sam. 2). For he could not stretch forward to the things that are before, but rushed backward, and, falling on his back in an incurable opisthotonos, he looked back toward Sodom with Lot's wife. And yet he had indeed rebuked his sons, saying: Why do you do these evil things which I hear about you from all the people? Do not, my sons, for it is not a good report which I hear about you (1 Sam. 2:23, 24). Of fathers of this kind Isaiah too complains with a tearful voice, saying: And sons of foreigners have been born to them (Isa. 2). But if he commanded this to the Ephesian laymen, most of whom, as usually happens among the people, were occupied with the business of this life, that they should bring up their sons in all discipline and admonition of the Lord, what must we think of the priests, about whose order he writes to Timothy, saying: Having his children in submission, with all propriety (1 Tim. 3:4)? And he presses and repeats the same thing to Titus: Having faithful children, not under accusation of riotous living, or unsubmissive (Titus 1:6, 7); and, as though the vices of the children were imputed to the parents, inserting a causal conjunction, he says: For a bishop must be without crime, as the steward of God (Titus 1:7). That bishop, therefore, is not without crime whose son has not been submissive and is under accusation of riotous living.

6:5-8

Slaves, obey your fleshly masters with fear and trembling, and in the simplicity of your heart, as to Christ; not serving to the eye, as pleasing all, but as slaves of Christ, doing the will of God from the heart; serving with faithfulness as to the Lord, and not to men; knowing that whatever good each one has done, this he will receive from the Lord, whether slave or free.

When the Prophet says to Jerusalem: Who are you, that you were afraid of mortal man, and of the son of man (Isa. 51)? and Peter in his Epistle: Do not fear their fear, but sanctify the Lord Jesus Christ in your hearts (1 Pet. 3:14, 15); and the Savior is in harmony with the same: Do not fear those who can kill the body and have nothing more that they can do to you, but fear him who can kill both soul and body into Gehenna (Matt. 10:28); and Solomon too bears like witness: Son, honor the Lord, and you will be strong; but besides him fear no other (Prov. 7): the Apostle seems to command different things, that slaves should obey their fleshly masters with fear and trembling, and that the wife75 should fear her husband. And one who answers more simply will say this: these precepts were laid down not for perfect slaves and for those who had come to know the secrets of wisdom, but for those who had the beginnings of faith and needed humbler teachings. Another, however, would assert that he who does not have the spirit of slavery again in fear is by no means subject to this rule, that he should obey his fleshly masters with fear and trembling; and he would say the same of the wife too, who is commanded to fear her husband. A third, moreover, will think that in this passage too, as in the case of the woman, fear is said for reverence; but he will be hard pressed by the fact that trembling has been added to fear. For reverence can befit a wife, so that she fears her husband reverently. But where there is trembling, dread will not signify reverence but fear. Necessarily, then, it was added in the case of slaves that after the fear of the Lord they should have trembling too; and to distinguish him from the spiritual Lord, the master is now called fleshly: so that for a slave, whoever has believed in God and has not yet attained the summit of knowledge, it is not unbecoming to serve a fleshly master with fear and trembling, in the simplicity of his heart, and to serve him faithfully as he would Christ. Not serving to the eye, as those do who desire to please men, but so as to turn necessity into will, and to make of slavery a reward; above all when the master of the flesh does not command things different from the Lord of the spirit. For this is what he says: Not serving to the eye, as pleasing men, but as slaves of Christ, doing the will of God. But let the slave have that very faithfulness not forced but spontaneous and from the heart, serving his master as he would Christ, from whom he will receive the reward of faithful slavery, no less than if he had served as a free man of his own will. At the same time this too should be noted, that he has attached different things to the obedience of children and of slaves. For to the children he says: Obey your parents in the Lord; but to the slaves: Obey your fleshly masters with fear and trembling. So that, just as fear makes a difference between the slave and the wife, so obedience too should differ between children and slaves. And beautifully, when commanding slaves to obey their masters, he added, as to Christ; and again, as slaves of Christ, doing the will of God: namely, so that the slave should not listen to his fleshly master if he wishes to command things contrary to the precepts of God. And just as he had written back to the Corinthians in keeping with the time (1 Cor. 7), so that divorces should not occur between husbands and wives on the occasion of faith in Christ, if one of the two had wished to believe; so to the Ephesians and to the Colossians, because very many at the beginnings of the faith thought that Gentile masters were to be despised, he now lays down precepts for these conditions with moderation: so that he does not seem to stir up the slaves against their masters, and on the other hand by no means teaches that masters are to be listened to if they command vicious and abominable things.

6:9

And masters, do the same things to them, forbearing threats, knowing that both their Lord and yours is in heaven, and there is no respect of persons with him.

What are these things which he commanded the slaves above, that he should say that masters must do the same things as slaves? I think they are those which he had said: in simplicity of heart, and doing the will of God, and from the heart, and with faithfulness, or goodwill toward the slaves, since εὔνοια can signify either. For whatever good each one has done, this he will receive from the Lord, whether he has served as a servant, as has been said, or has been a master as a free man, as he ought: so that he is not terrifying, not quick to beatings, knowing that he himself also has a Lord in heaven, with whom there is no respect of persons, and who alone judges wills, and according to them prefers the better to the worse, choosing deeds, not men.

6:10

For the rest, be strengthened in the Lord, and in the might of his power.

I know that in the Greek, in place of power (virtus), strength (fortitudo) has been set, that is, ἰσχὺν, because among them virtue (virtus) is called ἀρετὴ. But among us this is the custom of the Scriptures, that they name both ἰσχὺν and ἀρετὴν indifferently virtus: especially since the strength of the body is reckoned a virtue of the mind. And yet among the philosophers too fortitude is set among the four virtues, and it is certainly plain that it belongs to the mind and not to the body. What he says, then: Be strengthened in the Lord, and in the might of his power, is understood wholly of Christ, so that those who have believed may be strengthened in all the powers that are understood concerning him. And prudently, after the special commands about what is to be observed by husbands and wives, fathers and children, masters and slaves, he now gives a general command to all in common, that, strengthened in the Lord and in his might, they should prepare themselves against the devil, of whom he writes in what follows.

6:11

Put on the whole armour of God, that you may be able to stand against the wiles of the devil.

From what we read below, and from what is said in all the Scriptures about the Lord Savior, it is most clearly proved that by the whole armour of God which we are now commanded76 to put on, the Savior is understood; so that it is one and the same to have said, Put on the whole armour of God, as if he had said, Put on the Lord Jesus Christ. For if the belt is truth, and the breastplate is righteousness, and the Savior is named both truth and righteousness, no one doubts that he himself is both the belt and the breastplate. Accordingly, he himself will also be the preparation of the Gospel of peace, and the shield of faith, and the helmet of salvation, and the sword of the spirit, which is the word of God, the living and effective word, sharper than every sword sharpened on both sides. But what other armour of God can we suppose, with which he must be clothed who has to fight against the wiles of the devil, except the power which is Christ? For whoever has put him on according to all the things that are understood concerning him will be able to fight against all the snares of the devil; and in so far as he is girded about with truth, he will not easily be led away into the dogmas of falsehood. And in so far as he has put on the breastplate of righteousness, he cannot be pierced by the darts of iniquity. Moreover, when he has been shod with most beautiful shoes in the preparation of the Gospel of peace, as one who has prepared his works at the end, and has therefore become a man of peace, he does nothing warlike or tumultuous, nor will he be condemned with those who are unprepared. Unbelief too, which is the enemy of faith, will avail nothing where the shield of faith is. The head also, and the ruling part of the heart and soul, in which all the senses are located, encircled with the helmet of salvation, will not be shaken. Finally, as a warrior and a brave man, he will cut down, kill and slaughter all the sects contrary to the truth, holding in his hand the sword of the spirit, that is, the word of God. Wishing, then, to show the manifold devices of the devil by which he strives77 to take us, through those places in which we do not keep our heart with all watchfulness, the apostle named them his μεθοδείας, that is, inventions or wiles. For if we abstain from the pleasure of bodies, he catches us, being insufficiently careful, in covetousness. And if we despise covetousness together with pleasure, he creeps in through luxury, and makes our belly our god, and through it he storms even those things which seemed strong. And just as wise commanders of armies are wont to attack above all those parts of cities which are poorly fortified, so that, once they have broken in through them, the fortified parts may easily be taken; so the devil too seeks to break in through the places he sees lying open, or at any rate not firmly shut, and to reach the very citadel of the heart and soul. And what need is there for me to speak of his many snares, when from these the other kinds of his wiles may be known? Now diabolus is a Greek name, which is interpreted "accuser". But according to the idiom of the Hebrew language, since the tribe of Zabulon too has some likeness to this word, he can be called καταρύων, that is, "flowing down", because, namely, he flowed down little by little from virtue to vice, and fell from heavenly things to earthly.

6:12

For we have no wrestling against blood and flesh, but against principalities, against powers, against the world-rulers of this darkness, against the spiritual forces of wickedness in the heavenly places.

I do not think that Paul, writing to the Corinthians, could have said, You have no wrestling against flesh and blood, since to them he says: Let no temptation take hold of you but what is human. But God is faithful, who will not permit78 you to be tempted beyond what you are able (1 Cor. 10:13). For I consider that the contests against flesh and blood are those which are there called human temptations, when the flesh lusts against the spirit and provokes us to do its works, fornication, impurity, luxury, idolatry, enmities, contentions, emulations, angers, quarrels, dissensions, heresies, plots, drunkenness, carousings (Gal. 5), and other things like these. But it is not a human temptation, nor a fight against flesh and blood, when either Satan himself, transformed into an Angel of light, strives to persuade us to think him an Angel of light, or does something like these things, in all power, with lying signs and portents, in all deception of iniquity. For when the enemy has ensnared someone so that the man receives him and speaks in him, Thus says the Lord, it is not as flesh and blood that he deceives him, nor as a human temptation, but as principality and power, ruler of darkness and spiritual wickedness (Eph. 4). Therefore let us not give place to the devil; but even if the spirit of one who has power should rise up against us, according to what is written, let us give him no place (Eccles. 10:4). Someone may say that what he says, We have no wrestling against blood and flesh, but against principalities and powers, and the rest that follows, is said so that we may be taught that not even those vices which we think are supplied to us by the flesh belong to the flesh or the blood, but that they are suggested by certain spiritual forces of wickedness. For there are certain demons who serve loves and love songs, as the Prophet too records, saying: They have been led astray by a spirit of fornication (Hos. 4:12). For they are also said to have certain barbarous names, as those whom the common people truly call sorcerers have often confessed, and incantations, and prayers, and various colors, and different kinds either of metals or of foods, at which the demons, when invoked, are said to be present and to capture unhappy souls. Others, again, are said to stir up angers and rages and wars; others to preside over enmities and to incite hatreds among men. Because, then, they say, the Apostle wishes to teach us that these kinds of vices are not produced from the nature of the body and from the matter of flesh and blood, but at the prompting of demons, therefore he says: We have no wrestling against blood and flesh, but against principalities and powers, and the rest. Now we have brought forward into the open the examples of those unhappy men who serve the magic arts and are said to do these things, for this reason: to blunt the opinion of those who think that all vices belong to flesh and blood, and that the demons have no power to incite us to sin. We think that Jacob's wrestling too was of this kind, namely that he did not contend against flesh and blood (Gen. 32), when he remained alone, and a man wrestled with him, helping him and strengthening him against another, who was fighting with excessive sweat. And at the same time see whether those may not perhaps be ridiculous who suppose that Jacob wrestled the whole night in the manner of wrestlers. For what great thing is it if, as they say, in wrestling he either won or was beaten? But according to a struggle reasonable and fitting for the patriarch, we must believe that his wrestling was such as those have who could say: We have no wrestling against flesh and blood. There is no need now to shake out the whole mystery of that passage, since it must be discussed more fully in its own place, if I live. But we ask (see the Hebrew Questions on Genesis) where in the Old Testament Paul read these things, or by what authority he published things that are not written at all. And we conjecture that from the things written about battles and single combat (for example, David against Goliath, and the sons of Israel against foreigners and the other nations) in the Law, and in Joshua, and in the book of Judges, and of Kingdoms, and of Paralipomenon, the Apostle understood more deeply (1 Sam. 17); and that from the fleshly war, in which the combatants were defeated or prevailed, he perceived a spiritual war; and that the satraps of each place were indications79 of the powers above, while the kings of the individual nations were images of those who are now called rulers of the world and of darkness, and the wicked men showed forth the spiritual forces of wickedness in the heavenly places. And the Apostle seems to us to be saying this in other words: O Ephesians, what you read about the battles of Israel against the nations seems indeed to speak of flesh or blood, for example, of the Egyptians, the Edomites, the Ammonites, the Moabites and the other nations; but if you truly wish to know, recognize that all those things happened to them figuratively (1 Cor. 10). And they were written for our sake, upon whom the ends of the ages have run down, so that we may understand from them that our fight is not against flesh and blood, but against certain spiritual and invisible powers, against the rulers of that darkness which broods over this world and puts in men's way the error of unbelief, and against the spiritual forces of wickedness which dwell in the heavenly places: not that demons dwell in the heavenly places, but because the air above us has received this name. Hence the birds too which fly through the air are said to be the birds of heaven. For in another place too the Apostle says of the demons that they roam in this air: In which you once walked according to the age of this world, according to the prince of the power of the air, the spirit that now works in the sons of unbelief (Eph. 2:2). Now this is the opinion of all the teachers, that this air, which divides heaven and earth in the middle and is called the void, is full of opposing forces. After this we must consider from whom the principalities, and powers, and rulers of the darkness of the world, and spiritual forces of wickedness in the heavenly places received the power to be what they are. And indeed another may say that the apostate devil entrusted different offices to his attendants, and that God is not the author of these distributions. To him the careful reader can object this: and how is it written, For there is no power except from God (Rom. 13:1)? But if this is said of men, how much more of those who are of a subtler and better nature? Nor would one who holds this at once incur the charge of blasphemy, since each has been allotted different ministries according to his own will. For just as in cities we see those who have committed some crime either feeding wild beasts, or cutting marble, or cleaning out the filth of the sewers, or put in charge of gladiators and assigned to the shedding of the blood of the condemned; so the demons too, from the freedom of their own choice, have been allotted the province of snares, frauds, crimes and perjury, and the rest of the vices, so that they are rulers of darkness, because they did not wish to be princes of light. These rulers, then, of the world and of darkness, when, wrestling against someone, they have tripped him up and made him fall, at once bind him to their own world and to their own darkness, over which they rule. For this reason we must labor the more strongly, that we who have once heard: I have chosen you out of this world; you are no longer of this world; for if you were of the world, the world would love what was its own (John 15:19), may not return to the world nor be subjected to it; but let the world be crucified to us, and we to it, so that Jesus, the ruler of light, may join us to his own world and bring us under the Father's dominion, rescued from the power of spiritual wickedness and from their heaven, which is to pass away and cannot be called the seat of God. For it is impious that the spiritual forces of wickedness in the heavenly places should be believed to hold that heaven of which God speaks: Heaven is my throne (Isa. 66:1). Therefore he who understands how many things happen in this world, both when we are present and when we are absent, while we cannot see them, whether because of this frail and earthly body of our humility in which the soul is wrapped, or because a subtler nature is not laid open to fleshly eyes: he will see what the darkness is of which the Apostle now speaks: namely, that either this whole earthly life is called darkness (for the light shines in the darkness, and the darkness did not comprehend it (John 1:5), or that the earthly body, the body of death and of humility, overshadows, covers and blinds the light and the sense of the soul. But let us know that, except for the present passage, we have never read κοσμοκράτορας, that is, world-rulers, in either the Old or the New Testament: a name which the apostle Paul coined for this reason, because, in arguing with the Ephesians, he had to fit new names to new and invisible things. Further, on account of his saying in the heavenly places, since it seems ambiguous, it must be said that it can be understood with all of them, so that the sense is: We have no wrestling against powers in the heavenly places, and against rulers of this darkness in the heavenly places, and against spiritual forces of wickedness in the heavenly places; and not only against the spiritual forces of wickedness in the heavenly places. Especially if we understand (as has already been explained above) how the heavenly places are so named on account of the birds of heaven, and how it is said in common usage that the rain comes from heaven: not that the rain comes out of heaven. For the philosophers say that the clouds, from which the rains pour down and the shower sprinkles, are no more than two thousand paces distant from the earth. In this sense too the floodgates of heaven are said to have been opened in the flood. Yet the worse a person is, the nearer he will be to the places of the earth and to a denser substance. For both the earth and the air that surrounds us have their own density. Some say that souls too, set free from bodies, if they have been refined in the present life and worn down into a subtle body by the file, so to speak, of ἀσκήσεως (ascetic training) and of the virtues, will not dwell in the denser places, but will be near to God, who is incorporeal. But if they are such that it could be said of them: Sons of men, how long heavy, or fat of heart (Ps. 4:3), they are dragged down to the lowest places according to the weight of their grossness, and are weighed down by their density. After this, that question too must equally be discussed, whether there is some place denser than this earth and air, which most call the underworld (Infernus), in which those who are called the beings below (inferi) dwell. But who these are, and what kinship or difference they have with those who are called the spiritual forces of wickedness in the heavenly places, is not a matter to discuss at the present time. It remains that we learn more clearly, from two battle lines in the Apostle, what the difference is between the wrestling of flesh and blood and the rulers of this darkness and the spiritual forces of wickedness in the heavenly places. For when he wishes to point out human temptations, that is, the wrestling of flesh and blood, he speaks disdaining and despising them: Who will separate us from the love of God: tribulation, or distress, or persecution, or famine and nakedness, and peril, and sword? As it is written (Ps. 43:22): For your sake we are put to death all the day long, we are accounted as sheep for slaughter. But in all these things we overcome, because of him who loved us (Rom. 8:35, 36). But when he wishes to teach us about the adverse powers, and the rulers of darkness, and the spiritual forces of wickedness in the heavenly places, he makes a beginning as if from another head, and says: For I am sure that neither death, nor life, nor angels, nor principalities, nor things present, nor things to come, nor strength, nor height, nor depth, nor any other creature will be able to separate us from the love of God, which is in Christ Jesus our Lord. This chapter has perhaps been discussed at greater length than the reader would have wished; but I beg him to pardon the difficulty of the passage itself and the character of the Ephesians, who after their magic arts ought to have known by whom they had once been deceived.

6:13

Therefore take up the whole armour of God, that you may be able to resist in the evil day, and, having wrought everything, to stand.

By the evil day he points either to the present time, of which he had spoken above: Redeeming the time, because the days are evil, on account of the distress and the labours of this life, since not without sweat and struggle do we arrive at the palm; or else certainly to the day of the consummation and of judgment, when the devil, enemy and avenger, will desire to keep us in his own portion, from which he will be freed who understands concerning the needy and the poor: For in the evil day the Lord will deliver him (Ps. 40:1). This is the day of which it is also written in another place: Behold, it comes, the day of the wrath of the Lord (Isa. 13:9); and elsewhere: For the day of the Lord will come incurable, a day of fury and of wrath (Isa. 22:6); and again: Woe to those who desire the day of the Lord: why would you have the day of the Lord? And this is darkness, and not light. As if a man should flee from the face of a lion, and run into a bear; and go into his house, and lean his hands upon the wall, and a snake bite him. Is not the day of the Lord darkness, not light, and gloom that has no brightness (Amos 5:18)? For how is this day not evil, which is wrapped in darkness and gloom? Of it the prophet Joel also makes mention, saying: Sound the trumpet in Zion; proclaim on my holy mountain; and let all who inhabit the earth be confounded; for the day of the Lord is at hand; for near is the day of darkness and of whirlwind, the day of cloud and of gloom (Joel 2:1). And Zephaniah speaks of the same day, saying: The great day of the Lord is near, near and exceedingly swift. The voice of the day of the Lord is bitter, and harsh, and strong: a day of wrath is that day, a day of distress and of necessity (Zeph. 1:14 ff.), and the rest. After which he adds: And I will afflict men, and they shall walk like the blind, because they have sinned against the Lord (Zeph. 1:17). So then, that one may be able to resist the devil on this day, since he is the accuser of our brethren, let him take up the whole armour of God (for this is what πανοπλία means, not simply armour, as it has been translated in the Latin), and, girded with all the weapons and arms which are set out in what follows, let him know that he can stand then, if he has wrought everything, so that, full of all the virtues, he may plant a firm step and not be moved from the battle line, and may be one of those of whom the Lord says: There are some of those standing here (Matt. 16:28); and in another place: For by faith you stand (2 Cor. 1:24); and the Psalmist: He has set, he says, my feet upon a rock (Ps. 39:3). A third interpretation, too, is brought in by some, who say that not every battle against the devil is ended by death, but that when we have departed from this age, then the struggle will be stronger and more open for us, we present against them present; and in this way they will expound what we set down a little before, neither things present, nor things to come, saying that the things to come are these struggles which are to be entered upon after this life. And also this: Wherefore we strive, whether in the present or in the future, to please him (2 Cor. 5:9), so that they think the present time signifies this life, and the future, the time after death. This also which is now said: And having wrought all things, to stand, they will refer to the same sense: as though no one could work all things in the present life, but does something in part, just as he sees in part and prophesies in part; and he may then be able to stand perfectly, when he has wrought everything. But another expounds these words more simply, saying that the Ephesians are being exhorted and admonished, in view of the future temptations and persecutions which the apostle Paul saw by a prophetic spirit would come upon them after this epistle, to do all things through which they may be able to stand in the faith of the Gospel and not fall in persecution. The evil day, however, we judge to have been taken here properly from the forty-eighth psalm.

6:14

Stand therefore, girded about your loins with truth.

That, corresponding to the members of the flesh and of the body, all the members of the soul are also named in the Scriptures, no one doubts; and of these I think one member is now meant, the loins, which we are commanded to gird with truth. It is written also in the Gospel κατὰ Λουκᾶν (according to Luke): Let your loins be girded, and burning lamps in your hands (Luke 12:35). Since, therefore, the loins are always understood of generation and seed, according to that text: Of the fruit of your loin I will set upon your throne (Ps. 131:11); and elsewhere: Levi was still in the loins of his father Abraham when Melchizedek met him (Heb. 7:10), he seems to us to have girded his loins who in no way renders the debt to a wife, nor serves lust, but imitates the unbegotten God, not ministering to the business of generation. This same thing, I think, is signified also by the fact that John had a leather girdle about his loins (Matt. 3), and was not one of the unclean, who, cast out of the camp because of a flux of seed, cannot dwell with the ark of the Lord (Num. 5:2); nor one of those of whom it is written in Numbers: Let his garments be torn apart (Lev. 13:45). But he who is girded with Christ, the truth, gathers these garments up high and draws them upward, and with a spiritual belt veils, binds and encloses the shamefulness of the naked sides, and is ready for battle, and has shining works, which are called burning lamps.

And having put on the breastplate of righteousness. Just as he is wounded only with difficulty, above all in those places which hold life, who has put on the breastplate of virtue, close-set with hooks and with iron rings holding one another, so he who is surrounded with the manifold garment of righteousness will neither, like a stag, take an arrow in the liver, nor fall into desires and rages, but will be pure in heart, having as the maker of this breastplate God, who fashions all the arms for each of the saints, and does not allow him to be struck and burned alike by the dart that flies by day and by the burning arrows.

6:15

And your feet shod in the preparation of the Gospel of peace.

Observe more carefully that he has called a certain virtue of the soul feet, with which we walk in him who says: I am the way (John 14:6), and which we ought to shoe with the preparation of the Gospel of peace. As a figure of these shoes, those shoes in Exodus also went before, which those eating the Passover, and those who are ready to make a journey, are commanded to have. Thus, he says, shall you eat it: your loins girded, and your shoes on your feet, and your staffs in your hands; and you shall eat it in haste: for it is the Passover of the Lord (Exod. 12:11). For to eat in haste and with shod feet is a sign of preparation, so that, strengthened by the paschal food, they may be able to cross the wide and terrible desert. Whoever, therefore, is still walking, let him be shod; but whoever, the Jordan already crossed, has entered the land of promise, let him bare his foot. Loose, he says, the shoe from your feet: for the place on which you stand is holy ground (Exod. 3:5). If anyone is not Joshua son of Nun (ch. 5), nor an apostle, let him shoe his feet in the preparation of the Gospel of peace. But if anyone is an apostle and can be numbered among the twelve, let him carry no sandal of his own on the way, nor cover his heel to avoid scorpions and snakes; but, already consummated and perfect, let him stand on holy ground, and live in Christ, and follow the Lamb wherever he goes. It is asked whether he has now said "the Gospel of peace" to distinguish it from another Gospel? Or is this rather a property of the Gospel, that it should be named the Gospel of peace? He, therefore, who has peace is shod with the Gospel of Christ; and when he has been shod, he is prepared; and being prepared, let him not think himself perfect, but let him be prepared for this, that he may go on, and going on, may come to the end.

6:16

Above all taking up the shield of faith, with which you may be able to quench all the fiery darts of the wicked one.

As if he had said: In every work carry the shield of faith, so that, covered and protected, you may be able to receive the arrows as they come, and with the art of war turn them aside this way and that. This is the faith upon which even Abraham, after many works and virtues, was barely able to merit that Scripture should say of him: And Abraham believed God, and it was reckoned to him for righteousness (Gen. 15:6). Plain to see, moreover, are the darts of the wicked one, which he wishes to send into our hearts through most evil thoughts; of these he cast one into the heart of Judas, that he should betray the Savior. And so the enemy will not be able even to make a beginning of wounding the soul, if we hold the shield of faith, in which not only are the weapons that come broken, but even the very fire of the weapons is quenched, over which the prophet also laments, saying: All adulterers, their hearts are like an oven (Hos. 7:4). He who holds this boss of faith with a strong hand, and, trusting in the Lord, knows that he is safe from the darts that come, will speak fearlessly: In the Lord I trust: how do you say to my soul: Move to the mountain like a sparrow? For behold, sinners have bent the bow, they have prepared their arrows in their quivers, to shoot in the dark at the upright in heart (Ps. 10:1, 2). Since, then, he says, I trust in the Lord, on what reasoning do you give me counsel not to stand against the onsets of enemies, and against the darts which they have prepared against me in their quivers, wishing to strike not only me but also all the upright80 in heart? Behold, I stand upon the rock, and do not move to the dark mountains; and all the weapons of the enemies, driven back, are turned upon those very ones who aim them.

6:17

And take the helmet of salvation.

Because of this helmet of salvation, all the senses in our head remain intact, and above all the eyes, of which Solomon says in Ecclesiastes: The eyes of the wise man are in his head (Eccles. 2:14). For he knew what the head of a man was, and what those eyes were that are placed in the head of a man. For if the head of a man is Christ, and the eyes of the wise man are in his head, it follows that all our sense, mind, thought, speech and counsel (if indeed we are wise) are in Christ; and in Christ the Word, the light, righteousness, truth and all the virtues.

6:18-19

And the sword of the spirit, which is the word of God. Through all prayer and supplication, praying at all times in the spirit; and watching in it with all insistence and entreaty, for all the saints and for me.

The word of God flows from the Holy Spirit; but the contrary word speaks from the earth, and takes its beginning from there. For he who is from the earth speaks from the earth. He who comes from heaven is above all: and what he has seen and heard, this he testifies (John 3:31). Moreover, the word of God is the sword of the spirit, of which Paul now says: The sword of the spirit, which is the word of God (Heb. 4). For the word of God is living and effective, and sharper than any two-edged sword, and piercing even to the joints of the soul, and of the bones, and of the marrows; which cuts through spirits and divides them, making much progress through the prayer and supplication of those who at all times entreat the Lord in the spirit, according to that text: I will pray with the spirit, I will pray also with the mind (1 Cor. 14:15); and making progress to this end, that through vigils and insistent prayer the Apostle may be enriched in the word and doctrine of God, and that all this wealth may serve the salvation of others, so as to benefit those very ones too who make supplication for him. And at the same time the humility of the Apostle is to be admired, who asks the Ephesians to make supplications for him. For he says: With all insistence, and entreaty for all the saints and for me, so as to make mention of the saints separately, and of himself separately.

6:20

That utterance may be given me in the opening of my mouth, to make known with confidence the mystery of the Gospel, for which I perform an embassy in a chain, so that in it I may dare to speak as I ought.

What he now says: In the opening of my mouth; and elsewhere: My mouth is open to you, O Corinthians (2 Cor. 6:11); and: Opening his mouth, he taught his disciples, saying (Matt. 5:2); and: I opened my mouth and drew in the spirit (Ps. 118:131); and: I will open my mouth in parables (Ps. 77:2), and other things like these, are to be taken as if he had said: let the treasures be opened, and let the mysteries hidden from the ages be laid open, so that the Holy Spirit may enter to bring forth the things that lie hidden. For that this is the meaning of this testimony, that is, that utterance may be given me in the opening of my mouth, what follows proves. With confidence, he says, to make known the mystery of the Gospel. Not at all in parables and proverbs, as both the prophets spoke and the Lord himself, while still placed in the body, saying: The hour comes when I will no longer speak to you in proverbs, but I will tell you with confidence of the Father (John 16:25). This speech of confidence he alone will be able to obtain who does not have a heart that reproaches him: For if our heart does not reproach us, we have confidence toward God, and whatever we ask, we shall receive from him (1 John 3). Rare, therefore, is he who makes known the mystery of the Gospel with confidence, because rare is he who has confidence toward God. For who will boast that he has a chaste heart, or who will stand saying that he is clean from sins? (Prov. 20:9). After this, what it means that he says: For which I perform an embassy in a chain, namely, so that I might make known the mystery of the Gospel, must be touched on briefly. And indeed one who understands it simply will say that, because of his testimony to Christ, he sent these writings from prison and from chains while placed at Rome. But another will contend that he said these things on account of the body of humiliation, and that chain with which we are encompassed, and we do not yet know as we ought to know, and we see through a mirror in an enigma; and that he will truly be able to open the mysteries in the confidence of the Gospel only when he has laid aside the chain and gone out of prison a free man: unless perhaps he too is to be reckoned without chains even in chains who has his way of life in the heavens, and of whom it can be said: But you are not in the flesh but in the spirit, if indeed the spirit of God dwells in you (Rom. 8:9).

6:21-22

But so that you also may know the things concerning me, what I am doing, Tychicus, the beloved brother and faithful minister in the Lord, will make all things known to you; whom I have sent to you for this very purpose, that you may know the things concerning us, and that he may comfort your hearts.

Take it in two ways. Either Tychicus was sent to Ephesus for this reason: to announce to them that the bonds of the apostle Paul had become known throughout the whole praetorium, and that his chain had advanced the faith of the Gospel, at the time when he also wrote to the Colossians, saying: The things concerning me Tychicus will make all known to you, the beloved brother and minister and fellow servant in the Lord, whom I have sent to you for this very purpose, that you may know the things concerning us, and that he may comfort your hearts, with Onesimus, the dearest and faithful brother, who is one of you, who will make known to you all the things that are being done here (Col. 4:7 ff.). For it was a great consolation to hear that Paul, at Rome, in the mistress of cities and the citadel of the Roman empire, was triumphing from prison and from his bonds. Or else Tychicus was sent for this purpose: to announce to them the life and way of life of Paul, of which they were ignorant, and to give the deeds and virtues of the Apostle, as a kind of pattern of living, to those who were learning and to those who wished to imitate him. Nor could it be a small consolation to those who desired to emulate the things they had learned the Apostle had done.

6:23

Peace to the brethren, and love with faith, from God the Father and the Lord Jesus Christ.

If there are any other gifts that are bestowed by God the Father and the Lord Jesus Christ, among them peace holds not the least place: the peace which surpasses all sense and guards the hearts and understandings of the saints, a certain serenity and tranquillity of a soul at rest, which puts to flight every storm and whirlwind of disturbances. Like it is love with faith, which God the Father and the Son likewise give together, so that we may love God with our whole heart and our neighbors as ourselves, and may pray for our enemies. This peace and love, which the Apostle invokes upon believers, only those possess who deserve to be called by the name of brethren. Peace indeed to the brethren, and love with faith and peace. Therefore the Father bestows both love and faith just as the Son does; and the heresy is silenced which will not have it that the Son can do the same things as the Father.

6:24

Grace be with all who love the Lord Jesus Christ in incorruption. Amen.

Some have interpreted this so as to think that he loves our Lord Jesus Christ who is a stranger to the works of corruption, taking the works of corruption to lie in sexual intercourse. Hence both custom and common speech call virgins, and those who do not know intercourse with women, incorrupt; but corrupt those who have tasted pleasure of this kind. They also apply that testimony: If anyone corrupts the temple of God, God will corrupt him (1 Cor. 3:17). But I do not know whether they can explain what is written: Each one has his own gift from God, one in this way, and another in that (ibid., 7:7). Let us see, then, whether it may not perhaps be better to understand every sin as a corruption of the soul, and that those who are free from sin are called incorrupt: so that those who love the Lord Jesus Christ are in incorruption so long as they are not held by the bonds of sin, and the grace of God is with them. At the same time I think it has now been set down to distinguish those who love the Lord Jesus Christ, but not in incorruption: those have the grace of Christ who love him in incorruption. For how many love the Lord, ready to endure exile, ready for martyrdom, ready for poverty and every kind of insult for his sake, and nonetheless are overcome by the passion of the flesh! But on these the Apostle does not invoke grace, because the grace of the Lord is with all who love him in incorruption.
