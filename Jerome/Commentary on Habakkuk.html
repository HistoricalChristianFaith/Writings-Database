Commentary on Habakkuk

Latin source: Jerome's commentary on Habakkuk, in two books addressed to Chromatius, bishop of Aquileia (written at Bethlehem about 392–393; the second book is given to the Canticle of Habakkuk 3), from the critical text of M. Adriaen, Corpus Christianorum, Series Latina 76A (Turnhout, 1970), pp. 579–654, verified against the page images and collated with the text of Vallarsi and Migne, with the edition's evident misprints corrected. Each lemma gives Habakkuk in Jerome's own version from the Hebrew and then, after "LXX:", in his Latin of the Septuagint; all Scripture is translated as Jerome quotes it, from his own Latin. Italics mark the lemmata and the other Scripture he quotes. Psalms keep the Latin (LXX) numbering, with the Hebrew number in parentheses. Greek words are kept as printed, with an English gloss, and Hebrew words keep Jerome's own transliteration. This English translation is released into the public domain.

Prologue

First, Chromatius, most learned of bishops, it is fitting for us to know that among the Greeks and Latins the name of the prophet is read corruptly as Ambacum, who among the Hebrews is called Habakkuk, and it is interpreted embrace, or, to render it more expressively in Greek, περίληψις, that is, embracing. Next, that where the Seventy translators and Symmachus and Theodotion translated λῆμμα, that is, assumption, in the Hebrew there stands massa, which Aquila translated as weight; on this we have argued more fully in the prophet Nahum. Massa, moreover, is never set at the head of a title except when what is seen is heavy and full of weight and toil. Hence the present prophecy too must have something of severity, so that, as in Nahum the weight that was seen against Nineveh, the city of the Assyrians, was heavy, so in this one also it may be asked for whom the weight is that is revealed to the prophet's sight. Now there are four prophets in the volume of the twelve prophets, of whom three have at the beginning the title λῆμμα, that is, weight: Nahum, Habakkuk and Malachi. Zechariah, moreover, sets two titles of this kind, in the middle and near the end, of which one is: The burden of the word of the Lord in the land of Hadrach, and of Damascus his rest; the other at the end: The burden of the word of the Lord upon Israel. On Nahum, through your prayers, a book has already been published; on Zechariah and Malachi, if life keeps me company, there will be a discussion. Now we have Habakkuk in hand, who, either because he is beloved of the Lord, is called embracing, or, because he comes to grips with God in contest and wrestling and — so to speak — in an embrace, has received the name of one who embraces, that is, of one who wrestles. For no one has dared with so bold a voice to challenge God to a dispute about justice and to say to him: Why, in human affairs and in the πολιτείᾳ (commonwealth) of this world, does so great an iniquity of things hold sway? Shall I cry out to you, suffering violence, and will you not save? Why have you shown me iniquity and toil to see? The law is torn apart, and judgment does not come through to the end, because the wicked prevails against the just; therefore perverse judgment goes forth. Do you see that it is a rash voice, and in a way that of a blasphemer, to challenge one's Creator to judgment in this way, and for a fragile vessel to argue against the potter why it was made thus or thus? This too must be noticed: that the assumption or weight, which we have already said are heavy things, is the vision of the prophet; and, against the perverse doctrine of Montanus, he understands what he sees, and he neither speaks like a madman nor, in the manner of raving women, gives forth sound without mind. Hence the Apostle also commands that, if while others are prophesying something has been revealed to another, those who were speaking first should be silent. And immediately: For God, he says, is not of dissension, but of peace. From this it is understood that when someone falls silent by his own will and gives place to another to speak, he can both speak and be silent when he wishes. But he who speaks in ecstasy, that is, unwillingly, has neither silence nor speech in his own power. Learn this also — since you once and for all demand of me forcefully that I interpret the history for you as a kind of steps and ladders for one striving toward higher things —: that the prophecy is against Babylon and Nebuchadnezzar, king of the Chaldeans, so that, just as the earlier prophet Nahum, whom Habakkuk follows, had a prophecy against Nineveh and the Assyrians, who laid waste the ten tribes which were called Israel, so Habakkuk has a prophecy against Babylon and Nebuchadnezzar, by whom Judah and Jerusalem and the temple were overthrown. And so that you may know that Habakkuk lived at the time when the two tribes which were called Judah had already been led into captivity, Daniel can teach you, to whom Habakkuk is sent with a meal into the lions' den, although among the Hebrews this very history is not read. Therefore, whether one accepts that scripture or does not accept it, either way it is on our side: for either he accepts it, and then the book of Habakkuk is composed after the event had taken place; or he does not accept it, and Habakkuk writes as a prophet the things that he knows are to come.

Book One

1:2-3a

How long, O Lord, shall I cry out, and you will not hear? Shall I cry aloud to you, suffering violence, and will you not save? Why have you shown me iniquity and grief, to see plunder and injustice against me? LXX: How long, O Lord, shall I cry out, and you will not hear? Shall I cry aloud to you, suffering violence, and will you not save? Why have you shown me toils and griefs, to see misery and impiety? Meanwhile, according to the letter, the prophet complains against God: why does Nebuchadnezzar lay waste the temple and Judah; why is Jerusalem, once the city of the Lord, destroyed? Why does the people cry out and is not heard? Why does it cry aloud to the Lord, oppressed by the Chaldeans, and is not saved? Why, too, has the prophet himself, or the people in whose person he now speaks, lived for this and been brought this far, to see the iniquity of the enemies and his own toil? Why does injustice prevail against him? And he says this out of anguish of mind, not knowing that gold is refined in fire, and that the three boys came out of the furnace purer than they went in. But we can also take it generally: that the prophet, speaking in the person of human impatience, seeing sinners abounding and possessing riches in the world, their sons like a new plantation in their youth, and their daughters adorned like the likeness of the temple, their storehouses full, overflowing from this into that, their sheep fruitful and multiplied in their ways, and the other things that are written more fully in Psalm 143 (144), bursts out into a complaining voice full of grief. Why do you look upon the despisers, and keep silent while the wicked tramples down one more just than himself, and you make men like the fish of the sea, and like creeping things that have no leader? Something of this kind we read also in Psalm 72 (73): But my feet were almost moved, my steps had almost slipped, and the rest. And again in the same psalm: And if there is knowledge in the Most High. For behold, the sinners, abounding in the world, have obtained riches, and the rest, as far as my hands. But they say these things not knowing the unsearchable judgments of God and the depth of the riches of his wisdom and knowledge, and that God does not see as man sees. Man looks only at present things; God knows the things to come and the eternal. And it is as if a sick man, burning with fever, were to ask for cold water and say to the physician: I suffer violence, I am tormented, I burn, I am dying; how long, physician, shall I cry out, and you will not hear? And the most wise and most merciful physician were to answer him: I know at what time I ought to give what you ask; I do not take pity now, because that mercy is cruelty, and your will asks against yourself. So also the Lord our God, knowing the weights and measures of his clemency, sometimes does not hear the one who cries out, in order to test him, and to stir him the more to ask, and, as if refining him in the fire, to make him more just and purer. Understanding this, the Apostle, according to the fact that he obtained mercy from the Lord, says: Let us not fail in tribulations, and he blesses God at all times, and knows that he who perseveres to the end, he shall be saved. And he glories in toil and grief. And with Jeremiah he says: I will call upon tribulation and misery. So that, just as another calls upon God, so the holy man and unconquered warrior, in order to exercise and prove himself, longs for tribulation and misery to come.

1:3b-4

And judgment has come about, and contradiction is the stronger.

Because of this the law is torn apart, and judgment does not come through to the end, because the wicked prevails against the just; therefore perverse judgment goes forth. LXX: Against me judgment has come about, and the judge takes; therefore the law is torn apart, and judgment does not come through to the end, because the wicked prevails against the just; therefore perverse judgment goes forth. The prophet or the people is still speaking to the Lord: that judgment was given against him not by truth but by power, and that he obtained nothing from the law and justice. Hence the judgment itself did not have its end. Now the end of judgment is to judge justly. And why he dares to say this he shows in what follows, saying: Because the wicked Nebuchadnezzar prevailed against the just Judah; and that this is the reason why he said that judgment did not come through to the end, because it is unjust and perverse that Josiah, a just king, should be slaughtered by the Egyptian king, that Daniel, Hananiah, Mishael and Azariah should be slaves, and the Babylonian emperor should rule, and Belshazzar, among his harlots and concubines, should drink from the bowls of God. These things the prophet says about the state of his own times — for we follow even the lowliness of the history, since you once wished it —. But according to the LXX, it is the common complaint of the saints to God: why unjust judgment is passed against them, and they shed innocent blood in persecutions; and if ever they have stood before the tribunal of secular judges, the judge, having taken bribes, condemns the innocent and frees the guilty. This indeed can be said not only of the judges of the world but sometimes also of the leaders of the churches: that for the sake of bribes they tear the law apart and do not bring judgment through to the end, and the wicked prevails against the just, and in judgment the sin of the rich man is defended rather than the truth of the poor man. Hence the complaint that perverse judgment goes forth; but we ought not to be troubled over this inequality of things, seeing that even at the beginning of the world the just Abel was killed by the wicked Cain, and afterwards, while Jacob was in exile, Esau reigned in his father's house; and the Egyptians afflict the children of Israel with clay and brick; and the Lord, against whom the complaint is now directed, is crucified by the Jews, and Barabbas the robber is chosen. The day will fail me if I wish to enumerate how in this world, while the wicked prevail, the just are oppressed.

1:5

Look among the nations and see, and marvel, and be astounded, for a work has been done in your days which no one will believe when it is told. LXX: See, you despisers, and look, and marvel at marvels and perish, for I work a work in your days which you will not believe if anyone tells it. Symmachus, for what we have said, For a work has been done in your days, translated: For a work will be done in your days, the rest likewise. Again, at the beginning of the chapter, where in the Hebrew is written rau baggoim, and we translated Look among the nations, and the LXX put See, you despisers, while Aquila and Symmachus and Theodotion agree with our translation, in a certain other edition, ἀνωνύμη (anonymous), I found: You will see the slanderers; and in another, likewise without the author's title: You will see those who turn aside. Therefore, to the earlier complaints of the prophet, who protests and says, How long, O Lord, shall I cry out, and you will not hear? and the rest up to the end of this opening, the voice of the Lord answering is brought in: that he should see this iniquity, which he thinks is done in Israel alone, among the nations; and that not, as the prophet supposed, Judah and Jerusalem alone have been handed over to the Chaldeans, but all the nations round about; and that he would grow so strong, and would fall afterwards, that if anyone foretold what is to come, it would seem incredible because of the greatness of the thing. But this too that the LXX and the other translators have given, See, you despisers, or you will see the slanderers and those who turn aside, agrees with the sense of this place; and by the very word are rebuked the audacity and contempt toward the Lord of those in whose person the prophet had cried out: why they dared to despise the majesty of God, and to speak rashly, and, as far as lies in them, to slander the providence of God, and to turn away from the Lord, accusing him of iniquity. You will see, therefore, you despisers, and then you will marvel, and you will count all your complaint as nothing, when you have perceived what I do in your days, lest perhaps you say: What does the future concern us? A work that will be so great, and will crush all your protest, that if anyone now foretold that it would be, you would not easily lend belief. But what this work is, is shown in what follows.

1:6-11

For behold, I will raise up the Chaldeans, a bitter and swift nation, walking over the breadth of the earth, to possess tents not its own. It is dreadful and terrible; from itself is judgment, and its burden will go forth. Its horses are lighter than leopards, and swifter than evening wolves; and its horsemen will spread abroad; for its horsemen will come from afar, they will fly like an eagle hastening to eat. All will come for plunder; their face is a burning wind; and it will gather captivity like sand. And he will triumph over kings, and tyrants will be his laughingstock; he will laugh at every fortress, and will heap up a mound, and will take it. Then the spirit will be changed, and he will pass through and fall; this is his strength, that of his god. LXX: For behold, I will raise up the Chaldeans, a bitter and swift nation, which walks over the breadth of the earth, to possess tents not its own. It is terrible and illustrious; from itself will be its judgment, and its assumption will go forth from itself. And its horses will leap beyond leopards, and will be swifter than the wolves of Arabia, and its horsemen will ride, and will come with a rush from far away; and they will fly like an eagle ready to eat. Consummation will come upon the wicked, who resist their faces from the opposite side; and it will gather captivity like sand; and he will take delight in kings, and tyrants are his sport; and he will make sport of every fortress, and will cast up a mound, and will take it; then he will change his spirit and pass through, and will be propitiated; this is the strength of my God. What I had said to you, Look among the nations, and see, and wonder, and be astounded, for a work will be done in your days which no one will believe when it is told: this is what the following discourse describes: Behold, I will raise up Nebuchadnezzar and the Chaldeans, a most warlike and swift nation, to whose strength and boldness in making war almost all the Greeks who have written barbarian histories are witnesses. This nation is not content with its own borders, but, running to and fro, it roams over the breadth of the earth. And this is its work: not to work the land with the plowshare, but to live by plunder and the sword, and to possess cities not its own; before it lays a hand on them, before it bursts out into war, it carries terror in its countenance. But as for what he says, From itself judgment and its burden will go forth, for which Symmachus translated, It will judge for itself, and will go forth by its own decree, it is to be understood either thus: it will appoint rulers from its own nation, and its power and its sword will have no attendants from other nations; or certainly: as it has done, so it will be done to it; and it will be laid waste just as it laid waste. The horses also and the horsemen, who will come from afar, will be so quick to pursue and to lay everything waste that they will surpass leopards and evening wolves. For wolves are said to be fiercer as night draws near, roused to madness by hunger the whole day. The horsemen, then, will fly, not to fight, since no one will resist, but to range about like the eagle, to which everything among flying creatures is subject, hastening to devour. And just as at the blast of a burning wind all green things wither, so at the sight of them all things will be laid waste; and so great will be the number of captives and of plunder that, by ὑπερβολὴν (hyperbole), it could even be made equal to the sand. He too, that is, Nebuchadnezzar, will reign over the whole world, and, triumphing over kings before his chariot, he will hold them in derision and count them among his amusements; and he will be of such great power and pride that he will strive to overcome nature and to take the most strongly fortified cities by the might of his army. For he will come to Tyre, and, throwing a mound into the sea, he will make a peninsula of the island, and land will provide an entrance into the city between the waves of the sea. For this reason he will both laugh at every fortress, and heap up a mound, and take it, that is, the fortress, or Tyre; and this indeed is clearly shown in Ezekiel, where it is said: Nebuchadnezzar, king of Babylon, subjected his army to great labor against Tyre. Every head was bald, and every shoulder stripped of hair, and no wages were given to him and to his army against Tyre; and for the work in which he served against it. And when he has heaped up a mound, and nothing has stood in the way of his strength, then his spirit will be changed into pride, and, believing himself to be God, he will set up a golden image in Babylon, which he will compel all the nations to worship. When he has done this, he will pass into the form of a beast, and afterwards will fall; for which Aquila and Symmachus translated καὶ πλημμελήσει, that is, and he will offend; holy Scripture having this custom, that it puts uasam, that is, he will offend, for what means: he will cease to be what he had been. We also have something of this kind in the idiom of our own language, when we say: The army has been beaten, for what means: it has been killed and cut down; and: The vineyard and the field have sinned, for what means: the vintage and the crops have had no yield. But what is said at the end of the chapter, This is his strength, that of his god, is to be read εἰρωνικῶς (ironically), so that the sense is: This is his strength, which Bel his god gave him. To his worship he compelled all nations, even by written decree and the threat of death, with a most savage command. These things according to the Hebrew. Now let us come to the LXX, so that, setting out each sentence, we may fit to it the allegorical interpretation. Behold, I will raise up the Chaldeans, a bitter and swift nation, which walks over the breadth of the earth, to possess tents not its own. God threatens the despisers and slanderers of his providence that he will raise up the Chaldeans, who are interpreted as it were demons; signifying either the most wicked angels, who minister to his fury and wrath and to the tribulation he brings upon sinners, or the souls of the most wicked men, through whom he torments those who deserve it. Now these Chaldeans are a bitter and swift nation, not sparing and quickly fulfilling what has been commanded them. And it walks over the breadth of the earth — for wide and spacious is the way that leads to death —, along which that rich man in the Gospel also walked, who shone in purple, and those of whom it is said: Who sleep on beds of ivory, and abound in luxuries on their couches. Who eat suckling calves from the herds, and drink refined wine, and are anointed with the finest ointments; who, because they walk on the wide way, are called the breadth of the earth, which is trodden by the footsteps of the Chaldeans. For they would not walk along the strait and narrow way that leads to life, along which Paul walked, glorying in tribulations and distresses. And the Chaldeans walk over the breadth of the earth to possess tents not their own. For every rational soul, although through its own vice and fault it has become a lodging of the Chaldeans, is nevertheless by nature a tent of God. And although in the Gospel the most wicked demon says, I will go into my house from which I came out, he is not to be believed; for no rational creature was made for this, to be a dwelling of a demon. There follows: It is terrible and illustrious; from him will be its judgment, and its assumption will go forth from him. The Chaldean is terrible because of the many and varied punishments he brings upon the despisers; illustrious, because he takes to himself the glory of divinity. And through his oracles and false responses, and the cures of diseases which he himself had sent in, he seems illustrious among the ignorant and the despisers of God. For the judgment and punishment of the despiser will be from him, that is, from himself, or from the Chaldean. For according to the Apostle they will be handed over to him for punishment, that they may learn not to blaspheme; and whoever repents and is converted to God will go forth from him, though before he was held in his hands and was the assumption of the Chaldean. If ever we see that someone has served the devil for a long time, and afterwards has been converted to God, let us say of him: And his assumption has gone forth from him. For whoever repent and forsake the demons, to whom, like horses, they formerly offered their backs to be sat upon, and with the swiftness of leopards and of wolves at evening have thrown off and dashed down their riders, and have come with an empty and light back to carry him who, meek and poor, mounted the foal of an ass, these, as if hurrying from afar, and not content with running and rushing, will take to flight, and will come like an eagle, to feed on the flesh of the word of God and to satisfy the hunger of so long a time. For in what is said, Καὶ ἐξιππάζονται οἱ ἱππεῖς αὐτοῦ, which we too translated, And its horsemen will be ridden, according to the sense we set out above, Symmachus translated: Its horsemen will be poured out, that is, they will fall and be dashed to the ground. Now they are rightly called wolves of Arabia, that is, of evening and of the west, in whom a most wicked way of life has set, and who, once placed in darkness, abandon the evening with all their stride; and when they have left it, and have flown, ready to eat the flesh of the word of God, then consummation will come upon the wicked, that is, upon the Chaldeans, who resisted the faces of the penitent so that they might not return to their Lord. Wherefore consummation will come upon the wicked, who resist their faces from the opposite side. And when these have been consumed, and the captivity snatched from their hands, then the divine word will gather like sand the captivity of the Chaldeans, and will take delight in kings, and tyrants will be its sport, seeing that the devil, once most powerful, and his kingdoms — showing which to the Savior also, he says, All these I will give you, if you fall down and worship me —, have been destroyed by his coming; for it is the delight of prudence and the pleasure of wisdom when folly is destroyed, and the once great power of tyrants, overcome and cast down, is turned into derision. For not the dragon alone, who is the beginning of the Lord's creation, made a sport for the angels, was formed to be held in sport by the Lord; nor will God give him alone, like a sparrow to a little child; but if anyone else also is cruel and of a tyrannical mind, he will be handed over to the word of God for derision. And it says: He will make sport of every fortress. But what other fortress is there, except that of which the Apostle speaks: For the weapons of our warfare are not carnal, but mighty for God for the destruction of fortresses, destroying thoughts, and every height that lifts itself up against the knowledge of God. If therefore there are any fortifications, either in discourses which promise that they have a height and greatness contrary to the truth, or in all the glory and riches and strength that is praised in the world, all will be destroyed, and the word of God will make sport of every fortress. And it will cast up a mound, and will take it by the mound and the earthly things it brings forward, convicting it of fragility in those things in which it formerly seemed to itself to have some strength. And when this has been fulfilled, then the spirit will be turned, and will by no means punish as it had punished before, but, passing through the sinners, it will pray for them and reconcile them to their former Lord; in all of which is shown the strength of our God, which has accomplished such great things. You see how rugged the places are, and opposed to the truth of the history; and how the things we interpreted according to the letter concerning the Chaldeans now seem, according to tropology, to sound the clemency and liberty of those who have escaped from the hands of the Chaldeans. The history is strict and has no freedom to wander. Tropology is free, and bounded only by these laws: that it follow the piety of understanding and the context of the discourse, and not be violent in joining things that are greatly contrary to one another.

1:12

Are you not from the beginning, O Lord my God, my Holy One? And shall we not die? O Lord, you have set him for judgment, and you have established him strong, that you might chastise. LXX: Are you not from the beginning, O Lord my God, my Holy One? And shall we not die? O Lord, you have set him for judgment; and he has formed me, that I might reprove in his discipline. Symmachus more plainly: Are you not from the beginning, O Lord my God, my Holy One, so that we should not die? O Lord, you have set him to judge, you have appointed him strong to chastise. To the complaint of the prophet, who said: How long, O Lord, shall I cry, and you will not hear? God had answered, saying: Look among the nations and see; and after this preface he had added: Behold, I will raise up the Chaldeans, a bitter and swift nation. And when the whole description was complete, whether of Nebuchadnezzar or of the devil, he recalls at the end: Then his spirit will be changed, and he will pass through, and he will fall; this is his strength, of his god. The prophet, hearing and understanding this, that Nebuchadnezzar against Judah, or the devil against believers, receives power for this reason, that he may chastise them, and that after the chastisement he himself also may be punished at the end, answered the Lord: Are you then, O Lord my God, my Holy One — and he says this with the feeling of one who coaxes and repents —, you who created us from the beginning? By whose mercy we subsist up to now? For I did not know that our adversaries could do so much, nor had I known that Nebuchadnezzar, or the devil of this world, had received power over all the nations as well. As far, therefore, as his strength is concerned, none of us can resist him. But as far as your mercy is concerned, all that we live is yours, that we have not been slain by him and led on to the works of death. For you, O Lord, have set him to judge, that he himself may be enemy and avenger, and that through him you may chastise whoever has sinned against you. But since we have once interpreted the Chaldeans as the demons, and Nebuchadnezzar as their king, the devil, we ought briefly to touch upon the description of the devil and his power, so that the prophet may justly say: O Lord, you have set him for judgment, and you have established him strong, that you might chastise. A nation of demons is raised up against the unbelievers and the despisers, bitter for punishing, and present everywhere. It walks also wherever the breadth of the earth extends, to possess men in whom Christ ought to have dwelt. It is horrible and terrible, and can hardly be overcome by anyone, nor is it broken until, weighed down by the greatness of its sins and the weight of its iniquity, it has come to the consummation. Its horses and horsemen, in the likeness of leopards and wolves, will always thirst for blood, and they will long for plunder; they will feign absence, and while they are not expected, they will come swiftly from afar. They will fly like an eagle, which, lifting up its flight, wants to set its nest among the stars of heaven, and always hastens to the prey. And in Ezekiel too the devil is described under the figure of Nebuchadnezzar and of the king of Egypt. There is none of the demons who spares; all will come hastening to the prey; before their face is a burning wind; whatever they look upon, whatever meets their gaze, they will long to burn and destroy. But the king will be most mighty in the midst of the captives, and he will receive their number, like the sand of the sea, from his attendants on every side, and he himself will triumph over kings, and tyrants will be his laughingstock. For he will deceive many saints by his tripping up, and those who seemed to themselves most mighty, and to exercise a tyranny against the demons and to cast them out of possessed bodies, he will subject to his servitude and count as a joke. But he himself, most strong, having gathered a band and assembled an army of the lost, will laugh at every fortress, and whatever is strong he will strive to overthrow. For he will heap up a mound, that is, he will surround it with earthworks, and when the earth has been heaped up, he will easily take every fortress. But after so great a victory his spirit will be changed, and his mouth will pass through even to heaven, and making himself God, he will begin to blaspheme his Creator. When he has done this, he will fall, and his ruin will show how strong his divinity was, and the pretense of the idols under whose images he was subjugating men to his worship. The prophet, therefore, hearing these things, that the king of this world is so great and of such a kind that he gathers captives like the sand of the sea and triumphs over kings; and that tyrants are his laughingstock, and he laughs at every fortress, and first heaps up a mound and afterwards takes it; and that he is of such great pride that he dares to resist against his Creator and to make himself as a god — the prophet, who before was speaking boldly to the Lord, and had declared that he himself, or his people, or those in whose person he was complaining, were just — now bursts out into words of coaxing and says: Are you then from the beginning, O Lord my God, my Holy One? And that we do not die, nor are taken by so great an enemy, is of your clemency. For you, O Lord, have appointed him as an executioner, and you have made him strong, so that either no one or hardly anyone can resist his strength. Moreover, according to the LXX, what is said at the end, And he has formed me, that I might reprove in his discipline, can be referred to the person of the prophet, so that the sense is: But I have been inspired as a prophet for this reason, that I may reprove those who transgress and teach the discipline of the Lord. Some think it is said of the Lord, who was formed by the Father, and assumed a body, for this reason, that he might instruct men in the doctrine of God the Father. But how far this disagrees with the context of what goes before, and with the tenor of the whole passage, will be not so much my judgment as the reader's.

1:13-14

Your eyes are pure, so that you may not see evil, and you will not be able to look upon iniquity. Why do you not look upon those who do iniquitous things, and are silent while the wicked devours one more just than himself. And you will make men like the fish of the sea, and like a creeping thing that has no prince? LXX: The eye is pure, so that it may not see evils, and you will not be able to look upon sorrow. Why do you look upon the despisers, will you be silent when the wicked has devoured one more just than himself? And you will make men like the fish of the sea, and like creeping things that have no leader. Jeremiah too says something of this kind to God: You are just, O Lord, I confess; yet I will speak judgments to you: Why is it that the way of the wicked prospers; that all abound who transgress and do wrong? You planted them and they have taken root, they have begotten sons and have borne fruit; you are near to their mouth, and far from their reins. Therefore Habakkuk also, in the same thought: Pure, he says, O Lord, are your eyes, and I know that you do not willingly look upon what is evil and unjust, nor can anyone doubt your justice. Yet why do you suffer the Babylonians to vaunt themselves with such great cruelty, and Israel, the just, to be oppressed by the wicked Nebuchadnezzar? Not that the one who is oppressed is perfectly just, but that he is more just than the one who oppresses him. And just as fish that have no prince, and irrational beasts, and the multitude of creeping things, being without providence, are subject to the stronger, and whoever is more powerful in strength rules over the other, so among men, a rational animal, made in your likeness, reason will have no force, nor merits, but bodily strength and irrational might. But if we wish to understand it generally, of providence, the prophet asking: Why does the devil have so much power in the world, and, while God rules, another exercises tyranny? — the sense will be of this kind, and this exposition will be joined to what goes before: I know, O Lord my God, my Holy One, that because you provide and defend, we do not die, and I know that you have set the adversary for this reason, that through him, as through an executioner, you might chastise sinners and not slay them. I know that nothing unjust pleases you, and that your eyes are pure from all iniquity, and that you cannot look upon the sorrows of those who have been subjected to injustice. Yet I cannot find the cause why the unjust Cain kills the just Abel, and you are silent. Why, while the whale rages and devours everything, not only the smaller fish but also your own Jonah himself is devoured? Why does the wicked conquer, and the just is conquered? Nor do I say this as though I knew of anyone being justified in your sight and being without sin, or as though I were ignorant of human frailty; but just as Sodom and Gomorrah are just in comparison with Jerusalem, and the publican in the Gospel becomes more just by comparison with the Pharisee, so too this one who is oppressed by the devil is indeed a sinner, but he is more just than the one who oppresses him. Why, then, is there no measure, nor weight, so that, if once the just is oppressed and subjected, he may be subjected not to the wicked but to one more just than himself? Shall I say that anything happens without you, and that against your will the wicked can do so much? To think this is blasphemous. Since, therefore, you are the ruler and Lord of the universe, it must be you who do what cannot happen without you. And he says these things, not because the prophet himself thinks so, as I have testified above, but because he expresses human impatience in his own person; just as we often see the Apostle take upon himself the various opinions of men, and now say: But I see another law in my members, fighting against the law of my mind, and taking me captive in the law of sin, which is in my members. And, as though he were a beginner: Brothers, I do not consider that I have laid hold of it, and: We know in part, and we prophesy in part. And again, as though he were perfect: As many, therefore, as are perfect, let us be of this mind, when surely it does not belong to one and the same man to say that he knows in part and that he is perfect. And lest perhaps you think that this is not the Apostle's custom but our own argument, he himself says to the Corinthians: But these things, brothers, I have transferred to myself and Apollos for your sakes, that you may learn in us. Otherwise, how can God so hold men as the fish of the sea, and as creeping things that have no prince, when the angels of each one daily see the face of the Father who is in heaven, and the angel of the Lord encamps round about those who fear him, and delivers them? Just as, therefore, among men the providence of God runs even through each individual, so in the other animals we can understand a general dispensation, indeed, and order, and course of things. For example, how the multitude of fish is born and lives in the waters, how creeping things and four-footed beasts arise on the earth, and by what foods they are nourished. But it is absurd to bring down the majesty of God to this, that he should know at each moment how many gnats are born, or how many die, what multitude of bugs and fleas and flies there is on the earth, how many fish swim about in the water, and which of the smaller ought to yield as prey to the larger. Let us not be such foolish flatterers of God that, while we drag his power down even to the lowest things, we do injury to ourselves, saying that there is the same providence for rational as for irrational things. Hence that apocryphal book is to be condemned for its folly, in which it is written that a certain angel named Tyri presides over creeping things, and that in this likeness proper angels have been assigned to the fish too, and the trees, and all the beasts, to guard them.

LXX: And the Lord answered to me, and said: Write the vision, and plainly on boxwood, that he who reads may pursue these things; for the vision is still for a time, and will rise at the end, and not in vain. If he fails, wait for him, for coming he will come and will not tarry; if he draws himself back, it will not please my soul in him. But the just shall live by my faith. For tablets and boxwood, which in Hebrew is called alluoth, Symmachus translated pages. And where the Seventy put: But the just shall live by my faith, all alike translated, shall live by his faith. Indeed Symmachus, translating more expressively, says: But the just shall live through his own proper faith, which in Greek is said ὁ δίκαιος τῇ ἑαυτοῦ πίστει ζήσει; for baemunatho, which means in his faith, if it had the letter iod and not uau at the end, as the Seventy thought, and were read Baemunathi, they would rightly have translated in my faith. But as it is, the likeness of the letters uau and iod, which differ from one another only in size, was the cause of the error. Why this was said, the following discussion takes in. According to the promise which is made to the holy man in Isaiah, saying: While you are still speaking, I will say, Behold, I am here, now too the Lord answered the prophet, and commanded him to write the vision and to set her out upon tablets, that is, to write more plainly. But I think the tablets are those of which the Apostle also speaks to the Corinthians: You are our letter, written in our hearts, which is known and read by all men; being made manifest that you are the letter of Christ, ministered by us, and written not with ink but with the Spirit of the living God, not on tablets of stone but on fleshly tablets of the heart. And Solomon too signifies something of the kind in Proverbs, saying: Write her upon the breadth of your heart. And he is commanded to write more plainly, so that the reader may be able to run, and his speed and his desire of reading may be held back by no hindrance. And he commands this because the vision is still far off, and set in an appointed time. And when the end of things has come, then he too will come, and the prophecy will be proved true, fulfilled in deed. But if perhaps, through your desire, O reader, and your ardor to see the vision, what has been promised should seem to you to delay a little, do not despair of its coming, but wait patiently: for you have me, who promise you and say: Coming he will come, and will not tarry. But if anyone is unbelieving of this pledge of mine, and while I say: Coming he will come, and will not tarry, begins to doubt and to waver silently within himself, thinking that what delays for a time will not come, this man will displease my soul, according to that saying: Your new moons and sabbaths my soul hates. But that God spoke of his soul, we ought to take for mind and thought, so that the meaning is: he will displease my sense. And just as he will displease who, when I promise, doubts that what I pledge will come, so the just man who believes my promise will live in his faith. With these lines, as it were, the picture of this chapter has been sketched. But what he says is of this kind, provided only that we mix in the translation of the Seventy too. Write in your heart; and as little children who receive the first elements of letters train their crooked strokes and trembling hand on boxwood, and by practice grow used to writing correctly, so you too, who have spoken in the person of the doubting people, write what I say on the tablets of your heart and on the boxwood of your breast. For it is a vision which is promised and which is commanded to be set out and written more plainly, so that it may be wrapped in no cloud and obscured by no windings of riddles; let a clear hope have a clear pledge. And this, O prophet, I command, not because you do not know — for you would not be a prophet if you were ignorant —, but so that what you have written more plainly the reader may be able to read and run through without hindrance and difficulty, which the Seventy translated: Ὅπως διώκῃ ὁ ἀναγινώσκων, that is, that he who reads may pursue, according to that sense which is written to Timothy: Pursue justice, piety, faith, charity, patience, gentleness. And to the Romans: Pursuing hospitality. And to the Corinthians: Pursue charity. But the vision itself, of which I said to you: Write the vision, and make it plain on boxwood, that he who reads may be able to pursue, is still set in an appointed time, of which the Savior says: In an acceptable time I have heard you, and in the day of salvation I have helped you. And it will rise at the consummation of the world, and in the last hour of the day, of which John also speaks: Little children, now it is the last hour, and it will not come in vain; for it will save many, and with the remnant of the Israelite people it will gather a multitude of the nations. But if she withdraws herself a little, and the vision begins to come more slowly than your wishes, O reader, who are commanded to read on the boxwood and on the tablets which the prophet wrote out, wait for her, for coming she will come, and will not tarry. But if your faith doubts, and you think that what I pledge will not come to pass, you will have it as a great punishment that you will displease my soul. But the just man, who believes my words and does not doubt the things I promise, will have as reward life everlasting. Nor ought you at once to charge, either in my slaying you or in my giving life to him, that there is respect of persons with me; for he himself is the cause of his own giving of life, who lives by his faith, just as you, by having withdrawn yourself and refused to believe, have displeased my soul. And in these words there is plainly a prophecy of the coming of Christ. Hence the question that was put forward is also solved: that until he comes, iniquity rules in the world, and judgment does not come to its end, and the true Nebuchadnezzar catches men with his net and dragnet like little fish, and the rational animal like a creeping thing that has no prince. Moreover, as to what we translated for vision: For the vision is still far off, and if he delays, wait for him, that is, the vision (uisum), let no one, deceived by error, think that I put uisus, which is masculine and less usual in Latin, for uisio, which is feminine. For hazon, which Aquila translates ὁραματισμὸν (vision), is called among the Hebrews uisus, in the masculine gender, and to the end, under the same gender of the vision, that is, of uisus, the masculine inflection is kept. But the Seventy, saying: Write the vision, and afterwards: If he fails, wait for him; for coming he will come, and will not tarry; if he withdraws himself, it will not please my soul in him, first translated uisio in the feminine gender, which among the Hebrews, as we have said, is masculine. Then, following the Hebrew gender, where it is inflected in the masculine, wait for him, and it will not please my soul in him, they too inflected it in the masculine gender. Whereas surely they ought, in keeping with their first translating uisio, to have put the feminine gender of the vision in the rest also, so as to say: Wait for her for coming she will come; but if she withdraws herself, it will not please my soul in her, that is, in the vision. This for this reason, that we might not seem to keep silent about what we knew. However, I am not unaware that according to their translation it can also be taken thus: Write the vision in which Christ is promised, and, whether on boxwood, or on tablets, or, as Symmachus translated, on pages, weave this discourse of your prophecy: that at the appointed time and at the consummation of the world my Son will come, to save the lost sheep of the house of Israel, and to join other sheep also to the ancient sheep, and, making one flock, to join the two rods, which Ezekiel, that is, the strength of God, holds joined and cleaving to one another in his prophetic hand. But if, O prophet, or you, O people — in whose person my prophet also seemed to doubt —, Christ withdraws himself a little and seems to delay, wait for him; for coming he will come, and will not tarry, and the rest which we have already expounded above. Moreover, why the Apostle, writing to the Romans, made use rather of the testimony of the LXX, But the just shall live by my faith, and not of what is found in the Hebrew, the reason is plain. For he was writing to Romans, who did not know the Hebrew Scriptures; nor was he concerned about the words, since the sense was safe, and the present argument suffered no harm from it. Otherwise, wherever the sense is different, and it is written one way in the Hebrew and another in the LXX, note that he uses those testimonies which he had learned from Gamaliel, the teacher of the law.

For although very many think it is to be understood thus, if the Jews are silent, the multitude of the nations will confess me, yet this is the more expressive and truer understanding: even if men were silent, and the envious tongue would not speak of the multitude of my signs, yet the stones themselves, and the foundations of the walls, and the structure of the walls will be able to proclaim aloud my greatness. And to make this more significant, let us set down examples from secular literature too. Crispus says in his Histories: "The Saguntines, renowned for loyalty and sufferings beyond mortals, with greater zeal than resources; for among them even then the half-ruined walls, the unroofed houses, and the scorched walls of the temples displayed the Punic hands". Something similar Tullius too says to Caesar for Marcellus: "The walls, so help me heaven — as it seems to me — of this senate house long to give you thanks, because in a short time that authority will be in these seats of its ancestors and its own". Moreover, where we have translated: And the timber that is between the joints of the buildings will answer, for which the Seventy put: And the beetle from the wood will speak these things, Symmachus, after his manner, translated more plainly, καὶ συνδεσμὸς οἰκοδομῆς ξύλινος ἀποφθέγξεται αὐτὰ, that is: And the wooden joint of the building will speak these things. Theodotion too, καὶ συνδεσμὸς ξύλου φθέγξεται αὐτά, and also the Fifth Edition, καὶ συνδεσμὸς ξύλου φθέγξεται αὐτά, and these translations also agree with that of Symmachus and with ours. For what is called chaphis in the Hebrew language signifies the timber that is set in the middle of a structure to hold the walls together, and among the Greeks it is commonly called ἱμάντωσις (a tie-beam). This, therefore, is what the prophetic discourse signifies according to the history: the stones of the walls which were destroyed by you, and their burnt timbers, will sound forth your cruelty. Besides the five editions, namely of Aquila, Symmachus, the Seventy, Theodotion, and the Fifth, I found in the twelve prophets two other editions as well, in one of which is written: For the stone will cry out from the wall like a worm speaking in the wood, and in the other: For the stone will shout from the wall, and the σκώληξ from the wood will speak these things. But Aquila too put something other than we said, καὶ μάζα, that is, and a lump from the wood will answer. What their translation means we shall say in the exposition of the Seventy translators, in whom O is put for woe, and the discourse is directed either to the devil or to the Antichrist, or to the heretics, who multiply an evil avarice for themselves. Now avarice is called evil to distinguish it from good avarice, so that there is a good avarice of the churchly teacher, who is never sated with the multitude of his followers, and the more disciples he has, the more he is roused to zeal for teaching. Woe, therefore, to him who multiplies for himself a most wicked avarice, so as to gather perverse conventicles for his house, and to set his nest on high, so as to be rescued from the hand of evils. For the devil and the Antichrist and the heretics promise that those who accept their doctrine will be in the heavenly places and will possess the kingdoms of heaven, and will not feel the fires of Gehenna. And though they have promised these things, their counsel will not be able to come to completion; but it will be a counsel of confusion and ignominy for their house, after the very end of things has shown to be false what had been promised, when it is proved to be a counsel of confusion, not of salvation. This teacher of perversity, as we have said, has consumed many peoples, and the more he had in his company, the more he sinned against his own soul. Then the stones of his church, and the κάνθαρος, that is, the beetle from the wood, will cry out against the avarice of the proud one, because he has deceived all nations by his persuasion. By the stones we can understand the dull hearts of those who believe the doctrines of the heretics, and by the beetle from the wood, all perverse masters who, taking up the preaching of the cross for the sake of shameful gain, speak from the belly. For their god is their belly, and they do all things for the sake of food, which is turned into dung — for the beetle or cantharus is a dung worm —, and they take up the cross only for this, that with viperous mouth they may teach the avarice and pride of their master the devil. If ever you see one of the heretics speaking against the Church as if of hidden and secret mysteries, and preferring the house of the devil to the house of Christ, say: The stone cries out from the wall, and the cantharus speaks from the wood. I have read in someone's volume that the cantharus is understood of the heretics for this reason, that they hold doctrines to be compared with dung. Hence the Apostle too says that he counted the error of the old teaching as scybala, that is, as dung. Not that the old law, as the Manichaeans think, is reckoned as scybala in comparison with the Gospel — which it is impious to say, since both Testaments belong to the one God —, but that the teachings of the Pharisees, and the precepts of men, and the Deuterosis of the Jews are called dung by the Apostle. I know that one of the brethren understood the stone that cried out from the wall as the Lord Savior, and the beetle speaking from the wood as the thief who blasphemed the Lord; and although this can be understood piously, yet I do not find how it can be fitted to the whole context of the prophecy. There are some who think that the cantharus speaking from the wood can also be referred to the person of the Savior, which is shown to be impious by the very order of the discourse. For the cantharus from the wood will speak these things is understood not in a good but in a bad sense, that is, it will speak of the most wicked avarice of him who multiplies against his own house, and of the confusion of the devil, and the rest that went before concerning his iniquity and crime. As for what Aquila says, and a lump from the wood will answer, we refer the lump to that sense which the Lord set down in the Gospel: Beware of the leaven of the Pharisees. And when the apostles were in doubt and could not know what it was, the evangelist interpreted it, saying: Now he had spoken to them of the teaching of the Pharisees. Rightly, therefore, does the teaching of the heretics speak from the wood; for they cannot persuade otherwise unless they hold out the glory of the wood in front of their perversity. And also concerning what we had said: For the stone will cry out from the wall like a worm speaking in the wood, or the stone will shout from the wall, and the σκώληξ from the wood will speak these things, some of our people say that the worm speaking in the wood is he who says in the psalm: But I am a worm and no man; and they refer the singing bird to the person of the same one, who says: I have become like a sparrow alone on the housetop, and other things like these.

He is filled with shame instead of glory; drink, you also, and fall into a stupor. The cup of the right hand of the Lord will surround you, and the vomit of shame will be upon your glory, for the iniquity of Lebanon will cover you, and the devastation of the cattle will terrify them, because of the blood of men and the iniquity of the land, and of the city and of all who dwell in it. LXX: Woe to him who offers drink to his neighbor with a turbid overturning, and makes him drunk, that he may look into their caves; a fill of shame from glory drink, you also, and be shaken. The cup of the right hand of the Lord has surrounded you, and shame has been gathered upon your glory, for the impiety of Lebanon will cover you, and the misery of the beasts will terrify you, because of the blood of men, and the impieties of the land and of the city, and of all who dwell in it. For "with a turbid overturning", Symmachus translated: Καὶ ἀφιὼν ἀκρίτως τὸν θυμὸν ἑαυτοῦ, that is: And sending forth his fury without judgment. Theodotion: Ἀπὸ χύσεώς σου, which means: From your pouring out. The Fifth Edition: Ἐξ ἀπροσδοκήτου ἀνατροπῆς τῆς ὀργῆς σου, which signifies: From the unexpected overturning of your wrath. Aquila: Ἐξ ἐπιτρίψεως χόλου σου, which we can render: From the sending forth of your fury. In another edition I found: Ὀυαὶ τῷ ποτίζοντι τὸν ἐταῖρον αὐτοῦ ἀέλλην πετομένην, which in our language sounds: Woe to him who gives drink to his friend, a flying whirlwind. But I have also read it translated elsewhere: Woe to him who gives drink to his neighbor ἔκστασιν ὀχλουμένην, that is, turbid madness. I give this so that it may be known with how great a variety the Hebrew word maspha, which the LXX translated overturning, disagrees among all the editions. Therefore the invective is still against Nebuchadnezzar, because, forgetful of his condition, and as if not knowing that he was a man, he offered gall and bitterness to drink to another man. But we can understand him either as the king of Judea, or, generally, as all men, in that he made them drunk with evils, so that he might see the nakedness of Zedekiah and of all the captives. For this Symmachus and the Fifth Edition translated: That he might see their shames. Now these things are said through the metaphor of a drunken man, disgraced by nakedness, because Nebuchadnezzar made all men drunk with the cup of his fury, and saw them all stripped and captive, and those who had once been glorious were reduced to the utmost slavery; for this is what he says: He is filled with shame instead of glory, so that we supply "the friend" and "the neighbor", or the partner in the kingdom, who drank your cup, O Nebuchadnezzar. Hence, because you have made very many drunk, you also drink from the cup of the Lord and fall into a stupor; you will be surrounded by the punishments of the right hand of the Lord, and with the shame of your vomit you will discharge all that you had swallowed, and from lofty glory you will be brought down to the utmost evils. For the iniquity of Lebanon will cover you: your pride, he says, and the overthrow of the temple, and the plundering of the sanctuary will lay you waste and devastate you. And because he had once named Mount Lebanon, under the metaphor of the same mountain he compares the victims and the sacrifices, or certainly the multitude of the peoples which was slain in Jerusalem, to animals or beasts, saying: And the devastation of the animals will crush you. But all this you will suffer because you laid waste Judah, overturned the land of promise, and the city of Jerusalem and all its inhabitants. I heard at Lydda one of the Hebrews, who was called a wise man among them and a δευτερώτης (a teacher of the traditions), telling a tale of this sort: Zedekiah, he said, blinded by King Nebuchadnezzar at Riblah, that is, Antioch, and mocked in various ways, was led to Babylon. There, when one day Nebuchadnezzar was celebrating the festivities of a banquet, he ordered a potion to be given to him which, once drained, would loosen the drinker's belly into a flux; and Zedekiah, suddenly brought in before the faces of the banqueters and compelled by the force of his belly, was befouled with excrement; and this, he said, is what Scripture now says here: Woe to him who gives drink to his friend, putting in his gall and making him drunk, that he may look upon his nakedness, and shame instead of glory; namely, that he who had been a most powerful king was brought by him to such disgrace. Hence God also threatens him that he himself will drink a potion of this sort, and suffer everything that Zedekiah suffered. How ridiculous this is, you know without my saying it. For if what follows: Drink, you also, and fall into a stupor, and the cup of the right hand of the Lord will surround you, and the vomit of shame will be upon your glory, they understand not of a cup but of the evils which Nebuchadnezzar is to drink, then that cup too with which drink was offered to Zedekiah must be understood as evils, and not, as they will have it, as a καθαρτικὸν (a purgative). But if they say that it really happened, and that a potion of this sort was mixed, as I related above, then this cup too which Nebuchadnezzar is to drink must be reckoned full of a καθαρτικῷ, so that God Sabaoth and the Lord Almighty, for a great vengeance, offers Nebuchadnezzar a καθαρτικὸν to drink, and makes him befouled with his own excrement. So much against the Jewish tradition. But let us come to the spiritual understanding. Woe to you, devil, or Antichrist, or perverse doctrine of the heretics, who with your teachings and your turbid potion make drunk the peoples of the deceived, and overturn their former faith, giving them a potion not from Siloam, not from the Jordan, not from the springs of Israel, but from the brook Kidron, and from the river of Egypt, of which Jeremiah says: What have you to do with the way of Egypt, that you should drink the water of Gihon? For this in the Hebrew is written sior, that is, turbid and muddy; for although the rivers of Egypt are believed to go out from the paradise of the Scriptures, yet because they are trampled by the feet of Pharaoh, they have lost their splendor, and, defiled by the Egyptian mud, have been turned into torrents, at having escaped which the holy one rejoices, saying: Our soul has passed through the torrent. But if anyone should object the brook Cherith, from which Elijah drank, and another torrent, from which the Lord drank on the way, for so it is written: He will drink from the torrent on the way, this must be said: that whoever is in Egypt and on the way of this age, though he be Moses and Aaron, though he be Jeremiah and Elijah, must nevertheless drink of the temptations of Egypt and of the wilderness; hence also the Word of the Lord, who had assumed flesh for this, that he might drink from the torrent, considering his majesty, said: Father, if it is possible, let this cup pass from me. LXX: What does the graven image profit, since they have graven it; they have formed it a molten thing, a false image? Since the maker trusts in his own fashioning, so as to make dumb idols. In sequence with what precedes, it is said of Nebuchadnezzar that he fashioned a statue of the idol Bel and set it up in the plain of Dura, or, as it is written in the Hebrew, Dora, about which we read more fully in Daniel. And so Scripture marvels at the madness and folly of the king, in that he himself ordered a golden statue to be made, and the maker trusts in the image that he has fashioned; and this we can indeed also take generally against all the worshipers of idols. Nor let us think that what is graven and what is molten are one and the same. For carving we can take as done in stones and in marbles, while casting is understood of those metals which can be melted and cast: for example, gold, silver, bronze, lead and tin. Let this be said so that, according to tropology, we may know what the difference is between a graven thing and a molten thing. We read in Deuteronomy: Cursed is everyone who makes a graven and a molten thing, the work of the hands of the craftsman, and sets it in a hidden place. The graven and the molten thing, I think, are perverse doctrines, which are adored by those by whom they were made. See how Arius carved for himself the idol of a creature, and adored what he carved. Behold how Eunomius cast a false image, and bows his neck to his own casting. And Scripture says pointedly: And he will set it, he says, in a hidden place. For they too have their orgies, and to certain disciples, as if to the perfect, they hand on hidden mysteries, which, if they come forth to the light, are at once convicted of being fictions. Their carving and their casting, therefore, will profit them nothing. Carving, which refers to stones, is understood of those doctrines which display their folly at first sight. Casting is found where there seems to be some reasoning of secular wisdom, and, as if with a kind of gold, so with the disciplines of the philosophers and the splendor of eloquence, the idol has been cast. The fashioned thing, then, will profit its maker nothing. And the dumb and deaf image will not be able to hear its worshiper. If ever you see someone unwilling to believe the truth, and, though the falsity of his doctrines has been shown, persevering in the pursuit he has begun, you will fittingly be able to say: He hopes in his own fashioning, and makes dumb or deaf images; for κωφά among the Greeks signifies both, although Symmachus, translating ἄλαλα, seems to have understood dumb rather than deaf. Nor let anyone be troubled by that ἰδίωμα (idiom) of the Scriptures, of which we have often spoken: Who, do you think, is the faithful and wise steward? And in another place: Who is wise, and will understand these things? In that "who" or "what" is taken for "rare", since we can also take this very expression from another place for "impossible": Who will separate us from the love of Christ: tribulation, or distress, and so on? And from the present chapter: What does the graven image profit, since its maker has graven it? For in both, impossibility is shown: that neither can any tribulation and distress separate the apostle's love from Christ, nor is there any profit in idols.

Woe to him who says to the wood: Wake up; arise, to the silent stone; can it teach? Behold, it is overlaid with gold and silver, and no spirit at all is in its inward parts. But the Lord is in his holy temple; let all the earth keep silence before his face. LXX: Woe to him who says to the wood: Awake and rise up, and to the stone: Be lifted up. And it is an image, and it is a beaten work of gold and silver, and no spirit at all is in it. But the Lord is in his holy temple; let all the earth revere him before his face. This too can likewise be taken either against Nebuchadnezzar or against all who venerate idols. And human error is described, in that men think the silver, gold, gems and silk with which the idols are either wrapped or covered to be gods, because of the brilliance of the material, although the craftsman could indeed give them form, but could not give the soul by which limbs are quickened. And on the contrary the Lord is said to be in his holy temple; not in a temple made by hands, but either in the heavens, or in each of the saints, according to the Apostle, who says: Do you not know that you are the temple of God, and the spirit of God dwells in you? And elsewhere: Your bodies are the temple of God; or in the Son, as he himself says: The Father who abides in me, he does the works. Or certainly according to that saying, that the heavens and the lands, the seas and the whole world:

A spirit within sustains them, and mind, poured through all the limbs,

moves the whole mass, and mingles itself with the great body.

The whole world, which consists of heaven and earth and is enclosed by the circles of the heavens, is said to be the house of God. Hence the Apostle too says confidently: For in him, he says, we live, and move, and are. But if anyone should object, how can it be said that there is no spirit in idols, when unclean spirits attend all images, let him learn the custom of holy Scripture: that a perverse spirit is never called spirit absolutely, but always with some addition, as there: They were led astray by a spirit of fornication. And in the Gospel: But when an unclean spirit has gone out of a man, and other things like these. But wherever spirit is read alone and absolutely, without any addition, it always refers to the good side, that is, to the Holy Spirit, as in that saying of the Apostle: He who sows in the Spirit will reap from the Spirit eternal life, and elsewhere: But the fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, and in another place: Walk by the Spirit, and you will not fulfill the desires of the flesh. Nor do we say this as though the Holy Spirit too were not set down with an addition: for he is called both the Holy Spirit, and the princely spirit, and the right spirit, and the spirit of God, and other things like these; but we say that the Holy Spirit is often set down both with an addition and alone, whereas a perverse spirit has nowhere been read except with an addition. But this too can be said, if anyone contentiously wishes to take a perverse spirit in this place: that it is one thing to say: No spirit at all is in it, and another if he had said: No spirit at all is around it; for a spirit can attend images, but it cannot be within them. Hence Aquila too rendered the Hebrew more expressively, saying: And its spirit is not in its inward parts, or in its midst. Moreover, it should be known that in certain Hebrew volumes "all" is not added, but spirit is read absolutely. But if anyone, overcome by reason, takes spirit in the good sense, and asks why, if it is said of the Holy Spirit, it is read with an addition: And no spirit at all is in it, let him know that by "every spirit" the various graces of the Holy Spirit are understood; so that the sense is: It will be able to have in itself nothing of grace, nothing of power. And this indeed is better understood according to tropology: that in all the idols of the heretics and the fashionings of the devil there is no grace of the Holy Spirit, but they seem to display an image of divinity and a beauty of doctrines, while in them there is nothing breathing and living. Let us say this too, lest we seem to have hidden from the reader what we know: that spirit and wind are called among the Hebrews by the same word, that is, rua, and that according to the sense of the passage it is customarily taken either as spirit or as wind. Therefore in this place too we can take spirit either for wind, in that nothing breathes in idols, or for the soul, in that the images are animated. And that spirit is taken for the soul the Savior's prayer clearly signifies: Father, into your hands I commend my spirit. For Jesus could not commend to the Father either a perverse spirit, which it is impious even to think, or the Holy Spirit, who is himself also God, rather than his own soul, of which he had said: My soul is sorrowful even to death, and: No one can take my soul from me, but by my will I lay down my soul, and of my own accord I take it up again.

Book Two

We are writing a second book on Habakkuk, my Chromatius, venerable father, dedicating a work of its own to his canticle, and setting about with all our strength a discourse composed in the epic manner and in that of the psalter, that is, in the lyric manner. Let the water-snake hiss, then, and let Sardanapalus insult us, fouler in his vices than in his name; let us pursue the journey we have begun, and, by your prayers, you who have overcome the flesh by virtue, let us expound the most manifest prophecy concerning Christ in the eighth prophet, that is, in the number of the Lord's resurrection.

3:1

A prayer of Habakkuk the prophet for ignorances. LXX: A prayer of Habakkuk the prophet with a song. Aquila and Symmachus and the Fifth Edition translated, as we did, for ignorances; Theodotion alone ὑπὲρ τῶν ἐκουσιασμῶν, that is, for voluntary sins, and for those who sin of their own accord. This is said so that we may understand — the Seventy excepted — that no one translated a prayer with a song. For in the Hebrew too it has al segionoth, which is rendered Ἐπὶ ἀγνοημάτων, and we translated for ignorances. Now the sense is this: earlier he had rashly said: How long, O Lord, shall I cry, and you will not hear? Shall I cry out to you, suffering violence, and you will not save? And then, in the second complaint: Why do you not look upon those who act unjustly, and keep silent, while the wicked devours one more just than himself? To this he had heard: Write the vision, and make it plain upon tablets. And after other things: Behold, he who is unbelieving, his soul will not be upright in himself; but the just shall live in his faith. And he had learned that either Nebuchadnezzar, or the devil, or the Antichrist had been set for the judgment of sinners, and strong to chastise the nations; now he does penance, and laments that he spoke rashly, and asks pardon, so that he may obtain mercy, because he acted in ignorance. Hence David too says: The sins, he says, of my youth, and my ignorances, do not remember. There are some who think that it is a prayer of the prophet that at the coming of Christ the error of men may be taken away, and that for this reason the name of prophet is set at the head of the title, because by the prophetic spirit he prays that the darkness be removed and the light restored, the image taken away and the truth bestowed. But that it is a prophecy, the whole fabric of the canticle shows, and above all this: In the midst of two living creatures you will be known, and: When the years draw near, you will be known, and, concerning the judgment: When my soul is troubled in wrath, you will remember mercy, and again, concerning the coming of Christ: God will come from Teman, and the Holy One from the shady and thick mountain, and also concerning the future: The tents of the land of Midian will tremble, and you will mount upon your horses, and stretching you will stretch your bow over kingdoms. But if anyone asks why Theodotion alone translated for spontaneous sins, we can say that it shows either the confession of the prophet, that he sinned not under some necessity but of his own accord, judging wrongly of the judgment of God, or certainly the future faith of the nations, in that, voluntarily abandoning their former error, they were going to believe in him who is promised in the canticle. We read in Psalm 16 (17): A prayer of David. And in another likewise. A prayer of David. And in Psalm 89 (90): A prayer of Moses, the man of God. And in Psalm 101 (102): A prayer of the poor man when he was in anguish, and poured out his petition before the Lord. And wherever else the name of prayer is brought in, yet in no place is it read with a song. And I do not know whether it is fitting to pray with a song, unless perhaps, following the Seventy, we say that the prophet prays for the coming of Christ, and prophesies this very thing with delight, and with psalms and song, so that in that it is a prayer he may entreat the Father, and in that it is a song, praise may be spoken of the Father, who sent the Son, and of the Son, who came. So much on the title of the canticle. Let us now see what is said in the canticle.

3:2

O Lord, I have heard your hearing, and I was afraid. O Lord, your work, in the midst of the years, revive it. LXX: O Lord, I have heard your report, and I was afraid. O Lord, I considered your works, and I was astonished; in the midst of two living creatures you will be known. Where we and Aquila and Theodotion translated revive it, Symmachus rendered revive it again. But what the Seventy said, I considered and I was astonished, is found neither in the Hebrew nor in any of the other translators, so that, when the things not found in the Hebrew are removed, one can read according to the Seventy: O Lord, your works in the midst of two living creatures you will be known; and because this seemed ἀδιανόητον (unintelligible), the preceding words were joined to it. Now we read in the Hebrew adonai, that is, O Lord, phalach, your work, bacereb, in the midst, sanim, of years heieu, revive it. This is said so that we may recognize that what is superfluous in the Seventy has plainly been added. The Hebrews explain this place according to the history thus: O Lord, I have heard your hearing, and I was afraid. I have heard, he says, the punishments which you have prepared for Nebuchadnezzar and for the devil, in which you said to him: Woe to him who multiplies what is not his. And secondly: Woe to him who gathers evil avarice for his house. And thirdly: Woe to him who builds a city in bloodshed, and prepares a town in iniquity. And fourthly: Woe to him who gives drink to his friend, putting in his gall and making him drunk. And fifthly: Woe to him who says to the wood: Awake; arise, to the silent stone. And just as I was terrified with fear that the great dragon is to be pierced with so many wounds, so I pray, O Lord, that you fulfil what you have promised, and, when the time is completed, render your Christ. For you said that the vision is still far off, and will appear at the end, and will not lie. Revive, therefore, what you have promised, that is, fulfil your promise; let your word not die unfulfilled, but be accomplished in deed. This indeed, according to our version, can also be understood of the resurrection of the Savior, that he who died for us may rise from the dead and be brought to life. But according to the Seventy the sense is far different, and we ought also to set down the explanation of the common edition. O Lord, I have heard your word in the Scriptures, and since you gave me an ear, according to what Isaiah says: He gave me in addition an ear to hear, I heard in such a way as you wish your word to be heard. And having contemplated your works more diligently, lest it be said to me: But the works of the Lord they do not regard, and the works of his hands they do not consider, I understood the Creator from the creatures; and at each of the things which you have made, and which you work daily in the whole world, I was wholly astonished, and having lost the sense of human nature, I was turned into a holy madness. Or certainly, troubled with wonder, I break out trembling into your praises, saying: In the midst of two living creatures you will be known. Many think this is to be understood of the Son and the Holy Spirit, because the Father is understood through the Son and the Spirit. These indeed they interpret as both the two Seraphim in Isaiah and the two Cherubim which are written of in Exodus, which look toward each other and have the oracle in the midst; and that in Isaiah, veiling the head and the feet of the Lord, they fly in the present age only, and cry out to one another the mystery of the Trinity, and that one of the Seraphim, which means burning, is sent and comes to the earth and cleanses the prophet's lips, and says: I have come to send fire upon the earth, and how I wish that it may burn. So others judge, and for this interpretation they use many testimonies of the Scriptures. Moreover, the simple interpretation and the opinion of the common people understand it of the Savior, in that he was recognized when crucified between two thieves. But those who interpret better say this: that in the first Church, which was gathered from the circumcision and from the foreskin, with the two peoples surrounding him on this side and on that, the Savior was understood and believed. There are some who understand the two living creatures as the two testaments, the new and the old, which are truly living beings, which are full of life, which breathe, and in whose midst the Lord is known.

In the midst of the years you will make it known. LXX: When the years draw near, you will be known. When the time comes, he says, and you have fulfilled your promises in deed, you will show that what you promised is true. Or else: when the consummation draws near, and at the last hour your Son comes to destroy sins, you will be known more manifestly. There follows:

When the time comes, you will be shown, when my soul is troubled. This, apart from the Seventy, is found neither in the Hebrew nor in any of the other translators. And the sense is: When the time comes of which it is said: In an acceptable time I have heard you, the time of the coming of the Lord Jesus Christ, then, O God the Father, your name will be known, which before was hidden from men, of which the Lord says in the Gospel: Father, I have revealed your name to men. But what is added: When my soul is troubled, in the edition of the LXX translators is joined with what follows, so that it can be read: When my soul is troubled in wrath, and there the division falls; next is added: You will remember mercy, meaning that for the prophet trouble alone is enough as punishment, and that, his soul being troubled at the wrath of God, no penalty at all is inflicted, but mercy shuts out wrath. Now the wrath of God too has its measures: how much, and for how long, and for what causes, and on whom it is inflicted, according to what is written: You will feed us with the bread of tears, and give us tears to drink in measure. But if the prophet is troubled at the wrath of God, and he who is troubled obtains mercy, what ought we to hope, or rather to fear, all of whose works are worthy of the wrath of God? But as for what follows according to the Hebrew.

When you are angry, you will remember mercy, we must not think that God forgets and after his wrath remembers his mercy, but that we, set in punishment, think he forgets, according to this: How long, O Lord, will you forget me, to the end? For whenever we too are covered by temptations as by waves, and the swift storm of the demons rages against us, we speak as to one asleep: Arise, why do you sleep, O Lord? And at the same time observe the clemency of God: he did not say: When you inflict punishments, you will remember mercy, but: When you are angry. Now he who is angry sometimes does not strike, but only threatens. The Apostle too, perceiving this, says: For the wrath of God is revealed from heaven against all ungodliness and iniquity of men. But where it is revealed, it is not inflicted, it does not strike, but it is revealed to terrify, and so that it may not be inflicted on those who are terrified.

3:3a

God will come from the south, and the Holy One from Mount Paran. Always. LXX: God will come from Teman, and the Holy One from the shady and thick mountain. Diapsalma. Aquila and Symmachus and the Fifth Edition set down the Hebrew word itself, Teman; Theodotion alone translated what Teman means, saying: Eloim will come from the south, and the Holy One from Mount Paran, to the end. From this we understand that From the shady and thick mountain was set down by the Seventy alone. But also what the Seventy translated διάψαλμα, and we rendered always, Symmachus translated: For ever; Theodotion: To the end; the Fifth Edition the Hebrew word itself: sela. God, therefore, will come from the south, that is, from the midday, from the clear light, and from those who are called sons of the days. Hence in the Song of Songs too the bridegroom drives away the north wind and calls the south wind, saying: Arise, north wind, and come, south wind, blow through my garden, and let my spices flow. God is always at midday: Where, she says, do you pasture? Where do you lie down? At midday. And to Abraham, when he was under the oak, God came only at midday. And Joseph, who went before as a type of the Savior, makes a banquet for his brothers at midday. The knowledge of God the Father, therefore, comes to those who are worthy in full light. And the knowledge of the Holy One, that is, of the Son of God, comes from the shady and thick mountain. The shady and thick mountain is understood either as the Father himself, full of powers and of all wisdom, protecting all things with his majesty, and stretching out his wings, and cherishing his young; or certainly as paradise and the heavenly places, full of angels, full of powers, full of most fruitful trees. And would that it might befall me too that, at my voice and my exposition, God may come in clear light, and his Son, of whom it is written: Be holy, for I am holy; that from the loftiness of a discourse shady and thick, and woven together on this side and that from the testimonies of the Scriptures, with the Father and the Son coming together, the hearer may become their dwelling, and the Scripture be fulfilled which says: I and my Father will come to him, and will make our abode with him. But since in place of the shady and thick mountain it is written in the Hebrew Mount Paran, and Paran means the mouth of one seeing, then beautifully, according to our interpretation, the knowledge of the Son comes from the discourse of a learned man, and not from any discourse whatever, but from one which is full of light, full of eyes, so that it may be carried, clear and pure, to the ears of the hearers. And as for his saying from the mountain, understand lofty doctrines. I have heard a Hebrew discuss this place thus: that Bethlehem, in which our Lord and Savior was born, lies to the south, and that he is the one of whom it is now said: The Lord will come from the south, that is, he will be born in Bethlehem and will rise up from there. And because he who was born in Bethlehem once gave the law on Mount Sinai, he is the Holy One who comes from Mount Paran. For Paran is a place near Mount Sinai. And what is added, diapsalma, that is, always, has this sense: He who was born in Bethlehem, and who gave the law on Sinai, that is, on Mount Paran, is always, in all benefits, past and present and future, both their author and their giver. Of the diapsalma, which in Hebrew is called sela, there has been fuller discussion on the psalter. And at the same time observe that, according to the Seventy, diapsalma is not set down except in the psalter and in the present place. From this we understand that the song of the prayer was rightly noted in its title by the Seventy.

3:3b-4

His glory covered the heavens, and the earth is full of his praise.

His brightness will be as light, horns in his hands. There his strength is hidden. LXX: His power covered the heavens, and the earth is full of his praise, and his brightness will be like light; horns in his hands, and he placed a mighty love of his strength. Where the Seventy translated: And he placed a mighty love of his strength, and we said: There his strength is hidden, Aquila translated: And he placed the hiding of his strength; Symmachus: And he placed his strength hidden; Theodotion alone, agreeing with our translation, says: And there is the hiding of his strength. For the word sam, according to the character of the place, is understood both as he placed and as there, and in the present place there should be read rather than he placed, so that the sense and order of the sentence is: Horns in his hands, and let there be understood, there, that is, in the horns his strength is hidden. Now it is manifest according to the Hebrew that at the coming of Christ all things were filled with glory, according to what is said in the Gospel: Glory to God in the highest, and on earth peace to men of good will. And elsewhere: He made peace in the heavens and on earth through the blood of the cross, and sat at the right hand of the majesty: For his word runs swiftly. And elsewhere: O Lord, our Lord, how wonderful is your name in all the earth. And again in Psalm 18 (19): Their sound has gone out into all the earth, and their words to the ends of the world. His brightness also, as the sun of justice, shone forth with clear light, and horns are in his hands, the standards and trophies of the cross, and in those very horns his strength is hidden: For though he was in the form of God, he did not think it robbery to be equal to God, but emptied himself, taking the form of a servant. He became obedient to the Father unto death, even the death of the cross. On the cross, therefore, his strength was for a little while hidden, when he said to the Father: My soul is sorrowful even unto death. And: Father, if it is possible, let this cup pass from me. And on the cross itself: Father, into your hands I commend my spirit. Moreover, according to the Seventy, in what is said: His power covered the heavens, we ought to know that what is covered is less than what covers it: provided, however, that what is covered is covered wholly and not in part. Since, therefore, the power of God covers the heavens, his power, by which the heavens are covered, is greater than the heavens. And we often read that the heavens are those who bear the image of the one above the heavens, and who tell the glory of God. That the Lord, the Savior, is also the Power of God, the Apostle proves: Christ the power of God, and the wisdom of God. This power is, as it were, the mother of all the particular virtues; for example, he is called power, wisdom, strength, justice, temperance, truth, holiness, redemption. But Christ was made for us by God wisdom, and justice, and sanctification, and redemption. These particular virtues, therefore, in which Christ is shown — according to the progress of those who receive him either as wisdom, or as strength, or as justice, and the other things of this kind —, are contained in the general power of God, that is, in the Lord, the Savior; and in this way we understand it of the earth too: that those who at first, because of the image of the earthly one, were called earth, and to whom it was said: You are earth, and to earth you shall go, are filled at the coming of the Savior with the praise of the Lord. And when both the heavens have been covered by the power of God — that is, protected and clothed on every side —, and the whole earth has been filled with the praise of God, then his brightness will be as light. Now the Apostle is not silent that God the Savior is the image of God and the brightness of his glory. He, after he appeared to us as the brightness of the glory of God, returned to his former majesty. For even if we had known Christ according to the flesh, yet now we no longer know him according to the flesh, but according to the spirit, because: What was made, in him was life, and the life was the light of men. The Savior too shows this more manifestly in the Gospel, saying: Father, glorify me with the glory which I had with you before the world was made, so that after the ascension to the heavens the brightness may be what the light is, that is, that the Son may begin to be what the Father is. And as for what follows: Horns in his hands, it is the custom of the Scriptures always to put horns for kingdoms. For that too which Hannah says in the first book of Kingdoms (1 Samuel): He exalted the horn of his anointed, signifies the magnificence of the kingdom of the Savior. And in Daniel the ten horns show ten kingdoms. And it is now said: Horns in his hands, in the same way as we read written elsewhere: The heart of the king is in the hand of God, meaning that the mind and the ruling part of the heart of a holy man — who is going on to the kingdom of heaven, who while still placed on earth reigns over his body without sins — does not wander outside, but is set in the keeping of God. But since both in the Hebrew and in the other editions it is not written: Horns in his hands, but in his hand, which is said iado, let us understand the strong and mighty hand of God to be his Son. And let us say that in this hand are placed all the kingdoms of heaven, and of those who strive to ascend to heaven, as Isaiah too sounds, saying: A vineyard was made for the beloved on a horn, in a fertile place, meaning in a kingdom. For this reason, I think, no horned animal is placed among the unclean in Leviticus, and the unicorn in the psalms, or ῥινοκερότα (rhinoceros), signifies this same thing, and this too: In you we will toss our enemies with the horn. And as for what we read according to the LXX: And he placed a strong love of his power, this too is to be understood of Christ: that God the Father covered the heavens with his power, and filled the earth with praise, and made his brightness to be as light, and placed the kingdom in the hand of his Son, for this reason, that he might make his beloved be loved by men, and loved not lightly, but vehemently and strongly, so that no one might take out of his hand those who had loved him strongly and clung in his love. On the contrary, the devil makes us love the world, and in place of the love of virtue love vices, and not lightly, but strongly, so that it can be said of us: And the devil placed a strong love of his vices.

3:5

Before his face death will go, and the devil will go forth before his feet. LXX: Before his face the word will go, and it will go forth into the field after his feet. Where we translated death, in the Hebrew three letters are set down, daleth, beth, res, without any vowel; if they are read dabar, they signify word; if deber, pestilence, which in Greek is called λοιμός. Accordingly Aquila too translated thus: Before his face pestilence will go; Symmachus: Before his face death will go before; the Fifth Edition: Before his face death will walk; the LXX and Theodotion alone translated word in place of death. So too in the following verse, where we said: The devil will go forth before his feet, and the LXX translated otherwise, following whom we shall discuss it afterwards, Aquila translated, in place of devil, a winged thing; but Symmachus and Theodotion and the Sixth Edition, a bird, which in Hebrew is called Reseph. Now the Hebrews hand down that, just as in the Gospel the prince of demons is said to be Beelzebub, so Reseph is the name of a demon who holds the chief place among the others, and because of his excessive swiftness and his running about in different directions he is called a bird and a winged thing; and that he is the one who in paradise spoke to the woman under the figure of a serpent, and received his name from the curse by which he was condemned by God, since Reseph means creeping on the belly. This, therefore, is what is said: As soon as the Lord has come and has been baptized in the Jordan, and at the descent of the dove the voice of the Father has thundered: This is my beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased, the devil will meet him as he comes out of the waters, and before his feet will stand death and the ancient serpent, who tempted him for forty days in the wilderness. But if we read according to the LXX: Before his face the word will go, and it will go forth into the fields after his feet, this signifies that the word of God goes before his visitation, which is now allegorically called his face, and prepares the hearts of believers, so that it may make the crooked straight and level the uneven into a plain, and the soul of the hearer, like a ploughed field, may be able to receive the spiritual seed.

3:6

He stood and measured the earth; he looked, and dissolved the nations, and the mountains of the age were crushed; the hills of the world were bowed down, by the journeys of his eternity. LXX: He stood, and the earth was shaken; he looked, and the nations wasted away; the mountains were crushed by violence, the hills of the age of his everlasting journey melted away. The Savior, standing and surveying all things, and measuring with his eye the whole of the world, scattered the multitude of the nations; and when these were scattered and dissolved, the mountains of this age were crushed, and the hills of this world were bowed down. For there are also other mountains and hills, which the bridegroom leaps and bounds over in the Song of Songs, of which it is said also in the second Psalm of Ascents: I lifted up my eyes to the mountains, from whence help will come to me. But the mountains of the age are the very ones that are also the dark mountains, concerning which Jeremiah commanded that our feet not strike against them. These are the hills on which Saul reigned when he was killing the priests of God: for Gibeah means hill. And elegantly, he says, the hills of the world were bowed down. For before the coming of the Savior they walked with neck erect, and no one could humble their pride. And they were crushed and bowed down by the journeys of his eternity, that is, of God's, because his eternity deigned to come to us; or else because always, from the beginning of the world up to his incarnation, he came to the saints, and the word of God came to be in the hand of each one, and in all who conquered he himself prevailed, and his eternal journey bowed down and crushed the hills and the mountains. Let these things be said by way of metaphor according to the Hebrew. Moreover, according to the LXX, after the word has gone before the face of God and has gone forth into the plains, God the Father comes there, where a region has been prepared for him by his word, and he comes after the feet of his word and stands; never going before, but always waiting for the word to pave the way for him. And where he has stood beside the feet of his word, there at once the earth, that is, the works of the flesh and of bodies, which cannot endure the presence of God, are shaken. And when they have been shaken, the power of the word and the presence of God looks upon all the nations of the soul, which we can understand as the thoughts and the manifold opinions pulling in different directions, which are at once dissolved and waste away. If anything too had exalted itself on the earth against the knowledge of God, and had taken possession of the mind of the hearer, it will be broken and crushed, as the word goes before and God comes. And when the mountains have been broken and crushed at the sight of God, the hills will clearly be consumed and brought to nothing. For they are not the mountains of God, but the mountains of the age. For the everlasting journey of God, looking to the things which his word goes before, and being stronger than the hills of the age, will consume and destroy them. Now the mountains can also be understood as the demons who dwell in the heretics and lift themselves up against the knowledge of God; and the hills as the other powers of the demons, which make men admire the beauty of bodies, ranks, riches, nobility of birth, and the other goods of the world. One may see, after the coming of the word of God and the presence of God the Father, how human souls are shaken, and all that is earthly is dissolved, and former thoughts are brought to nothing. Then the demons are destroyed, then the heights of the age are brought down to nothing, and all the knowledge of the heretics, which at first was swelling, is humbled, crushed and consumed at the coming of the word of God. And what before seemed beautiful and very great is cast away as despised and small. And this happens because of the coming of God and the lodging of Christ, according to what is written elsewhere: I will dwell in them, and walk among them, and I will be their God, and they shall be my people.

3:7

For iniquity I saw the tents of Ethiopia; the skins of the land of Midian will be troubled. LXX: For labors I saw the tabernacles of the Ethiopians; the tabernacles of the land of Midian also will tremble. The Ethiopians, foul and lovers of darkness, and strangers to all light, who feed on the flesh of the dragon, of which it is written: You gave him as food to the peoples of the Ethiopians, are understood as the demons, whose tabernacle becomes whoever in this age has labored for honors and riches; and this is significantly shown under the single word of iniquity: for every rich man is either unjust or the heir of an unjust man. See men cross the seas, keep watch before the doors of the powerful, suffer everything that the condition of slaves scarcely suffers, in order to gather riches, to obtain some rank, and, once they have achieved this, give themselves over to luxury and pleasures and every iniquity, so that what avarice has gathered, luxury may consume. These, therefore, for their labors are made a lodging of demons, and those who ought to have been the temple of God become the tabernacle of the Ethiopians. But also in what follows: The skins of the land of Midian will be troubled, or the tabernacles of the land of Midian also will tremble, understand the same people as the tabernacles of the Ethiopians and the tabernacles of the land of Midian. For after they have grown rich, and by right and wrong have climbed to the highest rank, then, from the consciousness of their sins, they will always dread death, always dread judgment, and at a slight fever, like robbers in prison, so they will sigh over the eternal punishments. Now Midian in our tongue sounds "from judgment", that is, condemnation; and it is shown that through fear of judgment and of eternal penalties they are always in dread, and that the torments which they feel they deserve, they endure in daily terror.

3:8-9

Were you angry with the rivers, O Lord, or was your fury against the rivers, or your indignation against the sea? For you will mount upon your horses, and your chariots are salvation. Rousing, you will rouse your bow, the oaths to the tribes which you have spoken. Always. LXX: Were you angry with the rivers, O Lord, or was your fury against the rivers, or your onrush against the sea? You who mount upon your horses, and your riding is salvation, bending, stretching your bow against the scepters, says the Lord. Diapsalma. Where the Seventy have diapsalma and Aquila always, the others translated likewise as above. And since my discourse hastens on to the tropological interpretation, I shall briefly take in the sense of the passage according to the letter and proceed to the rest. Just as you dried up the Jordan and the Red Sea, fighting for us — for you were not angry with the rivers and the sea, nor could senseless things incur any offense —, so now, mounting your chariots and seizing your bow, you will give salvation to your people, and the oaths which you swore to our fathers and to the tribes you will fulfill forever. But as for what he says: Were you angry with the rivers, O Lord, or was your fury against the rivers, or your onrush against the sea? He says it ambiguously, and with the speech of one asking rather than of one affirming. For there are both good rivers and bad ones. There is a very evil sea, and there is a very good sea. Of good rivers this is an example: The rush of the river makes glad the city of God. And that whoever drinks of the water of the Lord, from his belly will flow rivers of water springing up into eternal life. Of bad ones, that which Pharaoh says in Ezekiel: The rivers are mine, and I made them, in which the dragon dwells, and many things like these. Let rottenness enter into my bones, and swarm beneath me, that is, I gladly suffer what Job suffered: I long that not only my flesh but the marrow of my bones too should waste away, and that my bed should swarm with the rottenness of my body and with countless worms, so that after I have endured these things here for my sins, I may rest in the bitter day, in the day of tribulation, in the day of necessity and distress. And I shall go up to our girded people, that is, strong, warlike and combative. And beautifully, I shall go up: for one always goes up to a girded people. And elegantly, our: for he who has been afflicted, and has gladly endured pressures, and has set future rewards against present evils, boldly says our, so that, like Abraham, Isaac and Jacob, he too may sleep in a good old age, full of days, and be gathered to his fathers. But if anyone should say: Look, in expounding the history you have, without knowing it, been shut up in the nets of allegory, and you have mixed tropology with the history, let him hear that a metaphor in the history does not always amount to allegory, because the history itself is frequently woven metaphorically, and under the image of a woman or of one man something is proclaimed about a whole people. And now therefore we can say in the person of the people: I gladly undergo captivity, I endure distresses with an even mind, and I am pressed by the most heavy yoke of the Babylonians; and whatever belongs to the last and harsh necessity I shall suffer rejoicing, provided only that I may rest at the time when you will curse the scepters of the wicked one, and your horses will trample the mud of many waters, so that afterward, with your holy ones Zerubbabel and Joshua son of Jehozadak, and Ezra the priest, and Nehemiah, I may return to the land of promise. Up to this point, so that we might not seem to pass over the history altogether, we have in some measure done violence to the understanding, and we dragged into the captivity sentences that do not follow; now let us return to the Seventy translators and to the tropological exposition.

Just as Christ is the head of the Church and of every man, so of all the demons who rage in this world the head is Beelzebub, the prince of the demons, and each of their troops has its own heads and princes. For example, the spirits of fornications have their own commander, the spirits of avarice have their own ἄρχοντα (ruler), the spirits of vainglory, the spirits of lying, the spirits of unbelief have the presidents of their warfare. Therefore the most merciful God, who had sent death upon the heads of the wicked, who had raised up bonds up to the neck, at the end divides also the heads of the mighty in amazement, so as first to separate the princes from their subjects, and as it were to behead the body from its head, and that where there had been a most evil head, there the best head may be set in its place. Let us give an example, so that what we say may become clearer: whenever a tyrant is cut down, his images and statues are also taken down, and, only the face being changed and the head removed, the face of the one who conquered is set on top, so that, while the body remains and the heads are cut off, another head is substituted. I like to understand this of the conventicles of the heretics: that when the heads of the heresies have been separated from the rest of the peoples, Christ begins to be the head in their place. And at the same time consider the expressiveness of holy Scripture, that it did not say, you cut off, or you cut down the heads of the mighty, but you divided; for what is divided is not so much amputated and destroyed as separated into parts. So that, just as at the building of the tower the tongue which had been wrongly united was separated, and a most evil pact was cut asunder by a useful division, so also these heads, which together with their bodies seemed to have agreement among themselves — for the heads of the heretics are many, and though they have different eyes, they yet bark against the Church with one, so to speak, tongue of blasphemy —, might be divided into parts, and, separated from the deceived bodies, might make room for the good head. We can use this little verse whenever we see their kings and leaders shed Christian blood, and afterward the vengeance of the Lord follow, as we lately saw in Julian, and before him in Maximian, and earlier in Valerian, Decius, Domitian, Nero, and say to the Lord in exultation and prayer with a song: You divided in amazement the heads of the mighty, that is, in the amazement of believers, or in the amazement of all the nations, who did not think they could fall so quickly. While I was still a boy and was being trained in the school of grammar, and all the cities were polluted with the blood of victims, and suddenly, in the very heat of the persecution, the death of Julian was announced, one of the pagans said elegantly: How, he says, do the Christians say that their God is patient and ἀνεξίκακον (forbearing)? Nothing is more wrathful, nothing more prompt than this fury; he could not put off his indignation even for a short space. He may have said this in jest. But the Church of Christ sang with exultation: You divided in amazement the heads of the mighty. I too shall say something like it: Divide, O Lord, in the amazement of all, Ahab and Jezebel. I am not Elijah, but they are an Ahab and a Jezebel: they have killed Naboth, and taken his vineyard, and made it the garden of their luxury. Let some servant of yours, an Obadiah, be found to feed your poor man and beggar; let the harlot's blood be given to the dogs; let wicked and avaricious Ahab be slain by the javelin of the Lord.

LXX: They will be moved in her, they will open their bridles like a poor man eating in secret. It can also be interpreted otherwise: When the heads have been divided in amazement, as if beheaded from the rest of the body, they will open their mouth, which had been bridled as it were with the bit of condemnation, and in the likeness of those who eat they will clatter with their teeth struck together, wanting to eat again, but not having the power to devour. Understand how after the coming of Christ the heads of the demons, separated from the nations which had formerly been subject to them, wish to exercise their ancient power again. And although he said before: I will ascend above the stars of heaven, I will set my nest on high, and I will hold the whole world in my hand like eggs, yet they will be dragged down from the heights, and, losing their former furniture and all the substance of their house, they will scarcely attempt, like poor men, to eat and bite in secret. LXX: And you brought your horses over into the sea, troubling many waters. After God had sent death upon the heads of the wicked, and had cut down the heads of the mighty in amazement, and had broken them in the sea — for it is written in the psalms: You broke the heads of the dragon —, when the princes have been killed or broken, and the strong man has been conquered, one comes to his house, and all his vessels are plundered. But the vessels, and the house of the strong man, and the furniture of the prince, what else can we interpret them as, except the sea of this world, in which the dragon dwells? God therefore, an excellent rider and an outstanding charioteer, brings his horses, that is, the angels and the lofty powers, over the sea of this world, to trouble the many waters, the demons and the opposing powers. But if we wish to interpret it of the coming of Christ, according to what is written in the Apocalypse, that the Word of God sits on a white horse and the whole army follows him on white horses, we shall see how Christ mounted in his apostles, saying to them: Behold, I am with you all days, even to the consummation of the age, and: Going, go, baptize all nations, and the rest. And afterward he mounted upon one white horse, which I think is none other than the apostle Paul, riding upon whom he went round the whole world. Now the Word of God mounted his horses so that the many waters might be troubled, that is, either the many peoples who were formerly in the sea and were held subject to the dragon — For wide and spacious is the way that leads to death, and so that they might be troubled, first deserting their former error, then, being troubled, might receive the rider as he came —, or certainly, those of whom we spoke above, so that the hosts of demons would no longer rule in the sea as before, but, troubled, would give way and dread the wounds of the fighting rider. And would that the Word of God might mount in me too, and through the lance of my mouth kill him who reigns in many waters, so that at the king's death the waters which had been subject to them, being troubled, might offer their necks to my rider, and that we, brought together into one four-horse chariot, might become the Cherubim of the Lord, which means a multitude of knowledge. For never is a charioteer so well set, and never is he borne so adorned, as in those who are joined to one another by the multitude of knowledge and the reins of wisdom.

LXX: I kept watch, and my belly was afraid at the voice of the prayer of my lips; and trembling entered into my bones, and beneath me my strength was troubled. Or, as we find written elsewhere, ἡ ἕξις μου, which we can call my condition; for differing copies are found. These things can also be said in the person of the prophet, cohering with what precedes: Because you, O Lord, sent death upon the heads of the wicked, and raised up bonds up to the neck, and divided in amazement the heads of the mighty, and brought into the sea your horses troubling many waters, therefore I have kept my heart with all watchfulness, and my inward parts trembled, and my whole strength, or condition, was troubled, lest I should suffer the like. It can also have, as it were, its own beginning, the prophet relating his fear, and how he dreaded lest he had sinned in anything, and, admonished at the voice of the prayer of his lips, feared God so much that trembling entered into his bones, and, placed under the lofty hand of the Lord, he was troubled in all the strength, or condition, of his soul. But what he says: Trembling entered into my bones, is to be taken ἐμφατικώτερον (more emphatically), so that we may see the greatness of the fear of God penetrating the whole of the soul and moving the whole man, lest he should do anything that would displease God. And since Scripture allegorically, following the members of the body, mentions members of the soul as well, let us take the fearful belly as that power of the soul which receives spiritual food, and the lips as those by which the mind speaks with itself, and the bones as the robust and solid doctrines by which the whole framework of the soul is made firm. These things have been said by me briefly. But if anyone has found things shrewder and truer than these, give your assent rather to his explanation.

LXX: I shall rest in the day of tribulation, that I may go up to the people of my sojourning. Because I have kept my heart with all watchfulness, and my belly was afraid at the voice of the prayer of my lips, and trembling entered into my bones, and beneath me my strength or condition was troubled, and through so great a watchfulness I have been made a stranger to sins, therefore I now say confidently: I shall rest in the day of tribulation, that I may go up to the people of my sojourning, that is, the people which, just as I, has sojourned as a stranger in this world. And I shall go up, placed below as I am, and, as if striving from the valley toward higher places, I shall press on with all eagerness, so that at the time when the rest are in tribulation and distress, my care may be for the ascent, how I may rest with the people of my sojourning in the higher places. For the fig tree will not blossom, and there will be no bud in the vineyards; the work of the olive will lie, and the fields will not yield food; the flock will be cut off from the fold, and there will be no herd in the stalls. LXX: For the fig tree will not bear fruit, and there will be no buds in the vineyards; the work of the olive will lie, and the plains will not produce food; they have failed, because the sheep were eating, and there are no oxen in the stalls. According to the Hebrew, in which we said above: Let rottenness enter into my bones and swarm beneath me, that I may rest in the day of tribulation, that I may go up to our girded people, what preceded will be joined in this way to what follows: I wished to endure tribulation for the present, and afterward to go up to the strong people, for this reason, that the day of tribulation and necessity will come, and while the rest are set in distress, I shall rejoice in your majesty: For the fig tree will not blossom, and there will be no bud in the vineyards; the work of the olive will lie, and the fields will not yield food, and the rest. And since these words do not differ much from the LXX, let us discuss them together with their translation. When the day of tribulation has come, and I have gone up to the people which once sojourned with me, or certainly when the day of the overthrow of the Jews and of the former people has come, and the daughter of Zion has been left like a booth in a vineyard, and like a hut in a cucumber field, and like a city which is being besieged, I, who have been chosen out of a perishing people — of which it was said: Unless the Lord had left us a seed, we should have been as Sodom, and we should have been like Gomorrah —, shall join myself to the disciples of Christ, and because he teaches them on the mountain, leaving the crowds and the weak below, I shall climb up to the mountains. For the fig tree did not give fruit, the one to which the Lord came hungry in the Gospel, and he did not find fruit on it, and he cursed it, saying: You shall not bear fruit even unto the age. And consider diligently what he said: You shall not bear fruit unto the age; he does not say, unto the ages of ages, but when this age has passed away and the fullness of the Gentiles has entered, then this fig tree too will bear its fruits, and all Israel will be saved. This is the fig tree to which the householder came for the third time, and which he wishes to cut down as not producing fruit; for it the farmer, to whom it had been entrusted, pleads, that he may give it time, and he says: Lord, let it alone this year also, until I dig around it and put on manure, and if indeed it bears fruit; but if not, then you shall cut it down. That farmer is either Gabriel or Michael, to whom the people of the Jews was entrusted, who pleads with the Lord at the passion and says: Lord, give it time for repentance, and do not overthrow them, and if indeed they bear fruit; but if not, then you shall overthrow them. If they bear fruit, he says: he did not say what they would undergo; nor did he say: If they bear fruit, they will remain as they were; but, if they bear fruit: the sentence is left suspended, so that this is understood: you will transfer them into the church of the Gentiles, and transplant them into another vineyard. The Lord came for the third time and did not find fruit in them. First he gave the law through Moses, the second time he spoke through the prophets, the third time he himself came down. And after the passion, when forty-two years had been given for repentance, because they did not bear fruit, the fourth time they were overthrown. This, however, is left to our understanding. For in the parable it is not written what the householder did afterward, but only what the farmer pleaded for. From this we perceive that those of this fig tree who bore fruit were transferred to the people of the Gentiles, to which the prophet too goes up, saying: I shall rest in the day of tribulation, that I may go up to the people of my sojourning; but that those who did not bear fruit and remained in their hardness were overthrown. The voice of John in the Gospel also signifies this very thing: Behold, the axe is laid to the roots of the trees. Every tree that does not bear fruit will be cut down and cast into the fire. We have spoken of the fig tree, showing that it is the people of the Jews; let us speak also of the vineyard, which indeed anyone who has read Isaiah will easily understand: A vineyard was made for the beloved on a horn, in a fertile place, and afterward: And I waited for it to bear fruit; but it bore thorns, and not judgment, but a cry, and in Jeremiah: But I planted you a fruitful vineyard, wholly true; how have you been turned into the bitterness of a foreign vine? and more plainly in the psalms: You brought a vineyard out of Egypt, you cast out the nations, and planted it. This vineyard, then, to which the householder had often sent servants to receive from it wine, which gladdens the heart of man, because it was turned into bitterness, and at the last even dared to kill the householder's son, producing not grapes but thorns, and not judgment but a cry: Crucify, crucify him, and: We have no king but Caesar: therefore the boar from the forest has ravaged it, and the solitary wild beast has fed upon it. That the olive too is the people of the synagogue will be clearly proved by anyone who has read in the Apostle of the broken branches of the olive, and of us grafted in from the wild olive, so that in the branches we understand that the Jewish multitude was cut off, and in the roots that the election of the apostles was preserved; grafted into them we shall remain, if we bear fruit, and it will be said of us: Your sons like young shoots of olives around your table. Many think that the fig tree, the vineyard and the olive are taken as the mystery of the Trinity: that the fig tree, because of the sweetness of its fruit, is understood as the Holy Spirit; the vineyard as our Lord Jesus Christ himself, who says in the Gospel: I am the vine; and the olive as God the Father almighty, by whom all things are enlightened, and from whom light goes forth, and to whom we can say: O olive, in your light we shall see light, that is, in the Son we shall see the Holy Spirit. To these fruitful trees and to the most fertile vineyard come the unfruitful trees in the book of Judges and ask them to reign over them. But the olive, the fig tree and the vine never reign over the trees of the forests, which are destined for burning; rather the buckthorn rules over them, full of briars and like the hedgehog, which dwells in Babylon and always lives in pits. This little tree has not only thorns but fire too, wounding and burning whatever it touches. And at last fire went out and consumed the trees of the forests. But so that you may know, according to the higher understanding, in which we have taken it as said of the synagogue: The fig tree will not bear fruit, and there will be no produce in the vineyards, that this is said not of fruits but of good works, in the olive the riddle is plainly opened, and it is said: The work of the olive will lie. For the fruits which they ought to have borne are shown in works; and the work of the olive will lie, as of one who promises one thing and does another: they said to Moses: All things whatsoever the Lord shall say, we will do; and they were unwilling to believe in him who was preached by Moses. The plains also will not bear fruit. Consider that Jerusalem, which was once set on mountains, with the mountains around her, and her foundations on the holy mountains, is now called lowly and flat, a place that nourishes not only no men, that is, no rational animals, but not even flocks and oxen, of which Solomon also speaks in Proverbs: Take care of the regions that are in the plain, and cut the grass, and gather hay, that you may have sheep to eat. Oxen also will not be in the stalls, because where the stalls are full, the strength of the ox is manifest. The ox is a worker; the ox of the Lord bearing the yoke; the ox in whose track he who sows is blessed. All these things will be taken away from the people, because it acted wickedly against God its Creator. But if you wish to take the day of tribulation as the day of the consummation, you will refer everything to those who say they are of the Church and do not have the works of justice. And the fig tree, the vineyard and the olive, namely the mystery of the Trinity, will not bear their fruit in them; and they have not only no grain and food of rational beings, but not even fodder for flocks and beasts of burden in their fields, and their stalls are empty, and instead of lofty mountains they dwell in flat and lowly places.
