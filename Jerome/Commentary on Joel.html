Commentary on Joel

Latin source: Jerome's commentary on Joel, in one book addressed to Pammachius (written at Bethlehem in 406), from the critical text of M. Adriaen, Corpus Christianorum, Series Latina 76 (Turnhout, 1969), pp. 159–209, verified against the page images, with the edition's evident misprints corrected (most of them confirmed by the text of Vallarsi). Each lemma gives Joel in Jerome's own version from the Hebrew and then, after "LXX:", in his Latin of the Septuagint; all Scripture is translated as Jerome quotes it, from his own Latin. Italics mark the lemmata and the other Scripture he quotes. Headings follow Jerome's own division of the text into lemmata. Psalms keep the Latin (LXX) numbering, with the Hebrew number in parentheses. Greek words are kept as printed, with an English gloss in parentheses where Jerome gives none, and Hebrew words keep Jerome's own transliteration. This English translation is released into the public domain.

Prologue

The order of the twelve prophets among the Seventy translators is not the same as that which is kept in the Hebrew truth. For they put Amos second, Micah third, Joel fourth, Obadiah fifth, Jonah sixth, Nahum seventh, Habakkuk eighth, Zephaniah ninth, Haggai tenth, Zechariah eleventh, Malachi twelfth; but the Hebrews, after Hosea, who is first among both, read Joel second, Amos third, Obadiah fourth, Jonah fifth, Micah sixth, Nahum seventh, Habakkuk eighth, Zephaniah ninth, Haggai tenth, Zechariah eleventh, and twelfth, who is also the last, Malachi. And since we have once enumerated all the prophets of the one volume, it seems useful to us to note briefly the ἐτυμολογίας (etymologies) of each, both in Greek and in Latin. Hosea means σώζων, whom we can call one who saves. Joel ἀρχόμενος, that is, beginning. Amos βαστάζων, which among the Latins is called carrying. Obadiah δοῦλος Κυρίου, that is, servant of the Lord. Jonah περιστερὰ, that is, dove. Micah τίς ὡς, a name composed of two parts of speech, which with us sounds who as, or who like? Nahum παράκλησις, that is, consolation. Habakkuk περιλαμβάνων, that is, embracing or wrestling. Zephaniah κεκρυμμένος Κυρίου, that is, the hidden one of the Lord. Haggai, ἑορτάζων, whom we can call festive or solemn. Zechariah μνήμη Κυρίου, that is, remembrance of the Lord. Malachi ἄγγελός μου, that is, my messenger. In what sense all these are to be taken will be discussed in their own volumes. But the four remaining prophets, that we may complete sixteen, Isaiah, Ezekiel, Jeremiah, Daniel, have this meaning: Isaiah is called σωτηρία Κυρίου, that is, salvation of the Lord; Ezekiel κράτος Κυρίου, which we can call the strength or the dominion of the Lord; Jeremiah ὑψηλὸς Κυρίου, that is, the exalted one of the Lord; Daniel ἔκρινέ με Κύριος, that is, the Lord has judged me. Since these things are so, I beseech you, my Pammachius, who fight against the devil with every art of combat, that, while we fight against Amalek, you lift up your hands to the Lord with Moses, and overcome the adversaries of Israel by your prayers. We have lately come out of Egypt, and, Pharaoh having been drowned in Hosea, we have crossed the Red Sea; now, after the broad waste of the desert, we must sweat with much labor, that we may be able to reach the seventy palms and the apostolic springs. Though the scorpion rise up and try to strike with its arched wound, you, companion of the journey and charmer of venomous bites, show yourself a spiritual ψυλλέα (a Psyllian, a snake-charmer) for us; and what we promised to the holy and venerable Paula, your mother-in-law, take up as a dutiful heir; and whatever we can do less than your expectation, whether in talent or in learning, sustain with a friend's favor, and do not consider in us our strength, but our will.

1:1

The word of the Lord which was made to Joel, the son of Phatuel. The Seventy translators rendered Bathuel for Phatuel, which in Hebrew has no meaning at all. But Phatuel is rendered into our language as breadth of God, or God opening, as we read in Mark that the Savior said to the deaf and dumb man: Ephpheta, which is, be opened. For since he could say with the Apostle: Our mouth is open to you, O Corinthians, our heart is enlarged, and heard from the Lord: Make wide your mouth, and I will fill it; and since this very opening of the mouth is not in the power of man but in the power of God, as Paul says: A great and evident door has been opened to me; but there are many adversaries; therefore he is called God opening. And since the saint always makes progress, knowing in part and prophesying in part, until that which is perfect comes, he who is begotten of breadth and opening is called Joel, which in our language means beginning, or is God, as the Apostle says: Brothers, I do not consider that I have laid hold of it; and growing by this humility he deserves to hear with Moses: He who is, has sent me. For by way of distinction from those who are not, of whom we read also in Esther: Do not hand over your scepter to those who are not, God and his saints are said to be. Let these things about the name of Joel and of his father be touched on briefly, so that the word of God, which was in the beginning with God, may rightly be said to have been made, as John the Baptist relates: He who is to come after me was made before me, because he was before me. Moreover, that the word was made is referred to the merit of him to whom it is made, not to the condition of him who is said to be made, as we read elsewhere: The Lord was made salvation for me. And just as in the prophet Hosea, whom we expounded before this book at the beginning of the Twelve Prophets, where the prophecy is directed under the name of Ephraim to the ten tribes, which are often called either Samaria or Israel, so in Joel, who according to the Hebrews is the second, everything that is said must be believed to pertain to the tribe of Judah and to Jerusalem, and no mention at all is made in him of Israel, that is, of the ten tribes. We must also take the times in which he prophesied to be the same as those we read in Hosea: In the days of Uzziah, Jotham and Ahaz and Hezekiah, kings of Judah, and in the days of Jeroboam, son of Joash, king of Israel.

1:2-3

Hear this, old men, and perceive with your ears, all inhabitants of the earth, if this has happened in your days, or in the days of your fathers; tell of this to your sons, and your sons to their sons, and their sons to another generation. LXX: Hear these things, old men, and perceive with your ears, all inhabitants of the earth, if such things have happened in your days, or in the days of your fathers; for these things, tell your sons, and your sons their sons, and their sons to another generation. The old men are commanded to hear, the inhabitants of the earth to perceive with their ears. To the old men it is not said: Hear, all; to the inhabitants of the earth is added: Perceive with your ears, all. But hearing in the holy Scriptures is not that which sounds in the ear, but that which is perceived by the heart, according to what the Lord says in the Gospel: He who has ears to hear, let him hear. But what we have translated as Perceive with your ears is one word both among the Greeks and among the Hebrews: among the Greeks ἐνωτίσασθε, among the Hebrews eezinu, which properly is perceived not by the heart but by the ear. And so that we may know that hearing is more sacred than that which sounds in the ears, let us learn it from Isaiah, who says: Hear, O heaven, and perceive with your ears, O earth. Those who are old men and heavenly hear spiritually; those who dwell on the earth and are called earthly perceive with their ears. And this must be noted in all the Scriptures where these two words are placed together. We read this also in the case of Lamech, the sinner, who spoke to his wives Adah and Zillah: Hear my words, wives of Lamech, perceive my words with your ears, for I have killed a man to my wounding, and a young man to my bruising; he knew that what he was saying was obscure, and therefore he called his wives not only to the simple sound of the words, but also to the hidden understanding of what was said. If, then, anyone is an old man, and an old man chosen of ripe age in the Lord, as we read further on according to the Seventy translators, and has left behind the infancy of little children, let him hear what is said. But he who still dwells on the earth, and cannot say: I am a stranger and a sojourner, like all my fathers, let him perceive with his ears. If this has happened, he says, in your days, or in the days of your fathers. With rhetorical art he makes the hearer attentive by the greatness of the matter. No age, he says, remembers the things I am about to say; they did not happen in your time, nor in the time of your fathers and forebears. But by fathers understand grandfathers and remote ancestors, and by sons the sons of sons, all the offspring that follow, according to that Vergilian line: And the sons of sons, and those who will be born of them.

Therefore, both old men and inhabitants of the earth, tell it to your sons and to your posterity: let him who is an old man teach his children the mysteries; let him who is an inhabitant of the earth tell the simple history. Hence to this very day we who believe in Christ, from whose eyes the veil has been taken away with Moses, and of whom it is said: The gray hairs of a man are his wisdom, tell our sons things secret and wonderful. But the Jews, who dwell on the earth, speak earthly things that cling to the ground, of whom it is written: He who is of the earth speaks of the earth; he who comes from heaven is above all.

1:4

What the caterpillar left, the locust has eaten, and what the locust left, the cankerworm has eaten, and what the cankerworm left, the blight has eaten. LXX: likewise. The narration follows the exordium: there, to make the hearer attentive, he promised that he would say great and incredible things, which neither ancient history knew nor the present age knew to have happened. Here he has set down the caterpillar, and the locust, and the cankerworm, and the blight, so that things each of which rarely happens may be recorded as having happened all at once, and may therefore be wonderful. The caterpillar, which in Hebrew is called gezem, in Greek κάμπη, the Hebrews interpret as the Assyrians, the Babylonians and the Chaldeans, who, coming forth from one region of the world, laid waste everything belonging to the ten tribes as well as to the two, that is, to the Israelite people. The locust they interpret as the Medes and Persians, who, after the overthrow of the empire of the Chaldeans, held the Jews captive. The cankerworm, as the Macedonians and all the successors of Alexander, and above all King Antiochus surnamed Epiphanes, who settled in Judea like a cankerworm and devoured all the remnants left by the former kings, under whom the wars of the Maccabees are recounted. The blight they refer to the empire of the Romans, who, fourth and last, so oppressed the Jews as to drive them out of their own territory. Josephus writes of it more fully in seven volumes, recounting the triumphs of Vespasian and Titus. We read also of the expedition of Aelius Hadrian against the Jews, who so overthrew Jerusalem and its walls that out of the remains and ashes of the city he founded a city named Aelia after himself. These four kingdoms which overthrew Judea Zechariah writes that he saw in four horns, the angel saying to him: These are the horns which have scattered Judah and Israel and Jerusalem. And again: I lifted up, he says, my eyes and saw; and behold, four chariots coming out from between two mountains, and the mountains were mountains of bronze; in the first chariot, red horses; and in the second chariot, black horses; and in the third chariot, white horses; and in the fourth chariot, dappled, strong horses. And when the prophet had said to the angel who spoke in him: What are these, my lord? The angel answered: These are the four winds of heaven, which go forth to stand before the ruler of all the earth; and the sense is: These are they who go forth from the face of the Lord, to fulfill his will. Since with the inhabitants of the earth we perceive with our ears what the caterpillar, the locust, the cankerworm and the blight signify, let us now hear with the old men what has been said. All the schools of the philosophers cry out together that there are four perturbations by which the health of souls is overthrown. Two are present and contrary to one another, two are future and at variance with each other. The present ones are distress and joy. We say distress of the mind; otherwise, of the body it is called not distress but illness. Either, then, we are sad and consumed with grief, and the state of our mind is overturned; whence the Apostle also warns that a brother should not be swallowed up by too much sorrow. Or, on the contrary, we rejoice and exult with gladness, and cannot bear our good things with moderation; and it belongs to a just and brave man neither to be broken by adversity nor to be lifted up by prosperity, but to be moderate in both. We have spoken of the perturbation of present things; let us speak also of that of future things, in which there is fear or hope. We fear adverse things, we await prosperous ones; and what distress and joy work in the present, fear and hope do concerning the future, while we either fear more than is fitting that adverse things are to come, or the prosperous things we hope for make us exult so much that we do not keep measure, especially in things that are uncertain, because future things are hoped for rather than held. These perturbations the illustrious poet comprehended in one little verse, and that not even a full one: These fear and desire — this of the future —, grieve and rejoice — this of the present —, nor, he says, do they look up at the air, shut in darkness and a blind prison.

For those who are wrapped in the darkness of the perturbations cannot behold the clear light of wisdom. We must beware, then, lest distress, like the caterpillar, eat us; lest the locust lay us waste in joy, flitting here and there, and tossing itself in different directions with exultant gladness; lest the cankerworm, that is, dread and fear of things to come, devour the roots of wisdom; lest the blight, the desire of things to come, covet useless things and carry us off to ruin; but that in all things we may govern with the reins of wisdom the four chariots and the four horns, and the four horses, red, and dappled, and white, and black, that is, adverse things, or prosperous ones, or those joined from both. I think that the caterpillar is the passion beginning in the mind, which is slow and cannot run about, and by its very lingering and persistence drinks up and sucks out all the greenness; and if we do not kill it, it grows in us and flies off, and now it devours whatever it has touched; now, leaving things half-eaten, it goes on to others, and returning to its former seat it becomes the cankerworm, so that by its slowness it devours not only the crops and the leaves and the bark, but even the very pith. But if it happens, though this is rare, that even after the cankerworm something of the vital spirit remains in us, the blight ravages everything, so that it turns the stubble and the worthless hay to blackness, so that they are useless not only for food but even for dung. Of these four perturbations we shall discourse also at the beginning of Amos, if life remains our companion, where it is written: For three crimes, and for four, of Damascus, Gaza, Tyre, Idumea, the sons of Ammon, and Moab, and Judah, and Israel, I will not turn them back, says the Lord. What we have rendered as perturbations the Greeks call πάθη; if we render these κακοζήλως (affectedly) as passions, we shall have expressed the word rather than the sense of the word.

1:5

Wake up, you drunkards; and weep and howl, all you who drink wine in sweetness, because it has perished from your mouth. LXX: Awake, you who are drunk with your wine; and wail and lament, all you who drink wine in drunkenness, because it has been taken away from your mouth. As old men and elders we ought to hear: nothing so makes one drunk as the perturbation of the mind. There is a sorrow that leads to death: this is a drunkenness to be abhorred. There is an anger that does not work the righteousness of God, and, being very near to madness, it makes a man not master of his own mind, so much that the lips tremble, the teeth chatter, the face changes with pallor. And rightly is that saying of Archytas of Tarentum praised, who, when he was angry with his steward, said: I would kill you now, he said, if I were not angry. What shall I say of joy and pleasure, and above all of love, which blinds the eyes of the heart and permits the lover to think of nothing but what he loves? Must it not be called drunkenness when, for the sake of a worthless harlot and a shameful part of the body, the freedom of the soul is bent down to servile flatteries? When it makes its own labor the delights of another? When by theft, crime and perjuries it prepares wealth for future pleasure? And when, though it is seen by all, it thinks itself unseen, so long as it gains possession of what it desires. But avarice too blinds the mind of him for whom nothing is enough; and so do womanish fear and the craving for sweet vices. Hence it is said to them: Awake and wake up, you who are drunk, by no means with wine, as is contained in the LXX alone, but with every perturbation of vices. Weep and lament, and do penance, and take up the sorrow that leads to life, and howl, all you who drink wine in sweetness, or in drunkenness. For vices are sweet, because honey drips from the lips of a harlot woman; and for that reason it is not offered in the sacrifices of God, because the wine has perished, or has been taken away from your mouth, the drunkenness and sweetness that had deceived you. For it often happens by the providence of God that those who did not know him in prosperity know him in adversity, and that those who have wickedly abused riches are corrected toward virtues by want. According to this sense let the old men hear, and let the inhabitants of the earth perceive with their ears, that the command now concerns that wine in which there is debauchery, and those who have been made drunk with it cannot possess the kingdom of God. Let him who has been put to sleep by the drunkenness of wine awake, and wail that he has been drunk, and howl, so that afterward his howling and weeping may be turned into laughter, and he may rejoice that he no longer has the matter of drunkenness, which through abundance had made him drunk and mad.

1:6-7

For a nation has come up over my land, strong and without number; its teeth are like the teeth of a lion, and its molars like a lion's whelp. It has made my vine a desert, and has stripped the bark from my fig tree; stripping it, it has despoiled it, and cast it away; its branches have become white. LXX: For a nation has come up over the land, strong and without number; its teeth are the teeth of a lion, and its molars as of a lion's whelp. It has made my vine a destruction, and my figs a breaking; searching it has searched it, and cast it away; it has whitened its branches. The Jews think that in the days of Joel so countless a multitude of locusts came over Judea that they filled everything; and they left behind, I will not say the crops, but not even the bark and the branches of the vines and the trees, so that, all the greenness consumed, the whitening branches of the trees and the dry shoots of the vines remained. Whether this happened or not we cannot clearly affirm; for the history of Kings and Chronicles does not recount it. Certainly, had it happened, Scripture would never have kept silent, just as we read of the famine of three years and six months under Elijah. We say only this, that under the metaphor of locusts the coming of enemies is described: either of the Assyrians and Babylonians, who were then threatening; or of the Medes and Persians, who were to come afterward; or of the Macedonians, who we know came a long time after; or finally of the Romans, of whom we spoke above. Although it now seems to us rather to be said about the Babylonians and the Chaldeans, whose cruelty and savagery against the people of God is described; and, unless I am mistaken, I seem to have found something in this prophet. The impiety of the enemies is recounted under the figure of locusts, and again it is said of the locusts themselves as though they were compared to enemies, so that when you read of locusts you think of enemies, and when you have thought of enemies you return to the locusts. So a nation of locusts has come up out of the wilderness, or the army of the Chaldeans, over the land of God, strong and without number. For what is more countless and stronger than locusts, which human industry cannot resist? Its teeth, that is, of the nation of locusts — but understand everything τροπικῶς (figuratively) —, are like the teeth of a lion, and its molars like a lion's whelp, so that those who are equal to locusts in strength and multitude are compared to lions in ferocity and cruelty. This nation, he says, has made my vine a desert, the vine which I brought over out of Egypt and planted, and has stripped the bark from my fig tree, the people of the Jews, to whom the Savior came to eat its fruit, and did not find any, and cursed it, and it withered forever. But let us recognize that all these things are said under the metaphor of locusts, which so ravage everything that they gnaw off the bark of trees and cast it away stripped off, and, all the greenness consumed, leave the branches white and dry. We have spoken according to the history; let us pass on to the spiritual understanding, so that with the old men we may be able to hear: A nation has come up over the land of God, that is, the human soul. For all souls are God's; as the soul of the father, so also the soul of the son. And there comes up the nation of the princes of this world, and of darkness, and the spiritual forces of wickedness in the heavenly places, against whom we have our wrestling and struggle, of whom it is said: If the spirit of him who has power comes up over you, do not leave your place; the teeth of this nation are like the teeth of a lion, of whom the apostle Peter speaks: Our adversary the devil, like a roaring lion, goes about seeking whom he may devour. And its molars are like a lion's whelp. By the lion's whelp understand either him who is lifted up against everything that is called God and religion, or certainly every perverse dogma. Over the crushing of his molars, which lie hidden within and are not seen, so that they are not easily avoided, the psalmist also rejoices, saying: The Lord will break the molars of the lions. If, then, we give place to this nation to come up over us, at once it will make our vine a desert, the vine from which we used to make the wine that gladdens the heart of man; and it strips the bark from our figs, or breaks them, so that we may not have in us the sweetest gifts of the Holy Spirit, so that the holy man may not rest under our vine and fig tree, under which, when he is there, he will not fear the assault of the adversaries. Nor is it enough for this nation to destroy the vine and break the fig tree, unless, searching, it searches them, and kills whatever is living in them, so that, all the greenness consumed, white and dead twigs remain, and there is fulfilled in us: If they do these things in the green wood, what will they do in the dry?

1:8

Lament like a virgin girded with sackcloth over the husband of your youth. LXX: Lament to me over a bride girded with haircloth, over her virgin husband. The husband of youth, or, as the Seventy translated, παρθενικὸς, whom people commonly call the virgin husband, because he was the first to take away the flower of virginity, is understood to be none other than God, who in Abraham, Isaac and Jacob betrothed to himself a virgin bride stained by no filth of idolatry. To her he speaks also through Jeremiah: Did you not call me lord, and father, and prince of your virginity?. Hence the Apostle also says to the believers: I have betrothed you to one husband, to present you as a chaste virgin to Christ. As long as the bridegroom is with this bride, you cannot fast, nor lament, nor show by tears your longing for an absent bridegroom. But when the bridegroom has been taken away from her, she laments and wails, and girds herself with sackcloth and haircloth, and instead of a belt she is girded with a rope. We have understood who the virginal bridegroom is; but because this bridegroom, or husband, not only received a virgin as his bride, but also, in Hosea, took a harlot as his wife, for this reason it is written in Deuteronomy: If you go out against your enemies, and the Lord your God delivers them into your hand, and you lead them captive, and see among the number of the captives a beautiful woman, and love her, and wish to have her as your wife; you shall bring her into your house, and she shall shave her hair and pare her nails, and put off the garment in which she was taken, and sitting in your house she shall weep for her father and her mother for one month, and afterward you shall go in to her and sleep with her, and she shall be your wife. The husband of such a woman, that is, her lord, is not a virginal one; rather, he takes as his wife a harlot from the filth of the Gentiles. This we can say also of every soul of the believers. If it has believed in the Lord from a tender age, it has the Lord as a virginal bridegroom. But if it has come to the truth of the faith from the Jews or the Gentiles, and from the most filthy dregs of the heretics, and has passed from darkness to light, it will indeed have a bridegroom, but not a virgin one; and of such people it is said: In Egypt your breasts were crushed, and there you were deflowered.

1:9-12

The sacrifice and the libation have perished from the house of the Lord, and the priests, the ministers of the Lord, have mourned; the region is laid waste, the ground has mourned, because the wheat is devastated. The wine is confounded, the oil has languished, the farmers are confounded. The vinedressers have howled over the grain and the barley, because the harvest of the field has perished; the vine is confounded and the fig tree has languished; the pomegranate and the palm and the apple tree, and all the trees of the field have dried up, because joy is confounded from the sons of men. LXX: The sacrifice and the libation have been taken away from the house of the Lord; mourn, you priests, ministers of the Lord, because the plains have been made desolate; let the land mourn, because the wheat is afflicted, the wine has dried up, the oil is diminished, the farmers have dried up; mourn, you possessions, for the grain and the barley, because the vintage has perished from the field, the vine has dried up, and the figs are diminished; the pomegranate and the palm and the apple tree and all the trees of the field are dried up, because the sons of men have confounded joy. As far as the history is concerned, because of the multitude of locusts, or of the enemies who are described under their likeness, when everything had been laid waste and consumed, the sacrifice and the libation perished from the house of the Lord, of which they were accustomed to offer the one from fine flour, the other from wine. Therefore the priests, who are the ministers of the Lord, mourned, since neither the sacrifices nor the libations were duly celebrated, above all because the tithes too, which they had been accustomed to receive, were by no means offered. For the whole region was laid waste. The ground mourned, μετωνυμικῶς (by metonymy), for: those who dwelt on the ground; the wheat, the wine and the oil languished; the farmers and vinedressers too were confounded and howled, because not only the grain but also the barley, which is cheaper and more fruitful, dried up. And the legumes too, which I think are signified where he says: The harvest of the field has perished, that is, all that the ground is wont to produce. Why should I speak of grain, wine and oil and barley, when even the fruits of the trees have dried up, the fig tree has languished, and the pomegranate, and the palm, and the apple tree, and all the trees, whether unfruitful or fruit-bearing, have been consumed by the ravaging locust? All these things happened so that joy might be taken away, or confounded, from the sons of men. We can say that these same things happened also to the people of the Jews after the coming of the Savior, when, crying out with equal fury, they said: Crucify, crucify such a one; we have no king but Caesar; when Jerusalem was surrounded by an army, and they came into such extremity of famine and pestilence that they fed on the bodies of their sons, not yet grown; and all the sacrifices were taken away, and joy was confounded from the sons of men, because they refused to receive the joy of which the angel speaks to the shepherds: I announce to you great joy. According to ἀναγωγὴν (anagogy) this seems to us to be the sense: after the bride who had received the teaching of God has been separated from the bridegroom through her sins, and instead of garments of fine linen and linen has been girded with haircloth, that is, has taken on the dress of one who mourns, then the sacrifice will perish, of which it is written: A sacrifice to God is a contrite spirit, and the libation of wine, which gladdens the heart of man, from the house of God, which is the Church, as the Apostle says to Timothy: That you may know how you ought to conduct yourself in the house of God, which is the Church of the living God. And the sacrifice and the libation will be taken away from the house of the Lord when, iniquity having multiplied, the love of many has grown cold; and the leaders of the people and the ministers of the altar see that the plains and level ground of the believers do not bear the fruits of virtues, but that everything bristles with vices, when the caterpillar, the locust, the cankerworm and the blight have consumed the grain and the barley, and the vines and the oil, and we no longer have the cup of which it is written: Your inebriating cup, how splendid it is. And the oil will have perished, of which we read in Ecclesiastes: At all times let your garments be white, and let oil not be lacking from your head, by which the face is gladdened and the head of the one who fasts is anointed. Then the farmers will be confounded, when they see that in their possessions there springs up no wheat, by which men are nourished, and no barley, by which the irrational beasts of burden are sustained, and that the vine is confounded, of which it is written: Israel is a leafy vine, abundant fruit is in it; the Lord waiting for it to make grapes, and it made thorns. The fig tree too has languished, under which Nathanael was before he believed, and the pomegranate, to whose rind the cheeks of the bride are compared in the Song; and the palm, which, having lost its greenness, has dried up, of which it was once said: But the just man will flourish like the palm; and the apple tree, of which we read in the same Song: As the apple tree among the trees of the wood, so is my kinsman among the sons. What need is there to run through all the trees, since all have dried up, and instead of joy and gladness grief and confusion have overwhelmed the sons of men?

1:13-14

Let us pass to the spiritual understanding, taking up each point again. The blast of trumpets and horns we read of not only in Leviticus and in the book of Numbers, in which it is commanded that Moses make two silver trumpets, with which, on the kalends, and in the seventh month on the first day of the month, and at the jubilee, and at the other festivals, and when they go forth to war, those who are bound to this office should sound; but it is also written that the walls of Jericho fell at the blast of the trumpets. And the Lord says that he will send his angel with the blast of a trumpet, and the Apostle proclaims that the resurrection of the dead takes place at the sound of the trumpet. And in the Apocalypse of John too we read that seven angels received a trumpet each, and as they sounded in order, the things came to pass which the Scripture describes. But by the day of the Lord understand the day of judgment, or the day of each one's departure from the body. For what will come to pass for all on the day of judgment is fulfilled in each one on the day of death. It is a day of darkness and of distress, a day of cloud and whirlwind; for all things are full of punishments and torments. A people of angels, many and strong, will come, to render to each according to his works; and as the morning and the rising dawn first takes hold of the mountains, so judgment will begin from the lofty and the powerful, that the mighty may suffer torments mightily. For all the evils which are contained in the ancient histories, and which befell men either by the flooding of the sea, or by the overflowing of rivers, or by pestilence, diseases, famine, beasts, the devastation of enemies, cannot be compared with these punishments which are to be rendered on the day of judgment. The preceding section, from the place where it is written: Blow the trumpet in Zion, wail on my holy mountain; let all the inhabitants of the land be troubled, up to the place where we read: Great is the day of the Lord and very terrible, who shall endure it? announces, by the figure of locusts, the coming of the Chaldeans, and what evils are to come upon the people. Now he calls them to repentance, and exhorts them to turn to the Lord, so that, corrected with their whole mind, they may not suffer what the Lord threatens; and the sense is: All that is contained in the preceding discourse I spoke for this reason, to terrify you with my threat. Therefore turn to me with your whole heart, and show the repentance of your mind by fasting and weeping and mourning; so that, fasting now, you may afterwards be filled, weeping now, you may afterwards laugh, mourning now, you may afterwards be comforted. And since it is the custom that in sad and adverse things you rend your clothes, which the high priest too is recorded in the Gospel to have done, to aggravate the charge against the Lord the Savior; and which we read that Paul and Barnabas did on hearing words of blasphemy. Therefore I command you by no means to rend your garments, but your hearts, which are full of sins, and which, like wineskins, unless they have been rent, will burst of themselves. And when you have done this, return to the Lord your God, whom your former sins had made a stranger to you; and do not despair of pardon because of the greatness of your crimes, for great mercy will blot out great sins. For he is kind and merciful, preferring the repentance of sinners to their death, patient and great in mercy, one who does not imitate human impatience, but waits a long time for our repentance; and placable, or repenting over evils, so that if we do penance for our sins, he too may repent of his threat, and not bring upon us the evils which he has threatened, and with the change of our resolve he too may be changed. But here we ought to take evil not as the contrary of virtue, but as affliction, according to what we read elsewhere: Sufficient for the day is its own evil. And: If there is evil in a city which the Lord has not done. And likewise, because he had said above, kind and merciful, patient and great in mercy, and placable, or repenting over evils, lest perhaps the greatness of his clemency should make us careless, he adds in the person of the prophet and says: Who knows if he will turn and pardon, and leave behind him a blessing? I, he says, exhort to repentance, which is my part, and I know that God is ineffably merciful, as David says: Have mercy on me, O God, according to your great mercy, and according to the multitude of your compassions blot out my iniquity. But because we cannot know the depth of the riches and of the wisdom and knowledge of God, I temper my statement, and I wish rather than presume, saying: Who knows if he will turn and pardon? When he says who, it is to be understood as either impossible or difficult: Sacrifice and libation to the Lord our God; so that after he has given the blessing and has forgiven us our sins, we may be able to offer victims to God.

And if you avenge yourselves against me, quickly, swiftly I will return your recompense upon your head. For you have taken my silver and my gold, and my desirable things, and the most beautiful you have carried into your shrines; and the sons of Judah and the sons of Jerusalem you have sold to the sons of the Greeks, that you might remove them far from their borders. LXX: What have I to do with you, Tyre and Sidon and all Galilee of the foreigners? Are you rendering me retribution, or do you keep anger in your heart against me? — for this is what μνησικακείτε means in Greek —. Swiftly and quickly I will return your retribution upon your heads, because you have taken my silver and my gold, and my choice and best things you have carried into your temples, and the sons of Judah and the sons of Jerusalem you have sold to the sons of the Greeks, that you might cast them out of their borders. And these things too the Jews think are said against Tyre and Sidon and the borders of the Philistines, or Galilee of the foreigners, because at the time of the Jewish captivity, when they were conquered by the Romans, these persecuted the people of God; nay rather, in the people of God, God himself, who was set over the people, according to what is written: He who receives you receives me. Therefore, conversely, he who persecutes the people of God persecutes God himself, whose people it is. I will repay you, he says, what you have done to my people, because you have taken my silver and gold, that is, the vessels of the temple, and whatever was most precious and most splendid in it, the golden lampstand and the golden table of the showbread, and the two golden Cherubim, and the mercy seat, and the golden bowls and censers, and have consecrated them to your idols. But since these things are said to be going to happen after the great and terrible day of the Lord, which the apostles interpret of the resurrection of the Lord, and the Hebrews defer to the future time of judgment, it is rather to be understood of the Romans; for Vespasian and Titus, having built the temple of Peace at Rome, consecrated the vessels of the temple and all its offerings in that shrine, as Greek and Roman history relates. At that time the sons of Judah and Jerusalem — by no means Israel and the ten tribes, which to this day dwell in the cities and mountains of the Medes —, were sold to the sons of the Greeks, to be banished from their borders, and the whole world was filled with the Jewish captivity. They refer this against Tyre and Sidon so as to maintain that the vengeance for the blood of Christ and the overthrow of Jerusalem, which happened by the judgment of God, happened of itself. But let us, following the tropology we have begun, interpret Tyre and Sidon and the Philistines as those who hem in and afflict and persecute the people of God — for this is what Tyre sounds in our language —, and hunt him to death, which the name of Sidon indicates, and, drinking blood, fall down, or wallow in the mire, which is what the Philistines and Galilee signify. Swiftly and quickly the Lord will repay them what they deserve, because they have persecuted him. And his silver and gold, namely the words of the Scriptures, and the νοήματα, that is, the meanings, and everything that was beautiful in the Church, they have made the slaves of their errors. The heretic, whomever he has deceived and made to worship his own images, sells the sons of Judah and the sons of Jerusalem to the Greeks, or Gentiles, and makes them from Christians into heathens, to banish them from their borders, in which they had been begotten in Christ, so that they dwell no longer in Judea and in the confession of the truth, but in the error of the Gentiles. All these things we can also refer to the day of judgment, the vengeance not differing, although in time they seem to differ from the things above. For Galilee, in the Hebrew is written Galiloth, which Aquila translated Θῖνας, Symmachus borders; and Θῖνας, that is, mounds of sand, let us refer to the shores of Palestine, not to a Galilee of the Philistines, which does not exist at all.

LXX: Behold, I will raise them up from the place in which you sold them, and I will return your recompense upon your heads, and I will sell your sons and your daughters into the hands of the sons of Judah; and they will sell them into captivity to a nation that is far away, because the Lord has spoken. The Hebrew word Sabaim, which Aquila and Symmachus translated just as it is written, the LXX rendered as captivity, which would more properly mean captives. Now Sabaim is said to be a nation beyond India, to which also belonged the queen of Sheba, who came to hear the wisdom of Solomon, of whom Isaiah too speaks: And Sabaim, men of stature, shall pass over to you. From there also incense is said to come, as Vergil says: ..., and a hundred altars glow with Sabaean incense. Though some suppose the Sabeans to be Arabs. The Jews, then, promise themselves, or rather dream, that in the last time they will be gathered by the Lord and brought back to Jerusalem. And not content with this happiness, they assert that God himself will deliver the sons and daughters of the Romans into their hands, so that the Jews may sell them, not to the Persians and Ethiopians and the other nations that are near, but to the Sabeans, a most distant nation, because the Lord has spoken and avenges the injury of his people. So say they and our Judaizers, who promise themselves a thousand-year kingdom within the borders of Judea, and a golden Jerusalem, and the blood of victims, and sons and grandsons and incredible delights, and gates adorned with a variety of gems. But let us say that the Lord both raised up after his coming, and raises up daily, and will raise up those whom manifold error had led out of their borders. And he says beautifully, I will raise up, as of those lying down and fallen, so that those who lay in heresy may stand in the Church, repaying the heretics what they had done: so that he may deliver their sons and daughters, whom they had instructed both in mystical and in carnal things, into the hands of the sons of Judah, into the hands of those who have stood forth as princes of the Churches, and are equipped with the armor of the Apostle, and have the shield and spear of the Old and New Instrument; so that, when they have taken them, they may sell their sons and daughters to the Sabeans, and make them captives, that they may be far from their own borders; and, converted to better things, may begin to be subject to the dogmas of the Church.

Proclaim this among the nations, sanctify war, rouse the strong. Let them approach, let all the men of war go up. Beat your ploughshares into swords, and your mattocks into spears. Let the weak say, I am strong. Burst out and come, all you nations round about, and gather yourselves together; there the Lord will cause your strong ones to fall. LXX: Proclaim these things among the nations, sanctify war, rouse the fighters, bring them up and go up, all you men of war. Beat your ploughshares into swords, and your sickles into spears. Let the strong say, I prevail. Gather yourselves together and come in, all nations round about; and gather yourselves there; let the gentle one be a warrior. This passage is understood in two ways. These things wretched Israel promises itself according to the letter. We, following the tropology we have begun, can take them both of the nations of demons, and of those who daily fight against the Church, and in the end of those who under the Antichrist are going to fight against the saints of the Lord, who are gathered together for this purpose, that they may perish.

Come and go down, for the winepress is full; the vats overflow, for their wickedness is multiplied. LXX: Let all the nations arise and go up into the valley of Jehoshaphat, for there I will sit to judge all the nations round about; send forth the sickles, for the vintage is at hand. Understand by strangers either the demons, who are alien to God, or all the worst thoughts and sins, of which the prophet speaks: From strangers spare your servant; which would by no means find a way into us any more, if we have the peace of God and our heart has not lain open to the adversaries. These things the Jews and, as we have said, our Judaizers refer to the fable of the thousand years, when they think that Christ will dwell in Zion, and that the peoples of the saints will be gathered into a Jerusalem of gold and gems, so that those who in this age have been oppressed by all the nations may in this same age rule over all the nations.

3:18

And it will be in that day: the mountains will drip sweetness, and the hills will flow with milk, and through all the streams of Judah waters will go. LXX: And it will be in that day: the mountains will drip sweetness, and the hills will flow with milk, and all the outlets of Judah will flow with waters. When the Lord dwells in Zion and on his holy mountain, when no one will attempt to pass through holy Jerusalem, whoever in her is a mountain, and has reached the heights of the virtues, will sweat sweetness and honey; and from him will drip spiritual graces, of which the prophet speaks: How sweet are your words to my throat, above honey to my mouth. But he who is lower than the mountains, and has not yet climbed to the summit of perfection, will be called a hill, and from him will flow streams of milk, by which the untaught infancy in Christ is nourished, and the rivers of water which the Lord testified would flow from his belly. For all the streams, or outlets, of Judah will be filled with waters, and nothing in them will be dry, since all things overflow with spiritual grace.

And a fountain will go forth from the house of the Lord and will water the torrent of thorns. For the torrent of thorns the Seventy translated the torrent of cords, that is, σχοίνων, which means either cords or, among the Egyptians, the measure of a fixed stretch of road, as the psalmist says: My path and my cord you have searched out. For on the river Nile, or in its channels, they are accustomed to haul ships with ropes, having fixed stretches, which they call cords, so that fresh necks of haulers may take over the labor of the exhausted. Nor is it any wonder if each nation calls the fixed stretches of roads by its own names, since the Latins speak of a thousand paces, the Gauls of leagues, the Persians of parasangs, and all Germany of rastae, and with each of these names the measure is different. I say this because the Seventy translated the Hebrew word settim as cords. Moreover, no one doubts that everything we read of in the tabernacle of God was made of settim wood, which the LXX rendered incorruptible wood. Now it is a kind of tree in the desert, like the white thorn in color and leaves, not in size. For otherwise the trees are so large that very broad planks are cut from them, and the wood is very strong, and of incredible lightness and beauty, so that all the wealthiest and most painstaking even make from it the vessels of the winepresses, which they call ἄρσενας (males) and θηλύας (females); and this wood is not found in cultivated places and on Roman soil, apart from the desert of Arabia. For the torrent of cords, or of thorns, Symmachus rendered the valley of thorns. Now there is a place near Livias, beyond the Dead Sea, six miles distant from Livias, where Israel once committed fornication with the Midianites. This place the prophet Micah mentions in the person of God, saying: My people, remember, I pray, what Balak king of Moab devised, and what Balaam son of Beor answered, from Settim to Gilgal. For which there too the Seventy translated ἀπὸ τῶν σχοίνων, that is, from the cords to Gilgal. Therefore a fountain will go forth from the house of the Lord, which is interpreted as the Church. Of this Ezekiel and Zechariah also write at the end of their books: that they may change our thorns, and vices and sins, which have borne no fruit of righteousness, into the Lord's new-broken fields, and water our dryness with most abundant waters, and that instead of thorns and briars we may bring forth manifold flowers of the virtues. And in that place where Israel once committed fornication and was initiated into Baal-peor, may lilies of chastity and roses of modesty and virginity abound.

3:19

Egypt will be a desolation, and Idumea a desert of perdition, because they have acted wickedly against the sons of Judah, and have shed innocent blood in their land. LXX: Egypt will be in perdition, and Idumea a plain of desolation, because of the iniquities of the sons of Judah, because they have shed righteous blood in their land. In this place too the Jews sleep a very heavy sleep, imagining with a most vain hope that in the last time, when they are to receive not Christ but the Antichrist, there will be vengeance for them upon the Egyptians, who are their neighbors, and upon the Romans, whom they interpret as the Idumeans: namely, that just as Pharaoh and all his army, who held the people of God captive for four hundred and thirty years, were drowned in the Red Sea, so also the Romans, who are to possess the Jews for the same span of years, may be destroyed by the vengeance of the Lord. Let them promise themselves this with an empty hope. For they cannot explain why, when there are so many nations in the world, two nations alone, the Egyptians and the Idumeans, are singled out for punishment; or why the Romans should be understood by the Idumeans, when with the same license of lying we could on the contrary assert that under the name of the Idumeans either the Persians, or the Elamites, or the Franks, the Alamanni, and other nations are signified. But let us say that at the time of the Lord's resurrection, or on the day of judgment — for we accept both —, both Egypt and Idumea are to be destroyed, insofar as they are Egypt and Idumea. Egypt is called mesraim, which is interpreted ἐκθλίβουσα, that is, persecuting and afflicting the saints of God; Idumea, earthly, or bloody. Whoever, therefore, has persecuted the people of God, and has given himself to earthly works, and by a multitude of offenses has daily shed innocent blood, the blood, namely, of those whom he deceives, will be brought to perdition, just as we know that the souls of the martyrs too cry out under the altar: How long, O Lord, holy and true, do you not judge and avenge our blood on those who dwell on the earth?
