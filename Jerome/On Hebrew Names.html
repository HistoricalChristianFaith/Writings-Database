On Hebrew Names (Liber interpretationis hebraicorum nominum)

Latin source: Jerome's Book of the Interpretation of Hebrew Names (Liber interpretationis hebraicorum nominum), a list of the Hebrew proper names of each book of the Bible, from Genesis to the Apocalypse and the Epistle of Barnabas, with their meanings, reworked from a Greek onomasticon ascribed to Philo and Origen (written at Bethlehem about 390), from P. de Lagarde's text as reprinted in Corpus Christianorum, Series Latina 72 (Turnhout, 1959), pp. 59–161, verified against the page images and collated with Lagarde's own edition (Göttingen, 1870), with the edition's evident misprints corrected; its critical apparatus is not translated. Each entry gives a name as Jerome spells and inflects it, followed by its interpretations, which keep the case of the name they explain; where the standard English Bible form of the name differs, it follows in square brackets. All square brackets are the translator's. Headings give Jerome's book titles in English; Leviticus and Ruth have no list of their own (their names follow Exodus and Judges), and there is no separate list for Ephesians or 1–2 John. Greek is kept as printed, with an English gloss in square brackets, and the Hebrew letters keep their Hebrew script, with the letter name in brackets. This English translation is released into the public domain.

Preface

Philo, the most eloquent man of the Jews, is shown also by the testimony of Origen to have published a book of Hebrew names and to have joined their etymologies alongside them according to the order of the letters. Since this book is commonly held among the Greeks and has filled the libraries of the world, it was my endeavor to turn it into the Latin language. But I found the copies so discordant among themselves, and the order so confused, that I judged it better to keep silent than to write something worthy of reproof. Therefore, at the urging of the brothers Lupulus and Valerianus, who think that I have made some progress in the knowledge of the Hebrew language, and moved by the usefulness of the matter itself, I have run through the individual volumes in the order of the Scriptures; and, restoring an old building with new care, I think I have done what even the Greeks ought to strive for. Besides, I also give this reminder in the preface: that if anything has been passed over here, the reader should know that it has been reserved for another work. For I now have in hand the books of Hebrew Questions, a new work, and one unheard of up to this point among Greeks and Latins alike; not that I may insolently extol my own study, but that, conscious of my sweat, I may invite those who do not know to read them. If anyone, then, wishes to have both those books and the present volume, and also the Book of Places, which I am about to publish, he makes light of the belching and nausea of the Jews. And lest perhaps, when the building was finished, the last hand should seem to be lacking, I have interpreted the words and names of the New Testament, wishing in part to imitate Origen, whom no one but the unskilled denies to be the teacher of the churches after the apostles. For among the other illustrious monuments of his genius he labored at this too: that what Philo as a Jew had omitted, he as a Christian should fill up.

It must not be supposed straightaway that, wherever names are set down beginning with the letter A, which among the Hebrews is called aleph, this letter alone is the one that is set down. For sometimes they have their beginning from the letters ain, often from he, not seldom from heth, which change their aspirations and sounds. It must be known, therefore, that both in Genesis and in the other books, where a name begins with a vowel, among the Hebrews it begins with different letters (as we said above); but since among us there is no such great diversity of sounds, we are content with a simple pronunciation. Hence it happens that the same words, which among them are not written alike, seem to us to vary in interpretation.
