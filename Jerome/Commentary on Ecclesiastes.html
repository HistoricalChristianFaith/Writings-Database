Commentary on Ecclesiastes

Latin source: Jerome's commentary on Ecclesiastes, in one book with a preface addressed to Paula and Eustochium (written at Bethlehem about 388–389, in memory of Blesilla), from the critical text of M. Adriaen, Corpus Christianorum, Series Latina 72 (Turnhout, 1959), pp. 249–361, verified against the page images and collated with Vallarsi's text as reprinted by Migne (Patrologia Latina 23, Paris, 1845), with the edition's evident misprints corrected; the one phrase Adriaen brackets as spurious is omitted, and his critical apparatus is not translated. Each lemma gives Ecclesiastes in Jerome's own version from the Hebrew; all Scripture, including the renderings of the Septuagint, Aquila, Symmachus and Theodotion that he sets beside it, is translated as Jerome quotes it, from his own Latin. Italics mark the lemmata and the other Scripture he quotes; lines from the poets are in quotation marks. Headings follow Jerome's own division of the text into lemmata, given by chapter and verse. Psalms keep the Latin (LXX) numbering, with the Hebrew number in parentheses. Greek is kept as printed, with an English gloss in parentheses where Jerome gives none, and Hebrew words keep Jerome's own transliteration. This English translation is released into the public domain.

Preface

I remember that almost five years ago, when I was still at Rome and was reading Ecclesiastes with the holy Blesilla, so as to rouse her to contempt of this world and to make her count as nothing everything she saw in the world, I was asked by her to discuss all its obscure passages in the manner of a little commentary, so that she might be able to understand what she was reading without me. And so, since she was snatched away by a sudden death when our work was just in hand, and we did not deserve, O Paula and Eustochium, to have such a companion of our life, and since I, struck by so great a wound, then fell silent, now, settled in Bethlehem, a more august city to be sure, I render to her memory and to you what I owe, advising you briefly of this: that I have followed no one's authority; but in translating from the Hebrew I have adapted myself rather to the usage of the Seventy translators, at least in those things which did not differ much from the Hebrew. At times I have also recalled Aquila and Symmachus and Theodotion, so that I might neither deter the reader's zeal by too much novelty, nor, on the other hand, against my conscience, abandon the fountain of truth and pursue the rivulets of opinions.

1:1

The words of Ecclesiastes, the son of David, king of Jerusalem. The Scriptures teach most plainly that Solomon was called by three names: peaceable (pacificus), that is, Solomon; and Ididia, that is, beloved of the Lord; and the name by which he is now called, Coeleth, that is, Ecclesiastes. Now Ecclesiastes is the name, in the Greek language, for one who gathers an assembly, that is, a church: one whom we can call a preacher (concionator), because he speaks to the people and his speech is directed not specially to one person but generally to all. Further, he was called peaceable and beloved of the Lord because in his reign there was peace and the Lord loved him. For the forty-fourth (45th) and the seventy-first (72nd) psalms too are headed with the title "of the beloved" and "of the peaceable". And although these psalms, since they pertain to the prophecy of Christ and of the Church, go beyond the happiness and the power of Solomon, yet according to the history they were written about Solomon.

He, accordingly, matching the number of his names, published three volumes: Proverbs, Ecclesiastes, the Song of Songs. In Proverbs he teaches the little one and instructs him, as it were, in his duties by means of maxims; which is why his speech is so often addressed again to his son. In Ecclesiastes, however, he trains a man of mature age not to think that anything in the affairs of the world is lasting, but that all that we see is perishable and brief. Finally, in the Song of Songs, he joins the man now perfected, who has trodden the world underfoot and is prepared, to the embraces of the Bridegroom. For unless we first leave our vices and, renouncing the pomp of the world, make ourselves ready and unencumbered for the coming of Christ, we cannot say: Let him kiss me with the kiss of his mouth. Not far from this order of teaching, the philosophers too instruct their followers: first they teach ethics, then they expound physics; and whomever they perceive to have made progress in these, they lead on as far as theology.

This too must be noted with some care: that across the three books the title of the author differs. For in Proverbs it is set down: The proverbs of Solomon, the son of David, king of Israel. But in Ecclesiastes: The words of Ecclesiastes, the son of David, king of Jerusalem; for Israel is superfluous here, and it is wrongly found in the Greek and Latin copies. In the Song of Songs, however, neither son of David nor king of Israel or of Jerusalem is set at the head, but only The Song of Songs of Solomon. For just as Proverbs and elementary instruction belong to the twelve tribes and to all Israel, and as contempt of the world suits none but the citizens of the mother city, that is, the inhabitants of Jerusalem, so the Song of Songs properly concerns those who desire only the things above. With beginners and those making progress, both a father's dignity and the authority of his own kingdom are rightly claimed. But with the perfect, where the disciple is taught not by fear but by love, his own name suffices, and he is an equal teacher and does not know himself to be a king. So much, for now, according to the letter.

But according to the spiritual understanding, the peaceable one, and the beloved of God the Father, and our Ecclesiastes, is Christ, who, having destroyed the middle wall and making void the enmities in the flesh, made both one, saying: My peace I give to you, my peace I leave to you. Of him the Father spoke to the disciples: This is, he says, my beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased; hear him. He is the head of the whole Church; speaking not to the synagogue of the Jews but to the multitude of the nations, he is king of the Jerusalem built of living stones: not of that one of which he himself says: Jerusalem, Jerusalem, you who kill the prophets; and: Behold, your house will be left to you deserted, but of that one by which he forbids us to swear, because it is the city of the great king. This is the son of David to whom the blind men in the Gospel cried out: Have mercy on us, son of David; and the whole crowd resounded with one harmonious voice: Hosanna to the son of David. Accordingly, the word of God does not come to him, as it came to Jeremiah and the other prophets; but because he is rich, and a king, and powerful – he himself, indeed, is the Word and the Wisdom and the other powers – he speaks words to the men of the Church, he imparts words to the apostles, of whom it is sung in the psalm: Their sound went out into all the earth, and their words to the ends of the world. Wrongly, therefore, do some suppose that we are incited by this book to pleasure and luxury, when on the contrary it teaches that all that we see in the world is vain, and that we ought not to seek eagerly after things that perish even while they are held.

1:2

Vanity of vanities, said Ecclesiastes, vanity of vanities, all is vanity. If all the things that God made are very good, how is all vanity, and not only vanity but even vanity of vanities? It is so that, just as in the Song of Songs a song excelling among all songs is shown, so in the vanity of vanities the greatness of the vanity may be displayed. Something of the kind is written in the psalm too: Nevertheless, every man living is all vanity. If a living man is vanity, then a dead man is vanity of vanities. We read in Exodus that the face of Moses was glorified to such a degree that the sons of Israel could not look upon it. That glory the apostle Paul, in comparison with the glory of the Gospel, says is no glory: For neither has it been glorified, he says, that which was glorified in this respect, because of the surpassing glory. We too, then, can in the same way say that heaven, earth, the seas and all that is contained in this circle are indeed good in themselves, but compared with God are as nothing. And just as, if, seeing the little flame of a lamp, I were content with its light, and afterwards, when the sun had risen, I could not make out what had been shining, and saw the lights of the stars too hidden by the radiance of the sun, so, looking at the elements and the manifold variety of things, I do indeed admire the greatness of the works; but, reflecting that all things pass away and that the world grows old towards its end, and that God alone always is what he was, I am compelled to say not once but twice: Vanity of vanities, all is vanity. In the Hebrew, for vanity of vanities, abal abalim is written, which all, apart from the Seventy translators, have likewise translated Ἀτμός ἀτμίδων or ἀτμῶν, which we can call vapor of smoke and a thin breath that quickly dissolves. Thus the frailty and nothingness of the universe is shown by this word. For the things that are seen are temporal, but the things that are not seen are eternal. Or else it is because the creation has been subjected to vanity, and groans and is in travail and waits for the revelation of the sons of God, and now we know in part and we prophesy in part. All things are vain until that which is perfect comes.

1:3

What remains to man in all his labor with which he labors under the sun? After the general sentence that all things are vain, he begins with men: that they sweat in vain in the labor of this world, amassing riches, educating children, canvassing for honor, putting up buildings, and, snatched away by sudden death in the middle of the work, they hear: Fool, this night your soul will be taken from you; and the things you have prepared, whose will they be? All the more so, since out of all their labor they carry nothing with them, but return naked into the earth from which they were taken.

1:4

A generation goes, and a generation comes; and the earth stands for an age. While some die, others are born; and, no longer seeing those whom you had seen, you begin to see those who were not. What is vainer than this vanity: that the earth, which was made for the sake of men, remains, and man himself, the lord of the earth, is suddenly dissolved into dust? Another interpretation: the first generation, that of the Jews, departs, and a generation gathered from the nations succeeds it; but the earth stands only so long as, while the Synagogue withdraws, the whole Church comes in. For when the Gospel has been preached in the whole world, then will be the end. And when the consummation is at hand, heaven and earth will pass away. And pointedly he does not say: The earth stands for ages, but for an age. Moreover, we praise the Lord not for one age, but for ages of ages.

1:5

The sun rises and the sun sets, and leads to its place, and rises there itself. The sun itself, which was given to mortals for light, daily points to the passing of the world by its rising and its setting. After it has dipped its burning wheel in the ocean, it returns by ways unknown to us to the place from which it had come forth; and, the circuit of the night completed, it bursts forth again in haste from its chamber. Now, for what we have set down following the common edition, leads to its place, the Hebrew has soeph, which Aquila interprets εἰσπνεῖ, that is, breathes towards, Symmachus and Theodotion, returns: because, of course, the sun goes back to its place and breathes towards it there, where it had first come forth. And all this is said so as to teach that by the changes of the seasons and the rising and setting of the stars human life slips away and perishes without knowing it. Another interpretation: the sun of justice, in whose wings is healing, rises for those who fear and sets at noon for the false prophets. And when he has risen, he draws us to his place. Where? To the Father, of course. For he came for this purpose: to lift us from earth to heaven and to say: When the Son of man has been lifted up, he will draw all things to himself. Nor is it surprising that the Son draws believers to himself, since the Father himself also draws them to the Son: For no one, he says, comes to me unless the Father, who sent me, brings him. This sun, then, which we have said sets for some and rises for others, both set once for the patriarch Jacob as he went out of the holy land, and rose for him again when he entered the land of promise from Syria. Lot too, when he went out of Sodom and came to the city to which he had been commanded to hasten, went up into the mountain, and the sun came out over Segor.

1:6

It goes to the south wind and turns about to the north wind; turning about in its turning the spirit goes, and the spirit returns to its circles. From this we can judge that in winter-time the sun runs to the southern region and through the summer is near the north, and that it has its beginning not at the autumn equinox but when the west wind first blows, when in spring-time all things burst forth into fruit. As for what he says: Turning about in its turning the spirit goes, and returns to its circles, either he has called the sun itself spirit, because it is a living being and breathes and thrives and completes its yearly orbits by its course, as the poet says: "Meanwhile the sun circles round the great year", and elsewhere: "And the year rolls back upon itself along its own tracks"; or else because it is said of the moon's shining globe too, and of the Titanian stars: "A spirit within sustains them, and Mind, poured through the limbs, moves the whole mass and mingles with the great body"; in that case he is speaking not of the yearly course of the sun but of its daily paths. For by an oblique and broken line it proceeds through the south wind to the north, and so returns to the east. Another interpretation: when the sun runs through the south wind, it is nearer to the earth; when through the north wind, it is lifted on high. Perhaps, then, to those who are pinched by the cold of winter and of tribulations (for from the north wind evils blaze up upon the earth) this sun of justice is nearer; but for those who dwell in the region of the north and are deprived of the summer's heat, he walks far off, and by his circles returns to the place from which he set out. For when he has drawn all things to himself and has illumined all with his rays, the restoration to the original state comes about, and God is all in all. Symmachus interpreted this passage thus: It goes to the south and circles to the north; walking about the wind goes, and by the ways it had circled the wind returns.

1:7

All the torrents go into the sea, and the sea is not filled. To the place from which the torrents come out, there they return, that they may go forth. Some think that the fresh waters which flow into the sea are either consumed by the sun blazing above or are food for the saltness of the sea. Look: our Ecclesiastes, who is also the founder of the waters themselves, says that they go back through hidden veins to the heads of the springs and from the mother abyss always bubble up again at their sources. But the Hebrews think, better, that under the name of the torrents and of the sea men are signified by metaphor, because they return into the earth from which they were taken; and they are called torrents, not rivers, because they quickly fail; and yet the earth is not filled by the multitude of the dead. Further, if we climb to higher things, the turbid waters rightly return into the sea from which they had come into being. And, if I am not mistaken, nowhere is a torrent read in a good sense without an addition. For You will give them to drink of the torrent of your pleasure is said with the addition of your pleasure. And on the contrary the Savior is handed over at the torrent Kidron. And Elijah, in a time of persecution, hides at the torrent Chorat, which itself also dries up. And the insatiable sea is not filled, just as in Proverbs too the daughters of the leech are not.

1:8

All words are weighty; a man will not be able to speak them. The eye will not be satisfied with seeing, and the ear will not be filled with hearing. Not only about physics but about ethics too it is hard to know. Speech cannot unfold the causes and natures of things, nor can the eye behold them as the dignity of the matter demands, nor the ear, though a teacher instruct it, attain to the highest knowledge. For if now we see through a mirror in an enigma, and know in part and prophesy in part, it follows that speech cannot unfold what it does not know, nor the eye see that in which it is blind, nor the ear be filled with that of which it is in doubt. At the same time this too must be noted: that all words are weighty and are learned with great labor; against those who think that knowledge of the Scriptures comes to them while they sit idle and merely wish for it.

1:9-10

What is it that has been? The very thing that will be. And what is it that has been done? The very thing that will be done. And there is nothing new under the sun. It seems to me that he is now speaking in common of the things he enumerated above, generation and generation, the mass of the lands, the rising and setting of the sun, the course of the rivers, the vastness of the ocean, and all that we learn by thought or by sight or by the ears: that there is nothing in the nature of things which has not already been before. For from the beginning of the world men have been born and have died, and the earth, poised upon the waters, has stood firm, and the sun has risen and set. And, not to run through more, it has been granted by God the craftsman to birds to fly, to fish to swim, to land animals to walk, and to serpents to glide. Something like this sentence the comic poet also says: "Nothing is said that has not been said before".

Hence my teacher Donatus, when he was expounding this little verse: "Perish", he said, "those who said our things before us". But if in speech nothing new can be said, how much more in the governance of the world, which was so perfected from the beginning that God rested from his works on the seventh day! I have read in a certain book: If everything that has been made under the sun existed in past ages before it was made, and man was made when the sun had already been created, then man existed before he was made under the sun. But this is refuted by the fact that on this reasoning beasts of burden and gnats and all animals, small and great, would also be said to have existed before the heaven. Unless perhaps he were to answer that it is shown from what follows that the discourse of Ecclesiastes is not about the other animals but about man. For he says: There is nothing new under the sun (of which one may speak and say:) Behold, this is new. But animals do not speak, only man; and if animals were to speak, that would be new, and the sentence that there is nothing new under the sun would be undone.

Is there a word of which it may be said: See, this is new? It has already been in the ages that were before us. Symmachus translated this more plainly: Do you think there is anyone who could say: See, this is new? It has already been done in the age that was before us. Now it agrees with what precedes that nothing new comes about in the world, and that there is no one who could arise and say: behold, this is new; since everything that anyone thinks he is showing as new has already been in former ages. Yet let us not think that the signs and portents and the many things which come about new in the world by the will of God have already been done in former ages, and so give a foothold to Epicurus, who asserts that through innumerable cycles the same things happen, in the same places, and through the same persons. Otherwise Judas too has often betrayed, and Christ has often suffered for us, and all the other things that have been done and are to be will likewise roll round again in the same cycles. But it must be said that by the foreknowledge and predestination of God the things that are to be have already been done. For those who were chosen in Christ before the foundation of the world already existed in former ages.

1:11

There is no remembrance of the first things; and indeed of the last things that are to come, there will be no remembrance of them among those who will be at the last. As forgetfulness hides from us the things that are past, so those who are yet to be born will not be able to know the things that are being done now or are to come, and all things will pass by in silence and be hidden as though they had not been; and that sentence will be fulfilled: Vanity of vanities, all is vanity. For the Seraphim too veil the face and the feet of God for this reason, that the first things and the last are covered. But according to the Seventy translators, who said: There is no remembrance of the first, and indeed of the last who are to come there will be no remembrance of them with those who will be at the last; the sense is the one from the Gospel, that those who are first in this world will be the last of all. And because God, being kind and merciful, remembers even the least, indeed all, he will not give to those who for their own vice have deserved to be last as great a glory as to those who, humbling themselves, chose to be last in the world. And so it is said also in what follows: There is no remembrance of the wise man with the fool for ever.

1:12

I, Ecclesiastes, was king over Israel in Jerusalem. So far, the preface of one discussing all things in general; from here he returns to himself, and teaches who he was and how he came to know all things by experience. The Hebrews say that this book is Solomon's, written as he was doing repentance because, trusting in his wisdom and riches, he had offended God through women.

1:13

And I gave my heart to seeking and considering in wisdom concerning all things that are done under the sun. This evil occupation God has given to the sons of men, that they might be occupied in it. The word anian Aquila, the Seventy and Theodotion translated alike as περισπασμὸν, which the Latin translator rendered as distraction (distentio), because the mind of man, distracted among various cares, is torn apart. Symmachus, however, translated it ἀσχολίαν, that is, occupation. Since, then, it is named quite often in this book, whether we have said occupation, or distraction, or anything else, let everything be referred to the sense given above. Ecclesiastes, then, first of all gave his mind to seeking out wisdom, and, stretching himself beyond what is permitted, he wished to know the causes and reasons why little children are seized by a demon, why shipwrecks swallow up the just and the impious alike; whether these things and things like them happen by chance or by the judgment of God. And if by chance, where is providence? If by judgment, where is the justice of God? Desiring to know these things, he says, I understood that a superfluous care and anxiety, tormenting men in various directions, has been given by God to men, so that they desire to know what it is not permitted to know. Beautifully, however, the distraction is given by God with its cause set down first. For how is it written in the epistle to the Romans: For this reason God handed them over to passions of disgrace? And again: For this reason he handed them over to a reprobate mind, to do what is not fitting. And then: For this reason God handed them over to the desires of their heart, to uncleanness. And to the Thessalonians: Therefore God will send them the working of error. And first the causes are shown why they are given over either to passions of disgrace, or to a reprobate mind, or to the desires of their own heart, or what they have done to receive the working of error. So too in the present case God has given men an evil distraction, that they may be distracted in it, because first, of their own accord and by their own will, they did this or that.

1:14

I saw all the works that are done under the sun, and behold, all is vanity and presumption of spirit. We are compelled by necessity to discuss Hebrew words more often than we would wish. For we cannot know the sense unless we learn it through the words. Routh: Aquila and Theodotion translated it νομήν, Symmachus βόσκησιν. The Seventy translators, however, rendered not the Hebrew word but the Syriac, saying προαίρεσιν. Whether, then, νομὴ or βόσκησις, the word comes from feeding; προαίρεσις sounds rather like will than presumption. What is said, then, is that each one does what he wishes and what seems right to him, and that men are carried in different directions by free will, and that all things under the sun are vain, while we displease one another over the ends of goods and evils. The Hebrew under whose instruction I read through the holy Scriptures used to tell me that the word routh written above signifies in this place affliction and malice rather than feeding and will; and malice not from evil, which is the contrary of good, but in the sense in which it is written in the Gospel: Sufficient for the day is its own malice. This the Greeks call more expressively κακουχίαν (affliction); and he said the sense is: I considered all the things that are done in the world, and found nothing else but vanity and malices, that is, miseries of spirit, by which the soul is afflicted with various thoughts.

1:15

The perverse will not be able to be adorned, and diminution will not be able to be numbered. He who is perverse will not be able to be adorned unless he is first corrected. Straight things receive adornment, crooked things correction. No one is called perverse except one who has been twisted away from what is straight. This is against the heretics, who introduce certain natures that do not admit of healing. And because diminution, that is, what is lacking, cannot be numbered, for that reason only the firstborn of Israel were numbered. But the women, and the slaves, and the little children, and the common crowd out of Egypt, being not the fullness but the diminution of the army, were passed over without number. This too can be the sense: so much malice is at work within the compass of this world that the world can scarcely return to the integral state of good, nor can it easily recover the order and perfection in which it was first established. Another interpretation: when all have been restored to wholeness through repentance, the devil alone will remain in his error. For all things that have been made under the sun have been overturned by his will and his spirit of malignity, while at his prompting sins are heaped upon sins. Finally: so great is the number of those seduced, and of those snatched by him from the Lord's flock, that it cannot be grasped by reckoning.

1:16

I spoke with my heart, saying: Behold, I have become great, and have added wisdom above all who were before me in Jerusalem, and my heart has seen much wisdom and knowledge. Solomon was wiser, not than Abraham and Moses and the other saints, but than those who were before him in Jerusalem. We read in the books of Kingdoms too that Solomon was of great wisdom, and that he asked God for this gift before all others. The eye of a clean heart, then, beholds much wisdom and knowledge; for he does not say: I have spoken much wisdom and knowledge, but: my heart has seen much wisdom and knowledge. For we cannot utter all that we perceive.

1:17

And I gave my heart to know wisdom and knowledge, errors and folly; I came to know that this too is feeding on wind, or presumption of spirit. Contraries are understood through contraries. And the first step of wisdom is to have been free of folly. But no one can be free of folly except one who has understood it. Hence too a great many harmful things have been created in the world, so that, while we avoid them, we may be schooled in wisdom. It was, then, a matter of equal zeal for Solomon to know wisdom and knowledge and, on the other side, errors and folly, so that by seeking the one and turning away from the other his true wisdom might be proved. But in this too, as in the rest, he says that he fed on the winds and was not able to grasp the perfect truth. As for presumption of spirit, or feeding on wind, since it is said quite often in this book, let what we discussed above suffice.

1:18

For in the multitude of wisdom is the multitude of fury; and he who adds knowledge adds grief. The more anyone has attained wisdom, the more indignant he is at being subject to vices and far from the virtues that he seeks. And since the mighty will suffer torments mightily, and from the one to whom more is entrusted more is demanded, therefore he who adds knowledge adds grief, and is saddened with a sorrow according to God, and grieves over his own sins. Hence the Apostle too says: And who is it that makes me glad, except the one who is saddened by me? Unless perhaps this too is to be understood: that the wise man grieves that wisdom lies so hidden and deep, and does not offer itself to minds as light does to sight, but comes forth through certain torments and unbearable labor, by constant meditation and study.

2:1

I said in my heart: Come now, I will test you with joy, and I will see in good: and behold, this too is vanity. After I had found that in the multitude of wisdom and the increase of knowledge there was sorrow and labor and nothing else but a vain and endless struggle, I turned myself to joy, so as to flow with luxury, to gather wealth, to abound in riches and to seize perishing pleasures before I died. But in this too I myself perceived my vanity, since past pleasure does not help the present, and pleasure once drained does not satisfy. Yet not only bodily joy but spiritual joy too is a temptation to the one who possesses it, so that I need a goad to correct me, and an angel of Satan to buffet me, lest I be lifted up. Hence Solomon too says: Riches, he says, and poverty, give me not; and at once he adds: Lest, being filled, I become a liar, and say, Who sees me? For indeed the devil too fell through the abundance of good things. Hence it is written in the Apostle as well: Lest, puffed up with pride, he fall into the judgment of the devil; that is, into such a judgment as that into which the devil also fell. But we can also say this: that spiritual joy, like everything else, is now pronounced vanity because we see it through a mirror and in a riddle. But when it has been revealed face to face, then it will be called by no means vanity, but truth.

2:2

To laughter I said, Madness, and to mirth, Why do you do this? Where we read madness, the Hebrew has molal, which Aquila translated πλάνησιν, that is, error, and Symmachus θόρυβον, that is, tumult. But the Seventy and Theodotion, as in many places, so in this one too agree, and rendered it περιφορὰν, which we, rendering word for word, can call a carrying-around (circumlatio). Just as, then, those who are carried about by every wind of doctrine are unstable and toss this way and that, so those who guffaw with that laughter which the Lord in the Gospel says must be changed into weeping are swept away by the error and whirlwind of the world, not understanding the ruin of their sins nor bewailing their past vices, but thinking brief goods to be everlasting and exulting in things that are more worthy of lamentation. This can also be taken of the heretics, who, resting content in false doctrines, promise themselves glad and prosperous things.

2:3

I considered in my heart to draw my flesh along with wine, and my heart led me into wisdom, and to lay hold on folly, until I should see what good there was for the sons of men which they should do under the sun in the number of the days of their life. I wished to draw out my life in delights and to lull my flesh, free from all cares, with pleasure as if with wine; but my thought and natural reason, which God the author implanted even in sinners, drew me back and led me to seek wisdom and to trample folly underfoot, so that I might see what that good might be which men could do in the course of their life. And he has elegantly compared pleasure to drunkenness. For indeed it intoxicates and overturns the vigor of the soul; and whoever is able to exchange it for wisdom, and (as is found in some copies) to lay hold on spiritual joy, he will be able to come to the knowledge of this matter: what in this life is to be sought, and what avoided.

2:4

I made my works great; I built myself houses; I planted myself vineyards, and so on, down to the place where he says: The eyes of the wise man are in his head, and the fool walks in darkness. Before I discuss each point, it seems useful to me to take everything together in a brief discourse and to gather the sense, as it were, into one body, so that what is said may be understood more easily. All the things that are thought good in the world I had, as a king and a mighty man. I built myself palaces on high; I planted hills and mountains with vines. And so that nothing should be lacking for luxury, I planted myself gardens and orchards, setting out diverse trees, which waters gathered into pools might irrigate from above, so that their greenness might be nourished by unfailing moisture for a longer time. I also had a countless multitude of servants, both bought and home-born, and many flocks of four-footed beasts, of oxen, that is, and of sheep, as many as no king before me had in Jerusalem. And treasures of silver and gold beyond counting were laid up, which the gifts of various kings and the tributes of nations had brought me. Hence it also came about that by excessive wealth I was provoked to greater delights, and choirs of the musical arts sang in concert for me with pipe, lyre and voice, and both sexes waited on me at banquets; but the more these things grew, the more wisdom was lacking. For into whatever pleasure desire had drawn me, I was carried along unbridled and headlong, and I thought this was the fruit of my labors, if I myself were consumed in lust and luxury. At last, coming back to myself and as if waking from a heavy sleep, I looked at my hands, and I saw that my works were full of vanity, full of filth, full of the spirit of error. For nothing that was thought good in the world could I find to be good. Reckoning, therefore, what the goods of wisdom were and what the evils of folly, I accordingly broke out in praise of that man who, reining himself in after his vices, can be a follower of the virtues. For there is a great distance between wisdom and folly, and as far as day is distant from night, so far are virtues separated from vices. And so it seems to me that he who follows wisdom always lifts his eyes to heaven and has his face raised up on high, and contemplates the things that are above his head; but he who is given over to folly and vices moves about in darkness and wallows in ignorance of things. I made my works great; I built myself houses; I planted myself vineyards. He makes his works great who is lifted up to the things above according to the image of the Creator. And he builds houses, so that the Father and the Son may come and dwell in them. And he plants vineyards, to which Jesus may tie his ass.

2:5

I made myself gardens and orchards: I planted in them every fruit-bearing tree. In the house of a rich man there are not only vessels of gold and silver, but also of wood and of earthenware. Gardens too, then, are made for the sake of all the weaker and the infirm. For he who is weak eats vegetables. Trees are planted, not all fruit-bearing, as we have it in the Latin copies, but of every fruit, that is, of diverse crops and fruits, because there are diverse graces in the Church; and one is the eye, another the hand, another the foot, and on those parts of ours that are less honorable we bestow greater honor. Among these fruit-bearing trees I reckon that the first place is held by the tree of life, which is wisdom. For unless it is planted in the middle, the other trees will wither.

2:6

I made myself pools of waters to water from them the forest that brings forth wood. The trees of the forests, the trees of the woods, which are not fruitful, which bear no fruit, are not nourished by the rain of heaven, nor by the waters from above, but by those which are gathered from the streams into pools. For Egypt too, low and lying flat like a garden of vegetables, is irrigated by earthly waters, which come from Ethiopia. But the land of promise, which is mountainous and lifted up, awaits the early and the late rain from heaven.

2:7

I bought menservants and maidservants, and I had home-born servants, and indeed I had a great possession of herds and sheep, above all who were before me in Jerusalem. If we wish, as we said above, to refer Ecclesiastes even now to the person of Christ, we can call his menservants those who have the spirit of fear in servitude and desire spiritual things more than they hold them; and we can call maidservants those souls which are still given over to the body and the earth. The home-born, too, we can call those who are indeed of the Church and go before the menservants and maidservants of whom we have spoken, yet have not yet been granted freedom, nor nobility, by the Lord. But there are others too in the household of Ecclesiastes, like oxen and sheep, for their labor and their simplicity, who without reason and knowledge of the Scriptures do indeed labor in the Church, but have not yet come to the point of deserving to be men and returning to the image of the Creator. Note more carefully that with the menservants and maidservants and home-born servants no multitude is added, but with the oxen and sheep it is said: and there was to me a possession of herd, and of sheep there was much. For in the Church there are more herds than men; more sheep than menservants, maidservants and home-born servants. But what is said at the end:

Above all who were before me in Jerusalem; it does not belong to any great glory of Solomon that he was richer than one single king, his father, since under Saul there was not yet a king reigning in Jerusalem, and it was held by the Jebusites, who had occupied the city itself. And so we must consider more deeply what Jerusalem is, and how Ecclesiastes was richer than all the kings who preceded him in Jerusalem.

2:8

I gathered for myself silver and gold, and the riches of kings and of provinces. I made myself male singers and female singers, and the delights of the sons of men, men to serve wine and women to serve it. Divine Scripture always applies silver and gold to speech and to sense. Hence in the sixty-seventh (68th) psalm too the dove, which is interpreted in the spirit, has silvered wings, more manifest and exposed to sight, but covers its more hidden sense within, in the paleness of gold. And he gathered the riches of kings and of provinces, or regions, into the Church of believers: of those kings of whom the psalmist sings: The kings of the earth stood up, and the princes gathered together into one; and of those regions toward which the Savior commanded eyes to be lifted, because they are already white for the harvest. The riches of kings can also be called the doctrines of the philosophers and the secular sciences; the man of the Church, understanding these carefully, catches the wise in their craftiness, destroys the wisdom of the wise and rejects the prudence of the prudent. The male singers and female singers, too, are those men or women who sing psalms with the spirit and sing psalms with the mind as well. The male singer, as a man both strong and spiritual, sings of higher things. But the female singer still turns about matter, which the Greeks call ὕλην, and cannot lift her voice on high. Wherever, therefore, a woman and the frailer sex is read of in the Scriptures, let us transfer it to the understanding of matter. Hence Pharaoh too does not want the males kept alive, but only the females, who are close to matter. And on the contrary, none of the saints, except very rarely, is said to have begotten females. And Zelophehad alone, who died in his sins, begot all daughters. Jacob, among the twelve patriarchs, is the father of one daughter, and is endangered on her account. The delights of the sons of men are also to be understood of wisdom, which, after the likeness of paradise, has various fruits and manifold pleasures. And of it the command is given: Delight in the Lord, and he will give you the petitions of your heart. And in another place: You will give them drink from the torrent of your pleasure. As for the wine-pourers and wine-pouresses (uini fusores et uini fusitrices) (for, to distinguish it from the masculine, I wanted here to decline it in the feminine, which the Latin tongue does not allow), Aquila translates it as something far different from what lies on the surface. For he named not human beings, that is, men and women, but kinds of vessels, calling them κυλίκιον and κυλίκια (little cup, little cups), what is called in Hebrew sadda and saddoth. Then Symmachus, although he could not render it word for word, is not far from this opinion, saying: Kinds of tables, and settings-out. We must believe, then, that Solomon had pitchers, or cups, or mixing-bowls, set out for the service, adorned with gold and gems; and that from one κυλικίῳ, that is, a mixing-bowl, the wine was drawn into other κυλικίοις, that is, smaller vessels; and that by the hands of the servers the crowd of drinkers received the wine. Since, then, we interpret Ecclesiastes of Christ, and Wisdom in Proverbs, having mixed her bowl, calls those passing by to herself, we must now take the Lord's body as the great mixing-bowl, in which the divinity is not undiluted, as it was in the heavenly places, but for our sake has been tempered with humanity in between; and through the apostles, the smaller κυλίκια, little cups and mixing-bowls, wisdom has been poured out to believers in the whole world.

2:9

And I was made great, and I increased above all who were before me in Jerusalem; and indeed my wisdom stood for me. That Ecclesiastes was made great hardly seems to suit the Lord, unless perhaps we apply to him this: He advanced in wisdom and age and grace. And: For which reason God exalted him. But when he says: Who were before me in Jerusalem, he speaks of those who, before his coming, governed the congregation of the saints and the Church. If we understand the Scriptures spiritually, Christ is richer than all; if only carnally, the Synagogue understands better than the Church. He took away, then, the veil that was laid over the face of Moses, and made us see his face in full light. Further, that wisdom stood: the sense is that even when he was set in the flesh, wisdom remained to him. He who makes progress in wisdom, for him wisdom does not stand still; but he who admits no progress, and does not grow moment by moment, but is always in fullness, he can say: And wisdom stood for me.

2:10

And all that my eyes asked for, I did not take away from them, nor did I keep my heart back from any joy, for my heart rejoiced in all my labor. And this was my portion from all my labor. The eyes of the soul and the gaze of the mind desire spiritual contemplation; the sinner, not knowing it, keeps his heart back from true delight. To this, then, Ecclesiastes gave himself wholly, and he compensated the lightness of tribulation in the world with eternal glory. For this is our portion and everlasting reward, if here we labor for the virtues.

2:11

And I looked at all my works which my hands had made, and at the labor which I had labored in doing them. He who does everything with diligence and caution can say this.

And behold, all is vanity and will of the spirit. As in the other places, he reckons all the things that are under the sun to be worthless, and diverse according to the variety of wills. And there is no abundance under the sun. Christ set his tabernacle in the sun. And so in one who has not yet come to the brightness, order and constancy of the sun, Christ will be able neither to dwell nor to abound.

2:12

And I looked to see wisdom and errors and folly, for who is there among men who can go after the king, before his maker? Since in what precedes, down to the place where Scripture says: The eyes of the wise man are in his head, I had taken everything together in a single discourse, wishing to indicate the sense briefly, and for that reason had touched on some things again according to ἀναγωγὴν (the higher, anagogical sense), I must now expound as I had begun. For in this place the sense differs greatly from the translation of the Seventy. He says, then, that after condemning delights and pleasures he returned to search out wisdom, in which he found more error and folly than true and certain prudence. For a man cannot know the wisdom of his Creator and King as clearly and purely as he knows it who is the Creator. And so even the things we know we rather suppose than hold, and we reckon rather than know what is true.

2:13

And I saw that there is an abundance of wisdom over folly, as there is an abundance of light over darkness. Although, he says, I have seen through to the fact that human wisdom itself is mixed with error and cannot slip into our minds as purely as it is in our King and Creator, yet I have come to know that even in what it is there is a great difference between it and folly, as great as the distance of day from night, of light from darkness.

2:14

The eyes of the wise man are in his head, and the fool walks in darkness. And I came to know that one event will befall them all. He who has come to the perfect man and has deserved to have Christ as his head will always have his eyes on Christ, and, lifting them on high, will never think of lower things. Since these things are so, and there is so great a distance between the wise man and the fool that the one is compared to day and the other to darkness, the one lifts his eyes to heaven and the other bows them to the earth, this thought suddenly crept upon me: why the wise man and the fool are ended by a common death, why the same blows, the same event, the same death, equal distresses press upon both.

2:15-16

And I said in my heart: As is the event of the fool, so will it befall me also, and why have I become wise? And I spoke in my heart, that this too is vanity. For there will be no memory of the wise man with the fool for ever, since, behold, in the days that will come, forgetfulness will cover all things; and how will the wise man die with the fool? I said: the wise man and the fool, the just and the impious, will die by an equal lot, and in this world they will endure all evils by a like event; what then does it profit me that I have followed wisdom and labored more than the rest? Thinking again and carefully going over it with myself in my mind, I found that my opinion was vain. For the wise man and the foolish man will not have a like memory in the future, when the consummation of the universe comes; and they will by no means be held by an equal end, since this one will go to refreshment, that one to punishment. In this place the Seventy translators rendered the Hebrew sense more plainly, although they did not follow the order of the words: And why have I become wise? Then I spoke abundantly in my heart, for the foolish man speaks out of abundance; for this too is vanity, for there is no memory of the wise man with the fool for ever, and so on. That is, convicting his earlier opinion of being foolish, he testified that he had spoken foolishly and had erred, because he had thought so before.

2:17

And I hated life, for the work that is done under the sun is evil to me, for all is vanity and feeding on wind. If the world is set in the evil one, and in this tabernacle the Apostle groans, saying: Wretched man that I am, who will deliver me from the body of this death? rightly does he hate all that is done under the sun. For in comparison with paradise and the blessedness of that life in which we enjoyed spiritual fruits and the delights of the virtues, now we are as if in a workhouse and a prison and the valley of tears, eating our bread in the sweat of our face.

2:18-19

And I hated all my labor which I labor under the sun, for I leave it to the man who will be after me. And who knows whether he will be wise or a fool? and he will be master over all my labor which I have labored, and in which I have become wise under the sun; but this too is vanity. He seems indeed to be treating of riches and wealth, since, according to the Gospel, when we are taken away by sudden death, we do not know what sort of heir we die leaving, whether he who is to enjoy our labor is a fool or a wise man. This happened to Solomon too: for he did not have in Rehoboam a son like himself. From this we understand that not even a son is worthy of his father's inheritance if he is a fool. But to me, contemplating more deeply, he seems to speak of spiritual labor: that a wise man labors day and night in the Scriptures, and composes books, and leaves a memory of himself to posterity, and none the less they come into the hands of fools, who often, according to the perversity of their heart, take from them the seeds of heresies and cavil at the labors of others. For if the discourse of Ecclesiastes is now about bodily riches, what need was there to say of labor and wealth: And he will be master over all my labor in which I have labored, and in which I have become wise under the sun? For what wisdom is it to gather earthly riches?

2:20-23

And I turned, so as to renounce in my heart all my labor which I labored under the sun, for there is a man whose labor is in wisdom and knowledge and virtue, and to a man who has not labored he will give his portion. And indeed this is vanity and great wickedness. For what comes to a man from all his labor, and from the will of his heart, with which he himself labors under the sun? For all his days are days of sorrows and of anger and of cares, and indeed in the night his heart does not sleep; but this too is vanity. Above he speaks of an uncertain heir, of whom it is not known whether he will be a fool or a wise man when he becomes master of another's labors. Now he repeats the same things, it is true, but the sense differs in this, that even if a man leaves his substance and his labors to a son, to a kinsman, to someone he knows, none the less the matter comes round into the same circle: one man enjoys another's labor, and the sweat of the dead is the delight of the living. Let each man consider himself, and he will see with how much labor he composes books; how "often he turns his stylus, about to write again things worthy to be read"; and yet to a man who has not labored he gives his portion. For what, as I have already said before, have wisdom and knowledge and virtue, in which he testified he had labored, to do with the riches of the earth, when it belongs to wisdom and knowledge and virtue to trample on earthly things?

2:24-26

There is no good for a man except that he eat and drink and show his soul good in his labor. And indeed this I saw, that it is from the hand of God. For who will eat, and who will spare, without him? for to the man who is good before him he has given wisdom and knowledge and joy, and to the one who sins he has given anxiety, to increase and to gather what may be given to the good man before the face of God. But this too is vanity and presumption of spirit. After I had treated of everything and had perceived that nothing is more unjust than for one man to enjoy another's labor, then it seemed to me that this is the most just thing in the world, and as it were a gift of God: that a man should enjoy his own labor, drinking and eating, and, as occasion requires, sparing the wealth he has gathered. For indeed it is a gift of God that a just man should be given such a mind that he himself consumes what he has acquired with cares and watchings. Just as, on the contrary, it belongs to the wrath of God against the sinner that he should gather wealth day and night and, never using it, leave it to those who are just in the sight of God. But this too, he says, as I looked at it carefully and saw that all things are ended by death, I judged most vain. So much, for now, according to the letter, lest we should seem to pass over the simple sense entirely and, while we pursue spiritual riches, to despise the poverty of the history. For what good is it, or what kind of gift of God, either to gape after one's own wealth and to snatch in advance a pleasure that is, as it were, fleeing, or to turn another's labor to one's own delights, and to think it a gift of God if we enjoy the miseries and labors of others? It is good, therefore, to take the true food and the true drink, which we find in the divine books, from the flesh and blood of the Lamb. For who can either eat or, when there is need, spare, without God? He commanded that what is holy is not to be thrown to dogs, and teaches how food is to be given to fellow servants in due season, and, according to another sense, when honey is found, to eat only as much as suffices. And beautifully does God give to the good man wisdom and knowledge and joy: for unless he has been good and has first corrected his ways by his own free choice, he does not deserve wisdom and knowledge and joy, according to what is said elsewhere: Sow for yourselves in righteousness, gather the vintage in the fruit of life, light for yourselves the light of knowledge. For righteousness must first be sown, and the fruit of life reaped, and afterwards the light of knowledge will be able to appear. As, then, God gave to the man who is good before him wisdom and the rest, so, leaving the sinner to his own free choice, he made him gather riches and stitch together, from this side and that, pillows of perverse doctrines. And when the holy man, pleasing to God, sees these, he understands that they are vain and put together by presumption of spirit. Nor should we wonder that he said: To the sinner he has given anxiety, and so on. For this must be referred to the sense of which I have often treated: that anxiety, or distraction, was given to him because he was a sinner, and that the cause of the distraction is not in God, but in him who first sinned of his own will.

3:1

For all things there is a time, and a time for every thing under heaven. In what went before he taught the uncertain and fluctuating state of the human condition; now he wants to show this: that all things in the world are contrary to one another, and that nothing stands forever, of those things at least which are under heaven and within time, because the other, spiritual substances are contained neither by heaven nor by time.

3:2

A time to give birth and a time to die. A time to plant and a time to pluck up what has been planted. No one doubts that both the rising and the passing away of men is known to God and fixed by him, and that to give birth is the same as to plant, and to die the same as to pluck up what has been planted. But since we read in Isaiah: From fear of you, O Lord, we have conceived and been in labor and given birth, this must be said: that in the perfect man that offspring which was born of fear dies, once he has begun to love God. For perfect indeed love casts out fear. The Hebrews understand all this that is written about the contrariety of times, up to that place where he says: A time of war and a time of peace, as concerning Israel. And since there is no need to set out verse by verse what they interpret and think, I shall touch on it briefly, leaving a broader discussion of this to the reader's own talent. There was a time for begetting and planting Israel, a time for dying and being led into captivity. A time for killing them in Egypt, and a time for freeing them from Egypt. A time for destroying the temple under Nebuchadnezzar, and a time for building it under Darius. A time for mourning the overthrow of the city, and a time for laughing and dancing under Zerubbabel, Ezra and Nehemiah. A time for scattering Israel, and a time for gathering it into one. A time for the people of the Jews to be bound about God like a girdle and a belt, and a time for leading them into the Babylonian captivity, and for their rotting there beyond the Euphrates. Read Jeremiah's περίζωμα (loincloth). A time for seeking them and preserving them, a time for losing and a time for casting away. A time for rending Israel, and a time for sewing it together again. A time for the prophets to be silent, now in the Roman captivity, and a time for them to speak, then, when even in a hostile land they did not lack God's consolation and address. A time of love, with which he loved them formerly under the fathers, and a time of hatred, because they laid hands on Christ. A time of battle, now, while they do not repent, and a time of peace in the future, when, as the fullness of the Gentiles enters in, all Israel will be saved.

3:3

A time to kill and a time to heal. Both the time for killing and the time for healing belong to him who says: I will kill, and I will make alive. He heals by calling to repentance. He kills, according to that sense: In the morning I slew all the sinners of the land.

A time to destroy and a time to build. We cannot build good things unless we have first destroyed bad ones. Just as the word was given to Jeremiah by God that he should first root out and dig under and destroy, and afterwards build and plant.

3:4

A time to weep and a time to laugh. Now is the time for weeping, and in the future the time for laughing: Blessed indeed are those who weep, for they shall laugh.

A time to mourn and a time to dance. That is why those are rebuked in the Gospel to whom the Lord says: We lamented to you, and you did not mourn; we sang, and you did not dance. We must mourn at present, so that afterwards we may be able to dance that dance which David danced before the ark of the covenant; and, displeasing Saul's daughter, he pleased God the more.

3:5

A time to scatter stones and a time to gather stones. I wonder how an eloquent man came to say something ridiculous in this place, saying: The discourse is about the destruction and the building of Solomon's houses, in that men now destroy, now build; some gather stones to construct buildings, others destroy what has been built, according to that line of Horace: "He pulls down, he builds, he changes square for round. He seethes, and is out of keeping with the whole order of life." Whether he said this rightly or wrongly I leave to the reader's judgment. Let us follow the order of the earlier explanation, saying that there is a time for scattering and gathering stones, according to what is written in the Gospel: God is able from these stones to raise up children to Abraham. In that there was a time for scattering the Gentile people, and a time for gathering them again into the Church. I have read in a certain book (following, however, the Seventy translators, who said: A time to throw stones and a time to gather) that the severity of the old law was tempered by the grace of the Gospel. For the law, rigid, unkind, unsparing, kills the sinner; the grace of the Gospel has mercy and calls to repentance. And that this is the time of throwing stones, or of gathering them: that stones are thrown in the law, and gathered in the Gospel. Whether this was said truly or not, let it be charged to its author.

A time to embrace and a time to be far from embracing. According to the simple understanding the sense is plain. With the Apostle agreeing in these same words: Do not defraud one another, unless perhaps by consent for a time, that you may be free for prayer, attention must be given to children, and in turn to continence. Or else, that there was a time for embracing, when that sentence was in force: Increase and multiply and fill the earth. And a time to be far from embracing, when there followed: The time is short. It remains that those who have wives be as though they had none. But if we wish to climb to higher things, we shall see that wisdom embraces her lovers: Honor indeed her, he says, and she will embrace you; and holds them within her arms and her bosom in a closer embrace. Further, since the human mind cannot always be stretched toward the sublime and think of divine and higher things, nor be continually in the contemplation of heavenly things, but must at times indulge the necessities of the body, therefore there is a time for embracing wisdom and holding her more closely, and a time for relaxing the mind from gazing on wisdom and from her embrace, so that we may attend to the care of the body and to those things which our life needs, without sin.

3:6-7

A time to acquire and a time to lose. A time to keep and a time to cast away. Under different words the sense is now the same as above and below, where he says: A time to destroy and a time to build. And then: A time to rend and a time to sew. For just as the Synagogue is destroyed so that the Church may be built, and a rending is made from the law so that the Gospels may be sewn together, which the individual evangelists accomplished, sewing together testimonies of the Lord's coming from the law and the prophets, so too there was a time for seeking and keeping Israel, a time for losing and casting him away. Or certainly a time for seeking the people from the Gentiles, and a time for losing the people of the Jews. A time for keeping those who believe from the nations, and a time for casting off the unbelievers from Israel.

A time to be silent and a time to speak. I think that the Pythagoreans, whose discipline is to be silent for five years and afterwards, once instructed, to speak, drew the origin of their rule from here. Let us too, therefore, learn first to be silent, so that afterwards we may open our mouths to speak. Let us keep silence for a fixed time and hang on the words of our instructor. Let nothing seem right to us except what we learn, so that after long silence we may become masters instead of disciples. But now, through the vice of the ages sliding daily for the worse, we teach in the churches what we do not know. And if by the arrangement of our words, or by the prompting of the devil, who is the abettor of errors, we have stirred up the applause of the people, against our own conscience we suppose that we know what we have been able to persuade others of. There is no art that we learn without a teacher; this one alone is so cheap and easy that it needs no instructor.

3:8

A time to love and a time to hate. A time to love, after God, children, wife and relatives, and a time to hate them in martyrdom, when family affection, become an enemy, assails those who stand unbending for the confession of Christ. Or certainly a time to love the law and the things which had been commanded by the law, circumcision, sacrifices, the sabbath, new moons; and a time to hate them, when the grace of the Gospel took their place. This too can be said: that since we now see through a mirror in an enigma, it is the time for loving present things, and in the future a time will come when, seeing face to face and advancing to better things, we shall begin to hate and despise what we love.

A time of war and a time of peace. As long as we are in the present world, it is a time of war; when we have departed from the world, the time of peace will come. For the place of God is in peace, and our city Jerusalem has taken its name from peace. Let no one, then, think himself secure now; in the time of war we must gird ourselves and take up the apostolic arms, so that one day, as victors, we may rest in peace.

3:9-11

What abundance is there for the one who works, in the things in which he labors? I have seen the occupation which God has given to the sons of men, to be occupied in it. He has made all things good in their time, and indeed he has given the world into their hearts, so that man may not find the work which God has made from the beginning to the end. I am not unaware of what has been said by many in this place: that God for this reason granted occupation in the present world even to the teachers of perverse doctrines, lest the mind of man should grow sluggish in idleness; and that this is the good which God has made in its time, and that they are nonetheless unable to comprehend nature and the knowledge of things. But to me it was explained thus by the Hebrew who instructed me in the Scriptures: Since all things slip away in their own time, and there is a time for destroying or building, for weeping and laughing, for being silent and speaking, and the rest that has been said about time, why do we strive and strain in vain, and suppose the labors of a brief life to be everlasting? Nor are we content, according to the Gospel, with the evil of the day, and to take no thought for tomorrow. For what more can we have in this world by laboring more, in which only this has been given to men by God: that each, by pursuing different things, should have things in which he could instruct and exercise himself? For all that God has made is good, but good in its time. It is good to wake and to sleep; and yet it is not good always to wake or always to sleep, because each thing is good in its turn, according to God's disposition, when there is need of it. God also gave the world to men to inhabit, that they might enjoy the varieties of the seasons and not inquire into the causes of natural things, how all things were created, why from the beginning of the world to its consummation he has made this or that grow, remain, change.

3:12-13

I have known that there is no good except to rejoice and to do good in one's life. And indeed every man who eats and drinks and shows good in all his labor, this is from the gift of God. For this reason man was given to the world as a tenant and a guest: that he might enjoy the brief time of his life and, the hope of a longer age cut off, might look on all he possesses as one about to set out for other places, and might do what good he can in his life, and not torment himself in vain with thoughts of amassing wealth. Nor let him think that he can gain more from his labor than food and drink and whatever of his wealth he has spent on good works; this alone is the gift of God. By these words we are not urged, as some suppose, to luxury and delights and to despair, like animals, according to that saying of Isaiah: Let us eat and drink, for tomorrow we shall die; but, according to the Apostle: Having food and clothing, with these we are content. And whatever we can have beyond that, we spend on feeding the poor and on largess to the needy. Further, since the flesh of the Lord is true food, and his blood is true drink, according to ἀναγωγήν (the higher, anagogical sense) this alone is the good we have in the present world: if we feed on his flesh and drink his blood, not only in the mystery but also in the reading of the Scriptures. For the true food and drink which is taken from the word of God is the knowledge of the Scriptures. Nor let anyone think that what Balaam prophesied: There will be no labor in Jacob, nor sorrow in Israel, is contrary to this, which is said to be of the gift of God: If anyone eats and drinks and shows good in all his labor. For many are the tribulations of the righteous. And the Apostle too complains of them, saying that he has sweated in labor and sorrow. But when the Lord has freed us from them in the future: There will be no labor in Jacob, nor sorrow in Israel. And just as we read: Blessed are those who weep, for they shall laugh, and our laughter will follow the words of Job prophesying: Of the true, moreover, the mouth will be filled with joy, so now we enjoy our labor in good works, by which we are hemmed in and pressed, so that afterwards we may cease to labor.

3:14

I have known that all things which God has made, they shall be forever; to them nothing can be added, and from them nothing can be taken away; and God has made it so that they may fear before his face. There is nothing in the world that is new. The course of the sun and the changes of the moon, and the dryness or greenness of the earth and the trees, were born and grew together with the world itself. And for this reason God has governed all things by a fixed reckoning, and has ordered the elements to serve human uses, so that men, seeing these things, may understand that there is a providence, and may fear before the face of God, while from the evenness, course, order and constancy of things they understand the Creator. For the invisible things of God are clearly seen, being understood through the things that have been made; his eternal power also and divinity. But if, once the first sense is finished, we wish to read as from a new beginning: And God has made it so that they may fear before his face, this is the sense: God has made all these things so that men may fear to swerve from what God has once ordained into something else. And he tempered it beautifully, saying: That they may fear before his face. For the countenance of the Lord is upon those who do evil.

3:15

What is it that has been? The very thing that is; and the things that are to come have already been; and God will seek him who suffers persecution. Whether past, or present, or future, all the things we see both have been, and are, and will be the very same. The sun which now rises both was before we were in the world, and will rise after we have died. We have named the sun so that from it we may understand that the other things too are the same as they were. And if they seem to perish through the condition of death, they do not perish, because they grow up again, revived, and nothing dies forever, but is reborn, and comes back to life as if with a kind of interest. For this is what he says: And God will seek him who suffers persecution; which is better said in Greek, καὶ ὁ Θεὸς ζητήσει τὸ διωκόμενον (and God will seek what is driven away), that is, what has passed, what has been driven out, what has ceased to be. But if this is said of all the things that are in the world, there is no doubt about man that, having died, he is reborn. But if anyone prefers to read it as from its own beginning, and God will seek him who suffers persecution, let him use this testimony in the persecution by the Gentiles, to console the one who perseveres in martyrdom. And since, according to the Apostle, all who wish to live piously in this world suffer persecution, let them have consolation, because God seeks the one who suffers persecution, just as he requires the blood of the slain, and he came to seek what had perished, and carried the straying sheep back to the flock on his shoulders.

3:16-17

And further I saw under the sun the place of judgment: there was impiety; and the place of justice: there was iniquity. I said in my heart: God will judge the just and the impious, for there is a time for every will, over every deed, there. The sense is plain, but it is wrapped in the cloud of the translation. Under this sun, he says, I sought truth and judgment, and I saw that even among the very benches of the judges it is not truth that prevails, but bribes. Or otherwise: I supposed that some justice was done in the present world, and that either the pious man now received according to his merit, or the impious man was punished for his crime; and on the contrary I found the opposite of what I thought. For I saw both the just man suffer many evils here and the impious man reign in return for his crime. But afterwards, conversing with my heart and reflecting, I understood that God does not judge piecemeal and one by one, but reserves judgment for the time to come, so that all may be judged together and there receive according to their will and their works. For this is what he says: And a time for every will, and over every deed, there; that is, in the judgment, when the Lord begins to judge, then there will be truth; now injustice rules in the world. We read something similar in Wisdom, which is entitled of Sirach: Do not say, What is this, or what is that? for all things will be sought out in their time.

3:18-21

I said in my heart concerning the speech of the sons of man, that God distinguishes them, and to show that they themselves are beasts to themselves, for the event of the sons of man and the event of the cattle, one event is theirs. As the death of the one, so also the death of the other, and there is one spirit for all, and man has nothing more than the cattle, for all is vanity. All go to one place; all were made from the soil, and all will return to the soil. And who knows whether the spirit of the sons of men itself ascends upward, and whether the spirit of the cattle itself descends downward into the earth? It is not to be wondered at that in the present life there is no distinction between the just and the impious, and that virtues avail nothing, but all things are rolled about by an uncertain event, when even between cattle and man, as regards the baseness of the body, there seems to be no difference: the same condition of being born, one lot of dying; alike we come forth into the light, alike we are dissolved into dust. But if this seems to be the distinction, that the spirit of man ascends into heaven and the spirit of the cattle descends into the earth, on what sure authority have we learned this? Who can know whether what is hoped for is true or false? Yet he says this not because he thinks that the soul perishes with the body, or that one and the same place is prepared for brutes and for man, but because before the coming of Christ all were led alike to the underworld. Hence Jacob too says that he will go down to the underworld. And Job complains that the pious and the impious are held fast in hell. And the Gospel testifies that, with a chasm set between them, both Abraham with Lazarus and the rich man in torments are in the underworld. And in truth, before Christ with the thief unbarred that flaming wheel and fiery sword and the doors of paradise, heavenly things were closed, and an equal baseness confined the spirit of cattle and of man. And although the one seemed to be dissolved and the other kept in store, yet it made little difference whether one perished with the body or was held in the darkness of hell.

Let us go back over the particulars, and discuss them briefly in their order in the commatic manner: I said in my heart concerning the utterance of the sons of man, that God might choose them. This alone, he says, God willed to be the difference between men and beasts: that we speak, they are dumb; we bring forth our will in speech, they are numb in silence. And though we differ from the brutes only by speech, yet it is shown to us that, as regards the frailty of the body, we are cattle. As the beast dies, so dies man too, and there is one breath for all, and this air by which we are nourished. For this is what he says: And one spirit for all, and man has nothing more than the cattle. And lest we should think that this was said also of the soul, he added: All were made from the earth, and will return to the earth. But from the earth nothing else was made except the body. And it is said pointedly of the body: You are earth, and to earth you will return. But as for what seems to be blasphemous: Who knows whether the spirit of the sons of man itself ascends upward, and whether the spirit of the beast itself descends downward into the earth? He is not contending that between cattle and man there is no difference as regards the dignity of the soul, but by adding the word who he wished to show the difficulty of the matter. For the pronoun who is always taken in the holy Scriptures to mean not the impossible but the difficult. As there: Who will declare his generation? And in the psalm: Lord, who ascends into your tabernacle, and into your holy mountain? and the rest that follows. And in Jeremiah, although in the Hebrew it is otherwise: He is also a man, and who knows him? Between men and beasts, then, this is the only difference: that the spirit of man ascends into heaven, and the spirit of the beast descends into the earth and is dissolved with the flesh; provided, however, that some man of the Church, learned in the heavenly disciplines, is a sure asserter of this matter, doubtful as it seems. So much, for now, according to the letter.

But as far as the spiritual understanding is concerned: For men and beasts the Lord will save; and in another place: A beast, he says, am I before you; and in all the prophets men and cattle are said to be saved in Jerusalem, and the land of promise to be filled with cattle and herds. Who knows whether the holy man, who is worthy of the name of man, ascends into heaven, and whether the sinner, who is called a beast, descends into the earth? For it can happen, through the uncertain and slippery state of this life, that both the just man falls and the sinner rises; and it sometimes comes about that one who is more rational and learned in the Scriptures, that is, a man, does not live circumspectly and as befits his knowledge, and is led down to the underworld, while someone simpler and more rustic, who might be called a beast in comparison with a man, lives better and is crowned with martyrdom, and becomes a tenant of paradise.

3:22

And I saw that there is no good except that a man will rejoice in his work, for this is his portion. For who will bring him, that he may see what is to be after him? In place of what we have set down: That he may see what is to be after him, Symmachus translated more clearly, saying: That he may see the things that are to be after these. There is nothing good, therefore, in this life, except that a man rejoices in his work, giving alms and preparing for himself treasures to come in the kingdom of heaven. This is the only portion we have which neither thief nor robber nor tyrant can take away, and which follows us after death. For once this life has been dissolved, we cannot again enjoy our labors, or know what will be in the world afterwards. Otherwise: Disturbed by my former error, in that I supposed there was no difference between men and beasts, I was led by a wrong opinion into this view, so that I said there was no other good than to seize the pleasure of the present. For once death had dissolved us, we could not enjoy the things from which we had departed ungrateful. Others have referred what he says: For who will bring him, that he may see the things that are to be after him, to this understanding, so as to say: It is better for a man to enjoy his labors, because this alone he could take away from his substance. For when death comes, he does not know with what kind of heir he will die, whether a worthy or an unworthy one will enjoy his wealth.

4:1

And I turned, and I saw all the oppressions that are done under the sun; and behold, the tears of those who suffer oppression, and there is none to comfort them; and in the hands of those who oppress them, strength: and there is no comforter for them. After this thought I turned my mind and my eyes in that direction, to see the oppressors and those who suffer oppression. And behold, these, who are unjustly crushed by the more powerful, protesting the outrage of the thing with tears, which alone they are allowed to have in calamities, cannot find a comforter. And so that the misery may be greater and the grief past consoling, they see the oppressors stronger in their iniquities. And this is the reason why they cannot be comforted. David treats this passage more fully in the seventy-second (73rd) psalm, and Jeremiah in his volume.

4:2-3

And I praised the dead who are already dead above the living, whoever themselves live until now. And better than these two is he who is not yet born, who has not yet seen the evil work that is done under the sun. In comparison with the miseries that press upon mortals in this world, I judged the dead happier than the living, according to that saying of Job when he discourses on the underworld: There the weary in body have rested, with those who had been bound, now secure, not hearing the voice of the taskmaster. But better than these two, namely the living and the dead, is he who is not yet born. For the one still suffers evils, the other has escaped naked, as it were, from a shipwreck. Moreover, he who is not yet born is happier in this, that he has not yet experienced the evils of the world. But he says this, not because he who is not yet born exists before he is born and is happier in this, that he has not yet been weighed down by a body; but because it is better not to exist at all, and to have no sense of one's being, than either to exist or to live unhappily. So too the Lord speaks of Judas, signifying his future torments: It were better for that man not to be born; that is, it would have been better for him not to exist at all than to suffer eternal tortures. Others, however, understand this passage thus, saying that those who are dead are better than those who live, even though they were sinners before. For the living are still in battle and are held back as if shut up in the prison of the body; but those who have met death are now secure and have ceased to sin. Just as John too, than whom there was none greater among those born of women, is less than he who is least in the kingdom of heaven and, freed from the burden of the body, does not know how to say with the Apostle: Wretched man that I am, who will free me from the body of this death? And that he who is not yet born, and has not seen the evils by which men are weighed down in the world, is better than these two. For our souls, before they descend to these bodies, dwell among those above, and are blessed for as long as they are held in the heavenly Jerusalem and in the choir of angels.

4:4

And I saw all labor and at the same time all the excellence of work; that the rivalry of a man is from his companion; and indeed this is vanity and presumption of spirit. I turned myself again to other things, and I saw all the strength and glory of those who labor, and I discovered that the good of one man is the evil of another, while the envious man is tormented by another's happiness and the glorious man lies open to plots. For what is more vain, what is so much a nothing, like spirit, than that men do not weep for their own miseries or mourn their own sins, but envy those who are better than themselves?

4:5

The fool has folded his hands and has eaten his own flesh. This is the man who in Proverbs too is described as the sluggard, holding his breast with his hands. To him want comes like a swift runner, and because of excessive hunger (though let it be taken as said hyperbolically) he eats his own flesh. He thinks it better to have one handful of meal and to live idle and sluggish than to fill both hands by laboring. But the whole of what he discusses aims at this: to show both that the man who labors and has something lies open to envy in the world, and again that the man who wants to live quietly is crushed by want, and that both are miserable, since the one is endangered on account of his wealth, the other is worn out with need on account of his want. Or certainly thus. He who envies another's happiness, and is swept along as if by a frenzy of spirit, and has received envy into his bosom and nourished it in his breast: this man eats his soul and his own flesh. For the happier he sees the man he envies, the more he himself pines and wastes away, and little by little melts away with jealousy and spite. Otherwise: hands are often taken for works, as there: The word of the Lord that came by the hand of Haggai; whether of that prophet or of this one, because he did such works that he proved worthy that the word of the Lord should come in his work. With this agrees also that saying of David: Who teaches my hands for battle. The fool, then, has folded his hands, that is, he drew them in and would not stretch them out; so he does not eat the labors of his hands, which he does not even have, but his own flesh, living according to the wisdom of the flesh and feeding on the works of the flesh.

4:6

Better is a full handful with rest than a fullness of hands of labor and presumption of spirit. Better is a little to the just than the many riches of sinners. And in Proverbs: Better is a small receiving with justice than much produce with iniquity. Elegantly, justice has rest, iniquity labor. And because the singular number is always taken in a good sense, the double in a bad one, therefore the one handful has rest, and the two hands are full of labor.

4:7-8

And I turned, and I saw vanity under the sun. There is one, and there is not a second, and indeed he has neither son nor brother, and there is no end to all his labor. And indeed his eye is not satisfied with riches; and for whom do I labor, and defraud my soul of good? But this too is vanity and the worst distraction. I turned to others and saw them laboring more than is necessary, gathering wealth by right and by wrong and not using what they had gathered, having everything, brooding over their riches, keeping them for another and not enjoying their own labor; above all when they have neither son, nor brother, nor kinsman, so that the labor might seem dutiful, as kept for their own people. And so I discovered that nothing is more vain than that man who gathers riches, not knowing to whom he will leave them. This, indeed, following the interpretation above, we can also understand of those who write books and leave them behind to fastidious readers. Some interpret this passage, from the point where he says: There is one and there is not a second, of the Savior, because alone and without any companion he came down to save the world. And although there are many sons of God, and they are called his brothers by adoption, yet none has proved worthy to be joined with him in this work. Of his labor there is no end, as he bears our vices and sins and suffers for us; and his eye will not be satisfied with riches, as he always longs for our salvation, and the more he sees anyone sin, the more he exhorts him to repentance.

4:9-12

Better are two than one, who have a good reward in their labor, because, if one falls, he will raise up his partner. And woe to the one, when he has fallen, and there is not a second to raise him up. And indeed, if two sleep, there will also be warmth for them; and one, how will he be warmed? And if one prevails against him, two will stand against him, and a threefold cord is not easily broken. After the miseries of solitude, in which that man was rebuked who torments himself in acquiring wealth without a sure heir, the discourse now turns to companionship. And it is told what good there is in the company of friends and in a shared comfort: that the fall of the one is lifted up by the help of the other, and that he who has a faithful friend gets through his household cares, and also the very rest of the night, better than he who broods only over the wealth he has acquired. And if some stronger enemy also rises up against one of them, the weakness of the one is upheld by the comfort of the other friend. And as much as two surpass one, if they are joined in love, so much more again does the company of three avail. For true charity, violated by no spite, grows in strength as much as it is increased in number. And let these things be said for now in the simple sense.

But since in the earlier place we set down the understanding of certain men concerning Christ, the rest too must be discussed in the same order. It is better for two to be together than one. For it is better to have Christ dwelling in oneself than to lie open alone to the plots of the adversary. For the reward of companionship is shown at once in the very usefulness of the fellowship. For if one falls, Christ raises up his partner. Woe indeed to him who, when he has fallen, does not have Christ in him to raise him up. And if one has also slept, that is, if he has been dissolved by death and has had Christ with him, warmed and quickened he will come to life again the sooner. And if the devil, the stronger in assault, has stood against a man, the man will stand, and Christ too will stand for his man, for his companion. Not that the strength of Christ alone against the devil is weak, but because free will is left to man, and when we strive, he himself becomes the stronger in the fight.

And if the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit also come, this companionship is not quickly broken. But what is not quickly broken will nevertheless at some time be broken. For in Judas the apostle too there was this threefold cord; but because after the morsel Satan entered into him, this cord was broken apart. Further, as to what he said above: And indeed if two sleep, there will also be warmth for them: and one, how will he be warmed? Let us take an example from Elisha, who himself drew himself together upon the boy and slept and warmed his little body and so brought him to life, rising again. Unless, then, Christ has slept with us and rested in death, we cannot receive the warmth of eternal life.

4:13-16

Better is a boy poor and wise than a king old and foolish, who does not know how to provide for the future. For out of the house of the bound he goes forth to be king, because even in his kingdom he was born poor.

I saw all the living who walk under the sun with the second youth, who will rise up in his place. There is no end to all the people, to all who were before them. And indeed the last will not rejoice in him; but this too is vanity and presumption of spirit, Symmachus rendered this passage thus: Better is a poor man with wisdom than a king old and foolish, who did not know how to guard against the turn of fortune. For the one went out of prison to reign; but the other, though he was born a king, was crushed by poverty. I saw all the living who walk under the sun with the second youth, who rose up in his place. Without limit is all the people that was before both: and those who come after will not rejoice in him. But this too is a breath and a feeding on wind. My Hebrew, whom I often mention, when he was reading Ecclesiastes with me, testified that Baracchiba, whom they admire above all, had handed down the following on the present passage. Better is the inner man, who arises in us after the fourteenth year, the year of puberty, than the outer man, who was born of his mother's womb, who does not know how to withdraw from vice, and who came out of the house of the bound, namely the mother's womb, for this, that he might reign in vices. He also, in his own power, has been made poor by committing every evil. I saw those who lived in the former man and afterwards dwelt with the second man, namely the one who was begotten in place of his former predecessor; and I understood that all had sinned in the former man before, at the birth of the second, there came to be two men. But those who have turned to better things, and these, after the letter of the philosophers, leaving the left-hand path, have pressed on to the right-hand stroke and have followed the second, that is, the last man, will not rejoice in him, that is, in the former. These two men both the Apostle attests and Leviticus does not pass over in silence: Man, man if he wishes this or that. The holy man Gregory, bishop of Pontus, a hearer of Origen, in his Metaphrase of Ecclesiastes understood this passage thus: But I prefer a young man poor and wise to an old and foolish king, into whose mind it never came that it is possible for one of those whom he had bound to go out of prison to the kingdom, and for himself to fall thereafter from his unjust power. For it sometimes happens that those who have been under the wise young man are without grief, yet in such a way that they had earlier lived under the old king. For those who were born afterwards, since they did not know the past evils, cannot even praise the young man who rose up afterwards, led away by perverse opinion and by the onset of the adversary spirit.

The Laodicean translator, striving to express great matters in brief speech, spoke here too in his usual manner: The discourse of Ecclesiastes, he says, is now about the change of goods into evils, as he tries to portray the foolish man, who, giving no thought to the future, delights in present and perishable things as though they were great and everlasting. And after the various things that tend to befall men in their life and to change, he brings in, as it were, a general judgment about death: that a countless multitude perishes and is little by little consumed and passes away, each one leaving another in his place, and again another, as the successor dies.

Origen and Victorinus did not think very differently from each other. After that general judgment, which is plain to all, that a poor and wise youth is better than an old and foolish king, and that it often happens that the one, through his wisdom, going out even from the king's prison, rules in place of the perverse master, and the foolish king loses the rule he held, they interpreted this passage of Christ and the devil, taking the poor and wise boy to be Christ. A boy, according to that saying: It is a great thing for you to be called my boy; poor, because he became poor when he was rich; and wise, because he advanced in age and wisdom and grace before God and men. He was born in the kingdom of the old man. And for that reason he says: If my kingdom were of this world, my servants would surely strive for me, that I should not be handed over to the Jews. But now my kingdom is not of this world. In the kingdom, then, of that old and foolish king, who showed him all the kingdoms of the world and their glory, the most excellent boy was born, and from the house of the fettered, of whom Jeremiah speaks in Lamentations, saying: That he might humble under his feet all the bound of the earth, he went forth to the kingdom, and went away into a far country, and after some time returned as king against those who did not want him to reign. With a prophetic spirit, then, Ecclesiastes saw that all the living who can be partakers of the youth who says: I am the life, leave the old and foolish king and follow Christ. And at the same time two peoples from Israel are signified: the former, which existed before the coming of the Lord, and the latter, which is to receive Antichrist in place of Christ; the meaning being that the former was not wholly cast off, since indeed the first Church was gathered from the Jews and the apostles; and that at the end the Jews, who are to receive Antichrist in place of Christ, will not rejoice in Christ.

4:17

Guard your foot when you go into the house of God, and draw near, to hear. For the gift of the foolish is sacrifice, because they do not know that they do evil. He gives precepts for life, and does not want us to stumble as we go to the Church. For it is not entering the house of God, but entering without stumbling, that deserves praise. And if it belonged to all who are in the Church of God to hear the word, he would never have added: and draw near, to hear. Indeed Moses alone came near to hear God; the rest were not able to approach. Fools, not knowing that this is the remedy of sin, think that they can make satisfaction to God by the offering of gifts, and do not know that this too is an evil and a sin: to want to amend what they have done not by obedience and good works, but by gifts and victims. With this agrees what is said elsewhere: Obedience above sacrifice. And: I desire mercy and not sacrifice.

5:1-2

Do not be hasty with your mouth, and let your heart not hasten to bring forth a word in the sight of God, because God is in heaven, and you upon earth. Therefore let your words be few, because a dream will come in a multitude of anxiety, and the voice of the foolish man in a multiplication of words. Most people think that in the present passage this is commanded: that we should not lightly promise anything before God, and vow, without considering our strength, things we cannot fulfil. For God is at hand and present, and although he is in heaven and we seem to be on earth, yet he hears what we say, and our foolishness is convicted from the multiplication of words. Others, however, understanding it better, affirm that this is commanded: that we should not, either in speaking or in thinking, imagine more of God than we are able; but that we should know our weakness, that as far as heaven is distant from earth, so far is our opinion separated from his nature; and that for this reason our words ought to be measured. For just as he who is occupied with many thoughts frequently dreams of the things he thinks about, so he who wants to discourse at greater length about the divinity will fall into folly. Or certainly thus: our words ought to be few for this reason, that even the things we think we know we see through a mirror and in a riddle, and what we think we hold we grasp as a dream. And when we have said a great deal, as it seemed good to us, the end of our disputation is folly. For by much speaking we do not escape sin.

5:3-4

When you have made a vow to God, do not delay to pay it, because there is no will in the foolish. Whatever you vow, pay. It is better not to vow than to vow and not pay. The simple understanding needs no interpretation. It is better not to promise than not to do what has been promised, because those who do not fulfil their vows displease God and are counted among the foolish. But as for what he says: There is no will in the foolish, the words 'of God' are understood, according to that saying of the Apostle: And indeed there was no will that I should come to you now. And if we wish to say something more searching as well, the Christian is commanded to complete his faith by deed and not to be like the Jews, who, pledging and saying: All things whatsoever the Lord commands we will do, worshipped idols. And after beating the servants and crushing them with stones, last of all they slaughtered the very son of the householder too. It is better, therefore, to weigh a doubtful decision for a long time than to be easy in words and hard in deed. For the servant who knows the will of his lord and will not do it shall be beaten with many stripes.

5:5

Do not give your mouth so that it causes your flesh to sin. And do not say in the sight of the angel that it is ignorance; lest God be angry at your voice and scatter the works of your hands. The Hebrew understood it thus: What you cannot do, do not promise. For words do not pass away into the wind, but are at once carried to the Lord by the angel present, who clings to each person as his companion. And you who think that God does not know what you have promised provoke him to anger, so that all your works are scattered. But in what he says: So that it causes your flesh to sin, not sifting it carefully, he understood it as if he had said: Do not give your mouth, so that you do not sin. To us, however, there seems to be another sense: that those are rebuked who complain of the force of the flesh and say that they are compelled by the necessity of the body to do things they do not want, according to that saying of the Apostle: For what I want, this I do not do, but what I do not want and so on. Do not, then, he says, look for empty excuses and give your flesh occasion to sin, and say: It is not I who sin, but the sin that dwells in my flesh. Finally, in that place where he says: And do not say before the angel that it is ignorance, Aquila, interpreting the Hebrew word segaga, rendered, for ignorance, ἀκούσιον, that is, not voluntary. For if, he says, you say this, you provoke God as though he were the author of evil and of sin, so that in his anger, if you seem to have anything good, he takes it out of your hands. Or certainly, since you think such things, he hands you over to a reprobate mind, so that you do things that are not fitting.

5:6

For in the multitude of dreams there are both vanities and very many words. But fear God. The Hebrews too explain this passage thus. Do not do the things above, of which we have already spoken, and do not readily believe dreams. For when you have seen various things, and in the rest of the night your soul has been stirred up by various terrors or excited by promises, despise those things that belong to a dream. And fear God alone. For he who believes dreams will give himself over to vanities and absurdities. Otherwise: Since I have said and commanded: Do not give your mouth so that you cause your flesh to sin, and seek various excuses, I now add this: since in the sleep of this life, and in the image, the shadow, the cloud in which we live, we can find many things that seem plausible to us and excuse our sins, I therefore warn you to beware of this alone, that you do not think God absent; but fear him, and know that he is present at all your works, and that you, created with free will, are not compelled, but will what you do.

5:7-8

If you see oppression of the poor and robbery of judgment and justice in a province, do not marvel at the matter; because one high above the high keeps watch, and one higher is over them, and moreover the king of the earth is in all things, in the tilled field. The tunic of Christ, woven from above, could not be torn even by those who crucified him; and the man from whom he had cast out demons the Savior commanded to go away clothed in the garments of the apostles. So let us too strive not to tear the garments of our Ecclesiastes, nor to stitch on, at our own pleasure, patches of opinions here and there, but to keep the one fabric of the discussion and follow the same sense and order. He had said above: Do not say in the sight of the angel that it is ignorance, lest God be angry at your voice, and the rest; and he had spoken against those who denied that human affairs are governed by providence. Since, then, a question arose against this precept, both why the just suffer oppression and why unjust judgments are given in the whole world, and God is not the avenger, he now brings in and resolves what could be objected, saying: If you see oppression of the poor man, who is proclaimed blessed in the Gospel, and things done by force, not by justice, do not marvel, and let it not seem to you something new. For God, high above the high, looks upon these things, he who has set his angels over the judges and kings of the earth, who are certainly able to prevent injustice and to have more power on earth than any powers of men whatever. But since he reserves judgment for the end, and at the consummation of the world, when the crop is ripe and the reapers have come, he is going to command that the wheat be separated and the darnel given to the fire, he therefore waits now and defers the sentence, as long as the field of this world is being more fully cultivated. That the field is interpreted as the world, the Lord explained in the parable of the tares and the wheat.

5:9-10

He who loves silver will not be filled with silver, and he who loves riches will not enjoy them. But this too is vanity. For in the multitude of goods there are many who eat them; and what strength is there for the one who has them, except to see them with his eyes? Wherever we put silver (argentum), it can also be translated money (pecunia), according to the ambiguity of the Greek language: for ἀργύριον signifies both. Moreover Tully relates that men of money (pecuniosi) were first so called because they had many herds (peculia), that is, cattle (pecora). For that is what they called them in ancient times. But little by little, through misuse, the name passed over to something else. The miser, then, is described, because he is never filled with wealth, and the more he has, the more he desires. The judgment of Flaccus also agrees on this, who says: "The miser is always in want", and that of the noble historian, that avarice is lessened neither by plenty nor by want. Riches, then, says Ecclesiastes, are of no profit to their possessor except only this, that he sees what he possesses. For the greater his substance, the more servants he will have to devour the wealth he has gathered. He, however, may only look at what he has, and cannot take in more than one man's food.

5:11

Sweet is sleep to the worker, whether he eats little or more. And the fullness of the rich man does not let him sleep. The discourse is still about the rich and avaricious man, and he is compared with the worker who sleeps without anxiety, whether he has eaten little or much, because the worker digests any food whatever by the toil and sweat of his work, and enjoys sweet sleep. But the rich man, stuffed with feasts and torn in different directions by his thoughts, cannot sleep, as the surfeit overflows and the undigested food seethes in the narrow confines of his stomach. Further, since the common departure from this life is also called sleep, better will be the rest of him who works in the present and according to his strength is engaged in good works than the riches of those of whom it is written: Woe to you rich, because you have received your consolation.

5:12-16

There is a most grievous sickness which I saw under the sun: riches kept by their owner to his hurt. And those riches perished in the worst distraction. And he begot a son, and there is nothing in his hands. As he came out of his mother's womb, naked he will return, as he came, and he will take nothing of his labor, that it may go in his hands. But this too is a most grievous sickness, that as he came, so also he goes. What more, then, will he have, because he labored for the wind? And all his days he will eat in darkness, and in very much indignation, and in infirmity, and in anger. Join what follows with what precedes: Ecclesiastes is describing the rich man who can neither enjoy his riches himself, and often comes into danger because of them, nor leave to an heir what he has gathered; but both he and his son, just as they came naked, so return naked to the earth, and nothing of their labors goes with them. For is it not a most grievous sickness to be tormented in thought over riches, and with empty labor to acquire, in sadness, in groaning, in indignation, in quarrels, wealth that will perish and that we cannot even take away with us when we die? And so much according to the simple sense. But that we may be raised higher, he seems to me to be speaking of the philosophers or the heretics, who gather riches of doctrines to their own hurt, and neither can the inventors gain any benefit, nor can they leave a lasting fruit to their followers. But both they themselves and their disciples return to the earth and lose their riches at the hands of him who said: I will destroy the wisdom of the wise, and the prudence of the prudent I will reject. For truly, just as they came out of their mother's womb, namely the perverse church, the opposite of that one of which it is written: But the Jerusalem that is above is free, which is the mother of us all, so they will go naked into the wind, laboring for nothing. They have failed, searching with searching, and are carried about by every wind of doctrine, and they have no light, but eat their mysteries in darkness; and they are always in infirmity, always in anger, treasuring up for themselves wrath in the day of wrath, and not having God favorable to them.

5:17-19

Behold what I have seen to be good, which is best: to eat, and to drink, and to see pleasantness in all one's labor with which he has labored under the sun, in the number of the days of his life, which God has given him; for this is his portion. And also every man to whom God has given riches and substance and has granted to feed on them, and to take his portion, and to rejoice in his labor: this is the gift of God. For he will not much remember the days of his life, because God occupies his heart in gladness. In comparison with the man who feeds on his wealth in the darkness of cares, and with great weariness of life heaps up what will perish, he says that the one who enjoys present things is better. For here there is at least a small pleasure in the enjoying, but there only the greatness of anxieties. And he gives the reasons why it is a gift of God to be able to enjoy riches: Because he will not much remember the days of his life. For God calls him away in the gladness of his heart: he will not be in sadness, he will not be vexed by thought, drawn away by present gladness and pleasure. But it is better, according to the Apostle, to understand the spiritual food and spiritual drink given by God, and to see goodness in all one's labor, because with immense labor and study we can contemplate true goods. And this is our portion, that we rejoice in our study and labor. Which, though it is good, yet until Christ our life is made manifest, is not yet fully good. And therefore God will not much remember the days of our life. It should be noted also that here περισπασμός is taken in the better sense, for the occupation of spiritual and true gladness.

6:1-6

There is an evil which I have seen under the sun, and it is frequent among men. A man to whom God has given riches and substance and glory, and nothing is lacking to his soul of all that he has desired, and God has not given him power to eat of it, but a stranger eats it. This is vanity and a most grievous sickness. If a man begets a hundred and lives many years, and the days of his years were many, and his soul is not filled with good things, and he has had no tomb, I said that the untimely birth is better than he. For it comes in vanity and goes in darkness, and in darkness its name will be hidden. And indeed it has not seen the sun nor known it; this one has rest rather than that one. And if he lives a thousand years twice over and has not seen good, do not all things hasten to one place? He describes the miserly rich man and asserts that this evil is frequent among men: that nothing of what in the world is thought good is lacking to him, and nonetheless he torments himself with the most foolish thrift, keeping it all to be devoured by others. And he also adds this hyperbolically: that even if he should beget a hundred children, and should live not, like Adam, nearly a thousand, but two thousand years, and his soul should waste away with greed and avarice, he would be of a far worse condition than the untimely birth, which perishes as soon as it is seen to be born. For that one has seen neither evils nor good things; but this one, though he possessed good things, was always tormented by sorrows and anxious thought, and the untimely birth would have rest rather than that long-lived miser; and yet both are carried off by an equal end, since this one and that one alike are taken away by a like death. This can also be understood of Israel: that God gave him the law and the prophets and the covenant and the promise, and, as the Savior says: The kingdom of God will be taken away from you and given to a nation producing its fruits, all these things have been transferred to a stranger and foreign people from the Gentiles, and the former see their good things and do not enjoy them. And that we are of a much better condition, who were reckoned like untimely births and newcomers by those who applauded themselves on their antiquity, glorying in the fathers and saying: Our father is Abraham; and yet both we and they hasten to one place, that is, to the judgment of God. But as to what he says in the middle: And indeed he had no tomb; either this signifies that that rich man gives no thought to his own death, and, though he possesses everything, is miserly even in the building of a tomb; or that often, slain by plots on account of those very riches, he is cast away unburied; or, which I think better, that he has done no good deed by which he could win himself a memory among posterity, and not pass through life in silence like cattle, when he had the means by which it could be made plain that he had lived.

6:7-8

All the labor of man is in his mouth, and yet his soul will not be filled. For what has the wise man more than the fool, what has the poor man, except to know how to go toward life? Everything that men labor at in this world is consumed by the mouth, and, ground by the teeth, is handed over to the belly to be digested. And when it has delighted the palate a little, it seems to give pleasure only so long as it is held in the throat. But when it has passed into the belly, the difference between foods ceases. And after all this, the soul of the one who eats is not filled: either because he desires again what he has eaten, and the wise man as much as the fool cannot live without food, and the poor man seeks nothing else but how he may sustain the instrument of his poor little body and not perish of hunger; or because the soul takes no benefit from the refreshment of the little body, and food is common to the wise man and the fool alike, and the poor man goes where he has perceived that there is wealth. But it is better to understand this of the man of the Church, who, learned in the heavenly Scriptures, has all his labor in his mouth, and his soul is not filled, since he always desires to learn. And in this the wise man has more than the foolish man: that, since he perceives himself to be poor, and poor, moreover, as that poor man is who in the Gospel is called blessed, he hastens to grasp the things that belong to life, and walks the narrow and strait way that leads to life, and is poor in evil works, and knows where Christ, who is life, abides.

6:9

Better is the sight of the eyes than one walking in the soul. But this too is vanity and presumption of spirit. Symmachus has interpreted this lucidly, saying: It is better to look ahead than to walk as one pleases. That is, it is better to do all things according to understanding, which is the eye of the soul, than to follow the will of the heart. For this is to walk in the soul, as Ezekiel says: Who walk in the will of their heart. Or certainly he rebukes the proud man who is pleased with himself, and says that he who looks ahead to all things is better than he whom nothing pleases except what he himself has done; than which nothing is worse, and which is emptier than any wind. Here again Aquila and Theodotion interpreted presumption of spirit as feeding on wind; Symmachus, however, as affliction of spirit. Moreover, it should be known that among the Hebrews spirit and wind are both called by the same name, that is: ruha.

6:10

What is it that is to be? Already its name has been called, and it is known that he is man, and he will not be able to be judged with one stronger than himself. The coming of the Savior is openly foretold: that he who was to come, before he was seen in the body, already had his name called in the Scriptures and known to the prophets and saints of God, namely that he is man; and that according to this, that he is man, he cannot compare himself with the Father. And in the Gospel he says: The Father who sent me is greater than I. Hence also in what follows we are commanded not to inquire beyond what has been written for us about him, so that man may not wish to know more than Scripture has attested. For since we are ignorant even of our own state, and our life passes away like a shadow, and things to come are uncertain, it is not expedient for us to inquire into greater things than we are able. Some think that this is signified in this place: that God already knows the name of all who are to be and are to be clothed with a human body; and that man cannot answer back against his maker, asking why he was made thus or thus. For the more we inquire, the more our vanity and our superfluous words are shown; and that free will is not taken away by the foreknowledge of God, but that causes go before, why each thing was made as it was.

6:11

For there are many words multiplying vanity.

7:1

What more is there for man? For who has known what is good for man in life, the number of the days of the life of his vanity? and he will make them like a shadow, for who will declare to man what will be after him under the sun? Since, he says, man is ignorant of his own state, and whatever he seems to know and discern he sees not as the truth of the matter is, but through a mirror, a shadow and an image, and does not know the things to come, and in much speaking does not escape sin, let him set silence upon his mouth, and let him believe that he has come of whom it is written, not inquiring how, or how great, or of what kind he came.

7:2

A good name is above good oil, and the day of death above the day of his birth. Consider, he says, O man, how short your days are, and that soon, when the flesh is dissolved, you will cease to be; make for yourself a longer-lasting fame, so that, as ointment delights the nostrils with its odor, so all posterity may delight in your name. Symmachus rendered this plainly, saying: A good name is better than sweet-smelling ointment. For it is the custom of the Hebrews to call good ointment oil. But what he says: And the day of death above the day of his birth, either shows that it is better to go out of the world and be free of tribulations and of the uncertain state of life than, entering the world, to endure all these things; or surely that in death it is known what kind of men we are, whereas at the first beginning of birth it is unknown what kind we shall be; or that birth binds the freedom of the soul with the body, and death dissolves it.

7:3

It is better to go to the house of mourning than to the house of feasting, in which is the end of every man; and he who lives will lay it to his heart. It is more profitable to go to the rites of a funeral than to the house of a feast, because there we are reminded, by the corpse lying before us, of our condition and of human frailty; but in the gladness of a feast, even if we seemed to have some fear, we lose it. The last verse Symmachus rendered more plainly, saying: And he who lives will look to the mind. From this it is proved that, in having seemed above to approve food and drink, he does not prefer pleasure to all things, as many wrongly suppose, but means that, in comparison with avarice and excessive stinginess, it is something more, though it be brief, if a man enjoys his wealth even for a moment. For he would never have preferred the sadness of mourning to the festivity of a feast if he had thought eating and drinking to be of any moment.

7:4

Anger is better than laughter, for in the sorrow of the countenance the heart will be amended. Laughter loosens the mind; anger rebukes and amends. Let us be angry with ourselves too, whenever we sin; let us be angry with others too. For through sadness of countenance the mind will become better, as Symmachus rendered it. And therefore: Woe now to those who laugh, for they shall mourn.

7:5

The heart of the wise is in the house of mourning, and the heart of the foolish in the house of gladness. Blessed, says the Savior, are those who mourn, for they shall be comforted. Samuel too mourned King Saul all the days of his life. And Paul says that he mourns over those who, after various sins, had refused to do repentance. Let the heart of the wise man, then, go to the house of such a man as will rebuke him when he does wrong, so as to bring him to tears, as will move him to weep for his own sins; and let it not go to the house of gladness, where the teacher flatters and deceives, where he seeks not the conversion of his hearers but applause and praise. Such a preceptor is to be lamented, rich in speech, rich in words, who, because he is sated, therefore receives his consolation. Finally, the following little verses also agree with this exposition. For he says:

7:6-7

It is better to hear the rebuke of a wise man than a man hearing the song of fools. For as the sound of thorns under a pot, so is the laughter of the fool. But this also is vanity. For it is better to be rebuked by a wise man than to be deceived by coaxing flattery. Like this is that saying: Better are the wounds of a friend than the willing kisses of an enemy. For as the sound of thorns burning under a pot gives off an unpleasant crackling, so too the words of a fawning master will be of no profit, whether he exhorts his hearers to the cares of the world, which the thorns are taken to mean, or prepares them for the burning to come. Symmachus, for what we have put: For as the sound of thorns under a pot, so is the laughter of the fool, interpreting the sense which we have set out above, says: For through the voice of the unskilled a man is bound with chains. That is, at the voice of such preceptors the hearer is entangled all the more, while each one is bound fast by the chains of his own sins.

7:8

Oppression troubles the wise man and will destroy the heart of his strength. Here take the wise man as one still set on the way of progress, according to that saying: Reprove a wise man and he will love you. For the perfect wise man needs no reproof and is troubled by no oppression. Let us use this little verse whenever we see a just and wise man suffer oppression and be troubled by the iniquity of a judgment, and God not at once present to come to his aid. But for what the Seventy, and Aquila, and Theodotion rendered, destroys the heart εὐτονίας αὐτοῦ, that is, of his strength or vigor, Symmachus says: and destroys the heart matthana (a gift); joining both the Hebrew word and its interpretation, and producing that sense which is written elsewhere: Gifts blind even the eyes of the wise.

7:9

Better is the last of a discourse than its beginning. In speaking, the epilogues are better than the opening; for in them the speaker's anxiety ends, in the other it begins. Or surely thus: he who begins to hear a discourse and goes to a preceptor is at the beginning, but he who hears the final parts is consummated and perfect. But it can also be understood in this way: while we are in this world, what we know is all beginning; but when that which is perfect has come, we shall be among the last and consummated things. The Hebrew explained this passage together with the following little verse thus: It is better for you to consider the end of an undertaking than its beginning, and to be patient than to be swept away by the fury of impatience. We also learn from this little sentence that there is no wisdom in men, since it is better to be silent than to speak out; and that when a speech has been finished, the hearer turns over within himself what has been said, whereas when we have only begun to speak, he has not yet received anything of profit.

Better is the patient man than one lofty in spirit. Because above he had granted anger, saying: Anger is better than laughter, lest we should think that anger which consists in passion is praised, he now commands that anger be removed entirely. For there he set down anger for the rebuke of those who sin and for the instruction of those under us, but here he has bridled impatience. And patience is necessary not only in straits but also in more joyful times, lest we be lifted up more than it will allow. It seems to me that the poor in spirit, who is also set among the beatitudes in the Gospel, is the opposite of him who is here called lofty in spirit.

7:10

Do not hasten in your spirit, so as to be angry, for anger rests in the bosom of fools. It is not because he grants that one should be angry more slowly that he now says: Do not hasten in your spirit to be angry; but because, when anger is raging and fresh, if it is put off it is more easily calmed and can be taken away. And because anger is always joined to pride, longing for vengeance, he said above too that the patient man is better than him who is lofty in spirit; and now he has given a sign of foolishness: that however powerful and wise someone is thought to be, if he is irascible he is proved foolish: For anger rests in the bosom of fools.

7:11

Do not say: What has come about? that the former days were better than these; for you have not asked wisely about this. Do not prefer the old age to the present, for God is the one maker of both. Virtues make the days good for the one who lives, vices make them evil. Do not say, then, that the days were better under Moses and under Christ than they are now. For at that time too many were unbelievers, and their days became evil; and now many believers are found, of whom the Savior says: More blessed are those who have not seen and have believed. Otherwise: You ought so to live that the present days are always better for you than the past, lest, when you have begun little by little to decline, it be said to you: You were running well; who hindered you from obeying the truth? And again: Having begun in the spirit, you are now being finished in the flesh. Otherwise: Do not say that the times of old were better than now, those of Moses than those of Christ, those of the law than those of grace. For if you choose to ask this, you act unwisely, not seeing how far the Gospel stands apart from the old testament.

7:12

Wisdom is good with an inheritance, and more so to those who see the sun, for as the shadow of wisdom, so is the shadow of silver; and what is more, the knowledge of wisdom will give life to him who has it. A wise man with riches is of greater glory than one who is only wise. For some need wisdom, others wealth; and he who is wise and not rich can indeed teach what is good, but sometimes cannot supply what is asked. Therefore he says: For as the shadow of wisdom, so the shadow of money; that is, as wisdom protects, so sometimes money protects too. And lest he should seem to have detracted from wisdom by placing it beneath a good of chance (for it is not in our power to have riches, which the unjust often possess in greater measure), he therefore shows that wisdom is greater, saying: And more, the knowledge of wisdom will give life to him who has it. In this, he says, wisdom is greater than riches, that without any wealth it gives life to him who has it.

Some interpret this passage thus: By inheritance, they say, he meant good conduct, through which we are heirs of God and joint heirs of Christ. Ecclesiastes therefore wishes to teach how far those who merit to see the sun of justice, and have wisdom together with good conduct, differ from those who, without wisdom, have devoted their zeal only to their life and conduct. This Daniel also shows, saying: Those who understand my words will shine like the lights of heaven; or as Theodotion rendered it: Like the brightness of the firmament. And those who have done my words, like the stars of heaven. But the shadow of silver, or of money, we ought to take according to ἀναγωγὴν (the higher, anagogical sense), as that of which the talents and minas are gathered in the parables of the Gospel, so that, when we are under the shadow of wisdom and under the shadow of such silver: The sun will not burn us by day, nor the moon by night. But this too can be said, that our life upon the earth is a shadow: And the spirit of our face, Christ the Lord, to whom we said: In his shadow we shall live among the nations. All our protection in this life is like a shadow, whether of wisdom or of the silver mentioned above, until the day breathes and the shadows are taken away. Symmachus, after his manner, rendered more clearly in this place too, saying: As wisdom protects, so likewise money also protects. And the following little verse plainly exhorts to the pursuit of knowledge.

7:14

See the works of God, for who will be able to adorn him whom God has perverted? In this place too Symmachus translated thus: Learn the works of God, for no one will be able to correct what he has diminished; that is, it is enough for you, from the holy Scriptures or from the very contemplation of the elements, to know and understand the things that have been made, but not to search out the causes and reasons why each thing was made so, or ought to have been made otherwise than it was made. For example, if someone wishes to ask why God speaks thus to Moses: Who made the dumb and the deaf, the seeing and the blind, was it not I, the Lord God? and says: Why were the blind and the deaf and the dumb created so? and other things like these; in this place we must take up the testimony of the seventeenth (18th) psalm, in which it is said to the Lord: With the holy you will be holy, and with the perverse you will be perverted. And it must be said that the Lord is holy with him who is holy, and is perverted toward him who has first become perverse by his own will. According also to that which is written in Leviticus: If they walk perverse toward me, I too will walk perverse toward them in my fury. This indeed will also be able to explain why God hardened the heart of Pharaoh. For as one and the same working of the sun melts wax and dries mud, and, according to its own substance, the wax melts and the mud is dried, so the one working of God's signs in Egypt softened the heart of the believers and hardened the unbelievers, who, according to their hardness and impenitent heart, were treasuring up for themselves wrath on the day of wrath from these wonders, which they saw being done and did not believe.

7:15

In the day of goodness be in good, and in the evil day see. And indeed God has made this one congruous with that one for the purpose of speaking, so that man may not find anything after him. I know that I heard these little verses expounded thus in church by one who was thought to have knowledge of the Scriptures: While you are in the present world and can do some good work, labor, so that afterwards, secure yourself, in the evil day, that is, in the day of judgment, you may see others tormented. For as God made the present world, in which we can prepare for ourselves the fruit of good works, so also he made the world to come, in which no opportunity of good work is given. He did indeed seem to persuade his hearers as he spoke, but to me there seems to be another sense, which Symmachus also translated, saying: In the good day be in good; but look upon the evil day. For God has made this like that, so that man might not find anything to complain of against him. Bear, he says, both good things and evil, as they befall you. And do not think that the nature of things in the world is either of good alone or of evil alone, since the world itself subsists from contraries: hot and cold, dry and moist, hard and soft, dark and bright, evil and good. And God has done this so that wisdom may have a place, and so that, in choosing good and avoiding evil, free will may be left to man, lest he say that he was begotten by God senseless and dull; but that God made things diverse for this reason, so that man could not complain of his own condition. And at the same time this testimony will be joined in due sequence to the words above, in which he says: Who will be able to adorn what God has perverted?

7:16

All things I have seen in the days of my vanity. There is a just man perishing in his justice, and there is an impious man long-lived in his malice. Something like this the Savior says in the Gospel: He who finds his soul will lose it; and he who has lost his soul for my sake will find it. The Maccabees, for the law of God and for justice, seemed to perish in their justice, and so did the martyrs who shed their blood for Christ. On the contrary, those who at that time ate swine's flesh, and those who after the coming of the Lord sacrificed to idols, seemed to live on in this world and, because of their malice, to continue long-lived. But it is the hidden patience of God both to afflict the saints now, so that they may receive evil things in their life, and not to visit sinners for their crime but to reserve them as if for the slaughter, so that he may restore eternal good things to the former and inflict everlasting evils on the latter. The Hebrews suppose the just perishing in their justice to be the sons of Aaron, because, while they thought they were acting justly, they offered strange fire. And the impious man long-lived in his malice they say is Manasseh, who, restored to his kingdom after the captivity, lived on for a long time thereafter.

7:17

Do not be very just, and do not seek more, lest you be stupefied. If you see someone rigid and harsh toward all the sins of the brethren, so that he grants no pardon either to one who sins in speech or to one who lags behind through a sluggishness that is sometimes natural, know that this man is more just than is just. For since the Savior commands, saying: Do not judge, that you may not be judged, and no one is without sin, not even if his life were of a single day, a justice that does not pardon the frailty of the human condition is inhuman. Do not, then, be very just, for a great weight and a small weight are an abomination before the Lord. Hence among the philosophers too the virtues are placed in the middle, and everything that is too much, whether upward or downward, is reckoned as a vice. But as for what he says: And do not seek more, lest you be troubled; or lest you be stupefied, he knows that our mind cannot comprehend perfect wisdom, and he bids us know the measure of our frailty. Finally, Paul too, to the one who was inquiring into more than a man could know, saying: Why does he still complain? For who resists his will? answered: O man, who are you, to answer back to God? and the rest. For if the one who is brought in as questioner had heard the causes of the question from the Apostle, he would perhaps have grown numb with stupor and found the grace useless. For there is also a gift, according to the same Apostle, which does not profit him to whom it is given. The Hebrews interpret this commandment, that is: Do not be very just, of Saul, who had mercy on Agag, whom the Lord had commanded to be killed. But that servant from the Gospel too, whom his lord had forgiven and who himself would not forgive his fellow servant, can be fitted to this little verse, as one who was very just.

7:18

Do not act very impiously, and do not be a fool; why will you die in a time not your own. Since God says: I do not will the death of the one who dies, only let him turn back and live, let it be enough to have sinned once; we ought to raise ourselves up after a fall. For if, according to those who discourse on physics, the swallow knows how to give sight to its young with its celandine, and wounded wild goats seek out dittany, why should we not know that the medicine of repentance has been set before sinners? But as for what he says: Do not die in a time not your own, we know that Korah, and Dathan, and Abiram, for their sedition against Moses and Aaron, were swallowed up by a sudden gaping of the earth, and that, for the amendment of others, very many have been judged even in this life, before the day of judgment. What he says, then, is this: Do not add sins to sins, lest you provoke God to inflict punishment on you even here.

7:19

It is good for you to hold on to this. And indeed do not withdraw your hand from this, for he who fears God will come forth from all things. It is good to do good to the just, but to do good to sinners too is not unjust. It is good to support those of the household of faith, but to give to everyone who asks is also a commandment. For he who fears God, and imitates his Maker, who rains on the just and the unjust, hastens to do good to all without respect of persons. Otherwise: Because this miserable life is changed daily by diverse events, toward adversity as much as toward prosperity, let the mind of the just man be prepared, and let him beg the mercy of God, so that whatever befalls he may bear it with a balanced mind. For he who fears God is neither lifted up by prosperity nor crushed by adversity.

7:20-21

Wisdom will strengthen the wise man above ten who have power, who are in the city; for there is not a just man on earth who does good and does not sin. Therefore wisdom strengthens the just man, and its help to him surpasses that of all the princes of the city, because, although someone be just, yet while he is set in this flesh he is subject to vices and sins and needs greater protection. Otherwise: The ten who have power and dwell in the city are the angels, who have attained the perfect number ten and give help to the human race. But if one considers all helps, greater is the help of wisdom, that is, of our Lord Jesus Christ. For after the angels had said: We have treated Babylon and she is not cured; let us leave her, and let each one go away to his own land, then the master of physicians himself came down, and us, spattered with blood and dripping with the gore of sins, who had spent all our substance on physicians, he healed by the touch of his fringe. And he healed in the city, that is, in this world, and strengthened the wise man, or, as the Seventy translated, helped him. For to everyone who has, it will be given and added to him. And because man, set in sins and fixed in the mire of the deep, needed greater help, therefore Wisdom herself came. Otherwise: Above he had said that good must be done both to those of the household and to strangers. Someone could then reply: If I wish to do good to all, I have not the means to do it. Nor can a just man have such great riches, which commonly abound rather to sinners. Therefore he now says: Those whom you cannot help with goods, help with counsel, cherish with consolation. For wisdom can do more for one set in distress than any of the greatest powers. And do this very thing with prudence. For great is the balance of justice: to whom, and how much, and how long, and what to give, whether in goods or in counsel.

7:22-23

And indeed do not give your heart to all the words that they will speak, for you will not hear your servant cursing you. For often your heart knows that you too have cursed others. Do what has been commanded, and, strengthened by the help of wisdom, prepare your heart either for evil things or for good, and do not care what your enemies say of you, or what opinion of you there is abroad. For as it is the part of a prudent man not to listen to a murmuring servant, nor to lend a curious ear to what he says of him in his absence, since if he does this he will always be in trouble, and at the servant's muttering he will be moved to anger; so too it is the part of a wise man to follow wisdom as she goes before him and to pay no heed to vain rumors. And by another example also he teaches that a just man must not care at all what men say, saying: As your conscience knows that you have spoken about many and have often disparaged others, so you too ought to pardon others when they disparage you. And at the same time he teaches that one must not judge easily, and that he who has a beam in his eye must not speak of another's mote.

7:24-25

All these things I have tried in wisdom, and I said: I will become wise; and she became farther from me than she was; and a deep depth, who will find it? He says that, as the books of Kingdoms also testify, he sought wisdom beyond all men and tried to reach her end, but that the more he sought, the less he found, and was sunk in the midst of gloom, surrounded by the darkness of ignorance. Besides, one who has been learned in the Scriptures, the more he begins to know, the greater the obscurity that daily arises for him in them. Otherwise: The contemplation of wisdom in this life is seen in a mirror and in an image; when, then, I reflect that in the future her knowledge is to be revealed face to face, then I shall clearly recognize that I am now far removed from her knowledge.

7:26-27

I went around, I and my heart, to know, and to consider, and to seek wisdom and reckoning, and to come to know the impiety of the fool and the error of the imprudent. And I find more bitter than death the woman who is a snare, and her heart nets, her hands bonds. He who is good before God will be rescued from her, and the sinner will be caught in her. For what the Seventy put: I went around, I and my heart, to come to know, Symmachus rendered, saying: I passed through all things with my mind, to know, and to discuss, and to investigate. Since, then, Ecclesiastes had said above that he had tried all things in wisdom, and that the more he sought her, the farther she had fled from him, he now says that he also sought something else in his wisdom: what evil in human affairs surpasses all evils, and what thing holds the first place in impiety, folly, error and madness. And he says that he found woman to be the head of all evils, because through her too death entered into the world, and she captures the precious souls of men. For all who commit adultery have hearts like an oven; it is she who makes the hearts of young men fly away. And when she has fallen upon the mind of a wretched lover, she drags him headlong and does not let him look before his feet, but like a snare and a net she entangles the young man's heart. For her hands are bonds. For this Aquila rendered: Her hands are bound, which in the Hebrew language is called assurim. For she can persuade, she cannot use force, nor drag the unwilling to herself. He who is just and good before God will be rescued from her; but the sinner, caught, will be led away to death. Let us not think that Solomon pronounced this sentence on the female sex rashly; he speaks what he has experienced. For he offended God precisely because he was captured by women. So much according to the letter. But according to the spiritual understanding, either we name every sin in general, and iniquity, woman, the iniquity which in Zechariah sits in the form of a woman upon the talent of lead; or we take the devil τροπικῶς (figuratively) as woman, because of his effeminate powers; or surely idolatry; and, to come nearer, the Church of the heretics, which calls to herself the man lacking sense, so that, enticed, he may receive stolen bread and stolen water, that is, a false mystery and a polluted baptism.

7:28-30

Behold, this I have found, says Ecclesiastes, one (feminine) to one, that I might find the number, which my soul has still sought, and I have not found. One man out of a thousand I have found, and a woman among all these I have not found. Only this have I found, that God made man upright; and they themselves have sought many thoughts. This, he says, I discovered, sifting all things diligently: that by sinning little by little, and adding one offense to another, we make for ourselves a great sum of sins. For Esebon, which all with one voice translated λογισμὸν, we can, by the ambiguity of the Hebrew speech, call number, and sum, and reckoning, and thought. But this too, he says, my soul sought: whether an upright woman is to be found. And while I found scarcely a few good among men, so that one out of a thousand could be found, a good woman I could not find at all. For they all led me not to virtue but to luxury. And because the heart of man is diligently set on malice from youth, and almost all have offended God, in this ruin of the human race woman is readier for the fall. Of her the pagan poet too says: "... a fickle and changeable thing is woman, always". And the Apostle: Always, he says, learning and never coming to the knowledge of the truth. And lest he should seem to condemn the common nature of men, and to make God the author of evil, as the maker of such as cannot avoid evil, he shrewdly took precaution and said that we were created good by God; but that because we have been left to our free will, by our own fault we slip toward worse things, while we seek greater things and think various thoughts beyond our strength. Otherwise: Daily setting before myself the reckoning of each thing, I could find no thought that was not disturbed from without by some perverse thinking. But among a thousand men I found a true human being, one made according to the image of his Maker; and among a thousand not of any kind whatever, but among a thousand men, whose number a woman could not fill: among a thousand who did not approach a woman and therefore remained most pure. But all these things are to be taken figuratively. For among many studious men, sweating in daily meditation, scarcely is a thought found that is pure and worthy of the name of man. We can also take thoughts for men and women for works, and say that hardly can anyone's thinking be found pure; but works, because they are carried out through the body, are always mixed with some error. But for what we said above, translating the Hebrew speech: One (feminine) to one, that the number may be found, or reckoning, or thought, Symmachus rendered more plainly: To find the reckoning one to one. For what we are used to saying absolutely and in the neuter, that is, this I have sought, this I wished to find, the Hebrews pronounce in the feminine, as also in the psalm: One thing (unam) I have asked of the Lord, this I will seek after, for unum.

7:30

Who is like the wise man; and who knows the solution of the word?

8:1

The wisdom of a man will light up his face, and the strong man will change his face. Above he had taught that a good man is hard to find, and had struck down the question coming against him: that men were made good by God, but slipped into sins of their own accord. Now, as if glorying, he lists what good God has given to man: namely wisdom, and reason and foresight, to know the hidden mysteries of God, to enter into his secrets with the understanding of the heart. But he speaks obliquely of himself: that no one was as wise as he, and no one knew so well the solutions of problems, and that his wisdom was praised by the whole people, a wisdom that did not only lie hidden within, but also shone out on the surface of the body and in the mirror of the face, and, beyond all men, painted the prudence of his mind upon his face. In place of what we have put, Who is thus as the wise man, the Seventy translated: Who knows the wise. And in place of what we have said, And the strong man will change his face, they put: And the impudent man will be hated for his face. And indeed, though there are many who promise wisdom, it is hard to find one who can tell the true wise man from those who seem to be wise. And though there are very many who say they can solve the hidden things of the Scriptures, rare is he who finds the true solution. What follows, however, The wisdom of a man will light up his face, and the shameless man will be hated for his face, we can explain by setting down the words of Paul: But we all, beholding the glory of the Lord with unveiled face; and of the psalmist who sings: The light of your face, Lord, has been sealed upon us. Here, moreover, by the wisdom of a man he means none other than the wisdom of God. Though it is the wisdom of God, it begins, according to the measure of a man's capacity, to be the wisdom of him who has deserved to have it. Every heretic, and everyone who defends a false doctrine, is of impudent face. Indeed Marcion and Valentinus say that they are of a better nature than the Creator is. And this could be borne in some measure if they maintained that they had the hope of this thing, and not that they already possess the nature.

8:2-4

I keep the mouth of the king and the speech of the oath of God. Do not hasten to go away from his face, and do not stand in an evil word, since he will do all that he wishes. As the king has said, having power: and who says to him, what are you doing? He seems indeed to teach, in accord with the Apostle, that kings and powers must be obeyed, especially since the Seventy translators speak in the imperative mood: Keep the mouth of the king; but I think that it is now said of that king of whom David says: Lord, in your strength the king will rejoice. And in another place, so that the one kingdom of the Father and of the Son may be signified, Scripture records: God, give your judgment to the king, and your justice to the king's son. For the Father judges no one, but has given all judgment to the Son. This king, the Son of God, is the Son of the king, the Father. His precepts, therefore, must be kept, his will carried out. And this is what is written in the book of Tobit: It is good to hide the mystery of the king. And he warns above all that we should not discuss why God has commanded each thing, but that whatever the pious mind of man sees to be commanded, it should hasten to fulfill. And let his will be in the law of the Lord. But since the Seventy translated otherwise, saying: And concerning the oath and the word of God do not hasten to walk from the face of God; we must know that the oath of God is written down in the divine volumes. This oath, then, sacred and secret, concerning the word of God, we ought not to tell to just anyone, nor bring it out into the open and pass hasty sentence on it. Nor, like Moses, should you hasten to see the face of God; but wait until he himself passes by, and you see only his back parts. And also what follows, Do not stand in an evil word, and the rest, let us understand of one who has been caught beforehand in the error of heresy; or of one who, though he holds the faith of the Church, is yet so overcome by sins that he is unfaithful. Do not persist in detraction, nor in foul talk, luxury, avarice, lust. But if you persist, the devil, the king of vices and of sin, will work perdition in you and will do whatever he wishes.

8:5

He who keeps the commandment does not know an evil word; and the heart of the wise man knows time and judgment. It is to be noted that does not know an evil word is put in place of: will not suffer it, or: it will not be in him. For so it is written of the Savior too: Who, though he had not known sin, God made sin for us. In place of word, Symmachus also rendered thing, saying: He who keeps the commandment will not experience an evil thing. He has taught, moreover, that the command of the king must be kept, and that we must know what, and why, and at what time he commands.

8:6-7

For to every matter there is a time and judgment. For the affliction of man is great upon him, because he does not know what is to come; for as it will be, who will announce it to him? Although things turn out in different ways, and the just man cannot know what is going to happen to him, nor understand the causes and reasons of each thing (for no one is aware of the things to come), still he knows that all things are done by God for the benefit of men, and are not ordered without his will. For the affliction of the human race is great, because, as the poet says: "The mind of man, ignorant of fate and of future lot". It hopes for one thing, another happens; it expects the enemy from one quarter, and is wounded by the javelin of another. But in place of what the Seventy translators and Theodotion said: For the knowledge of man is great upon him; the Hebrew has malice (malitia), not knowledge (scientia); but because the Hebrew letters res and daleth are alike, except for a small stroke, they read dath for raath, that is, knowledge for malice. He who has a knowledge of that language will know this better. That too which is written at the end of these verses: For he does not know what has been done; and what will be after him, who will announce it to him? we have now translated word for word from the Hebrew speech, so that we may know that there is another sense: namely, that we can neither know the things that have passed, nor understand the things that are to come as they will be.

8:8

There is no man having power over the spirit, to restrain the spirit, and there is none powerful in the day of death, and there is no sending forth in war, and impiety will not save him who has it. Our soul is not in our power, so that it should not be taken from us; and when the spirit goes forth at the command of God, it is of no use to close the mouth and hold back the fleeing life. And when destruction, the enemy and foe of our life, has come, we cannot obtain a truce. Nor can we, though once kings in the world and laying waste all things by our impiety, raise our hands against death, but we shall be dissolved into ash and earth. We must not mourn, then, if we cannot know the things to come, and are often oppressed by unjust men more powerful than we, since all things are ended by death, and the proud and powerful man who has plundered everything cannot keep back his soul when it is snatched away. Otherwise: The spirit, which disposes all things, cannot be restrained by any man, nor receive laws for its breathing. Of it, too, it was said above: Circling in its circling the spirit goes. In the day of death we are not powerful; for in the day of life the enemy is easily avoided. Likewise he too who is in war, and does not have the peace of God which surpasses all understanding, will not have the sending forth of which it is said to the bride: Your sendings forth are a paradise with the fruit of apples. And since impiety will not save him who has it, piety, on the contrary, will save. The devil can be called impiety, and our Lord Jesus Christ piety.

8:9-11

All these things I saw, and I gave my heart to every work that has been done under the sun, and man has lorded it over man to afflict him. And then I saw the impious buried, and they came, and they went forth from the holy place, and they were praised in the city, because they had done so; but this too is vanity. For because there is no contradiction quickly for those who do evil, therefore the heart of the sons of man is filled in them, to do evil. I gave my heart, he says, to look upon all that is done under the sun, and this above all: that man has received power over man, to afflict and condemn whomever he wishes. So while I was directing my mind to look upon these things, I saw the impious dead with such a reputation, and so buried, that they were reckoned holy on earth; men who even while they lived were thought worthy of the Church and of the temple of God, and who moreover, walking puffed up, were praised in their evils. For the sinner is praised in the desires of his soul, and he who does iniquity is blessed. This happens, however, because no one dares to contradict sinners, nor does God at once avenge the crime, but he puts off the punishment while he waits for repentance. But sinners, because they have not at once been rebuked and corrected, think that there will be no judgment at all, and persist in crime. We can use this testimony against bishops who have received power in the Church and rather give scandal to those whom they ought to have taught and urged on to better things. These are often praised in the Church after death, and are publicly proclaimed blessed, whether by their successors or by the peoples, for things they had done not commendably. And so this too is vain: for because they are not spoken of as they acted, and are not at once rebuked in their sin (for no one dares to accuse a superior), therefore, as if holy and blessed and walking in the commandments of the Lord, they add sins to sins. An accusation against a bishop is difficult. For even if he has sinned, it is not believed; and if he has been convicted, he is not punished.

8:12

For the sinner does evil a hundred times and it is prolonged for him. From this I know that it will be good for those who fear God, who will fear before his face. From the fact that to one who sins very much (for this is what a hundred times signifies) God gives room for repentance, and does not at once punish him in his crime, but waits for him to turn from his iniquity, I understand how kind and merciful God will be toward those who have fear of him and tremble at his word. Symmachus translated this place thus: For the wicked man who sins has died, long-suffering having been granted to him. But I know that it will be well with those who fear God, who have feared before his face. But there will be no good for the unjust man, nor will he survive for a long time, because he did not fear the face of God. And since it is clear what he has translated, this must be said: that the Hebrew word Maath, which the Seventy translated from then, and we have put a hundred times (centies), Aquila and Symmachus and Theodotion have interpreted has died, so that it is: He who sinned and did evil has died; for in that he sinned, he died at once. But if, following the Seventy translators, we read from then in place of has died, according to some this will be the sense: The sinner does not sin for the first time when he is seen to commit sin, but has already sinned before: For sinners are estranged from the womb, they have gone astray from the belly; and they ask how what follows, they have spoken falsehoods, can be explained; for the simple understanding does not seem to hold together, that little children, sinners, speak a lie as soon as they have been cast out of the womb.

8:13

And let it not be good for the impious man, and let him not prolong his days like a shadow, who does not fear before the face of God. He calls down evil on those who do not have the fear of God, and wishes that they not be put off long from punishment, but, taken away at once by death, receive the torments they deserve. The Apostle too says something similar: Would that those who trouble you were cut off. And elsewhere: Alexander the coppersmith showed me many evils: the Lord will repay him according to his works. How these things are said mercifully must be inquired into. So much according to the truth of the Hebrew sense. But if someone follows the Seventy translators, who, as if beginning from another sense, said: And I recognize that it will be good for those who fear God, that they may fear before his face, and it will not be good for the impious man, and he will not prolong his days in a shadow, who is not fearing before the face of God. He will be able to say this: Those things too will happen of which I treated a little before; yet I recognize most clearly that it will be well with those who fear before the face of God: For the face of God is upon those who do evil. And it will not be well with the impious man; for he does not fear before the face of God, and he will not prolong his days in a shadow, that is, the days of his life, which are like a shadow to the living. Not that those who live a long time prolong their days, but those who make them great by the greatness of good works. Hence Jacob too, confessing himself a sinner, as it were, says: Few and evil are my days. And confessing in the psalm: My days, he says, have declined like a shadow, and I have withered like grass. Not that he sought a long life in the present, in which all that we live is brief, a shadow and an image: For man walks about in an image; but that he fears for the future, lest the length of his life, where true life is, be shortened.

8:14

There is a vanity that is done upon the earth: that there are just men to whom come, as it were, the deeds of the impious, and there are impious men to whom come, as it were, the deeds of the just. I said that this too is vanity. Among the other vanities which are carried along in the world with varied event, I have detected this too: that to the just there often happen the things that should have happened to the impious, and the impious pass their life so happily in this world that you would think them most just. The Gospel will give the example of the rich man clothed in purple and of the poor man Lazarus. The seventy-second (73rd) psalm also discusses this matter: why evils sometimes befall the just, and good things the impious. But in place of what we have put, There is a vanity that is done upon the earth, Symmachus translated absolutely, saying: It is hard to know what is done upon the earth; the Hebrews interpret the just to whom evils happen, and the impious to whom the works of the just come, as the sons of Aaron and Manasseh: because those perished while sacrificing, and this one, after such great evils and captivity, was restored to his rule.

8:15

And I praised gladness, because there is no good for man under the sun but to eat, and to drink, and to be glad. And this will go out with him from his labor in the days of his life, which God has given him under the sun. We have interpreted this more fully above, and now we say briefly that he prefers that pleasure of eating and drinking, though brief and soon to end, to the straits of the world and to the things that are seen to be done unjustly in the world, because this alone man seems to have from his labor, if he enjoys even a little refreshment. But this interpretation, if it is taken as it is written, will prove wretched those who fast, hunger, thirst and mourn, whom the Lord calls blessed in the Gospel. And so let us take both the food and the drink spiritually, and the gladness over them, which in the labor of our life we can scarcely find. That these things are to be understood as we have said is shown by the following little verse, in which he says: I gave my heart to see wisdom and occupation: namely, that men are occupied upon the earth and spend days and nights in the meditation of the Scriptures, so that very often, for the investigation of the truth, sleep flees from their eyes.

8:16-17

Wherefore I gave my heart to know wisdom and to see the occupation that has been done upon the earth. For both by day and by night he is not seeing sleep in his eyes. And I saw all the works of God, that man will not be able to find the work that has been done under the sun, in which man has labored to seek, and he will not find. Indeed, even if the wise man says that he knows, he will not be able to find. He who seeks the causes and reasons of things, why this or that has been done, and why the world is governed by varied events; why one man is born blind and feeble, another seeing and sound; this one has poverty, that one riches; this one is noble, that one without glory: gains nothing else but to be tormented in his own question, and to have the dispute for a torment, and yet not to find what he seeks. And when he says that he has come to know, then he has the beginning of ignorance and is turning about in deep error. Yet he hints that there are causes of all things, and a justice why each thing happens so, but that they lie hidden in secret and cannot be grasped by men.

9:1

All this I gave in my heart, that I might consider all things: that the just and the wise, and their works, are in the hand of the Lord. And indeed love, and indeed hatred; there is no man who knows all things in their face. This passage too Symmachus translated more plainly, saying: All these things I set in my heart, that I might winnow all things: that both the just and the wise and their works are in the hand of God. And moreover man knows neither friendships nor enmities; but all things before them are uncertain, because like things befall all, the just and the unjust. Now this is the sense: In this too I gave my heart, and I wished to know whom God loved and whom he hated. And I found that the works of the just are indeed in the hand of God, and yet that they cannot know now whether they are loved by God or not, and that they waver in doubt whether they endure what they endure for their testing or for their punishment. In the future, therefore, they will know, and all things are in their countenance, that is, the knowledge of this matter goes before them when they have departed from this life, because then is the judgment, now the contest. And as for all who endure adversities, whether they endure them through the love of God, like Job, or through his hatred, like very many sinners, that is held uncertain for now.

9:2

In all things there is one event, to the just and to the impious, to the good and to the evil, and to the clean and to the polluted, and to him who sacrifices and to him who does not sacrifice. As the good, so the sinner; as he who swears, so he who fears an oath. Those things which in themselves are neither good nor evil, but are called intermediate by the wise of the world (because they befall the just and the unjust alike), trouble all the simple, who ask why things turn out so, and on that account they think there is no judgment, although the distinguishing of all things lies in the future and here all things are confused. As for his saying: The event is one, to the just and to the impious, he signifies the event either of distress or of death, and that for this reason they know in themselves neither the love of God nor his hatred. He who sacrifices also, and he who does not sacrifice, and the rest that are listed as contraries, are to be taken spiritually, according to that saying: A sacrifice to God is a troubled spirit.

9:3-4

This is the worst in all that has been done under the sun, that there is one event for all. But also the heart of the sons of men is filled with malice, and errors are in their heart in their life, and after these things to the dead. For who is there who shares with all the living? This too Symmachus, in his usual way, translated more openly, saying: But also the heart of the sons of men is filled with malice and insolence according to their heart in their life. And their last things come to the dead. For who can persevere, living, forever? Scripture repeats the same sense which we treated a little before: that although all things befall all alike, and there is no distinction in enduring good things or evils, or at any rate we are all withdrawn from this world by an equal death, we are nonetheless filled with errors and insolence and malice, and after all these things we are suddenly snatched away by destruction, and can no longer have fellowship with the living. Or at any rate thus: because common distresses press upon both the just and the unjust, for that reason men are provoked to sins; and yet after all the efforts with which they strove in vain, while they do not know it, they go down to hell.

There is confidence, because a living dog is better than a dead lion.

9:5-6

For the living know that they will die, and the dead know nothing, and there is no more reward for them. For their memory comes into oblivion. But also their love, and their hatred, and their zeal have already perished; and they have no portion any longer in the world, in all that is done under the sun. Because he had said above that the heart of the sons of men is filled with malice and insolence, and that after this everything is ended by death, now he fills out and repeats the same point: that as long as men live they can become just, but that after death no occasion for good work is given. For a living sinner can be better than a dead just man, if he is willing to pass over to his virtues. Or at any rate, than one who boasted himself in malice, power and insolence and has died, any poor man, however vile, can be better. Why? Because the living, through fear of death, can perform good works; but the dead can add nothing to what they once took with them from life; and all things are wrapped in oblivion, according to what is written in the psalm: I have been given over to oblivion, like one dead from the heart. But also their love, and hatred, and rivalry, and all that they could have in the world, is ended by the coming of death; nor can they any longer do anything justly, nor sin, nor add virtues, nor vices. Some, however, contradict this exposition, asserting that even after death we can both grow and diminish; and what he now says: And they will have no portion any longer in the world, in all that has been done under the sun, they understand in such a way as to say that they have no communion in this world and under this sun which we see, but that they do have it under another world, of which the Savior says: I am not of this world, and under the sun of justice; and that the opinion is not excluded which contends that, after we have departed from this world, rational creatures can both offend and earn merit. The Hebrew used to report that this little verse, in which it is said: Better is a living dog above a dead lion, is expounded otherwise among his people, thus: that one who is still alive and teaching, however unlearned, is more useful than a perfect teacher who is already dead. For example, so that by the dog he understood any one teacher out of many, and by the lion Moses, or any other of the prophets. But since this exposition does not please us, let us press on to greater things; and let us say that that Canaanite woman to whom it was said: Your faith has saved you, is the dog according to the Gospel; and that the dead lion is the people of the circumcision, as the prophet Balaam says: Behold, the people will rise up like a lion's whelp, and like an exulting lion. The living dog, then, is we from the nations; but the dead lion is the people of the Jews, forsaken by the Lord. And better before God is this living dog than that dead lion. For we, the living, know the Father, and the Son, and the Holy Spirit; but they, the dead, know nothing, nor do they await any promise and reward, but their memory is at an end. Neither do they themselves remember what they ought to have known, nor does the Lord any longer remember them. The love also with which they once loved God has perished, and the hatred of which they used to speak boldly: Did I not hate those who hate you, O Lord, and pine away over your enemies? And their zeal too, according to which Phinehas was zealous and the knees of Mattathias trembled. And it is plain that their portion too is not in the world. For they cannot say: The Lord is my portion.

9:7-8

Go and eat your bread in gladness, and drink your wine with a good heart, since your works have already pleased God. At all times let your garments be white, and let oil not fail from your head. Up to the place in which he says: Like fishes which are held in a most evil capture; like birds which are caught in a snare: so will the sons of men be caught in a most evil time, when it falls upon them suddenly. Before we discuss them one by one, everything must be briefly drawn together, so that it may appear in what single sense they are all spoken. In the preceding passage he had said beforehand that after men have died they fall away from the heart of the living, and no one has either love or hatred toward them, according to that saying of the poet: "No contest with the vanquished and those bereft of air"; and that under the sun they can do nothing further. Now, therefore, he brings in, as it were, human error and the habit by which men urge one another to enjoy the goods of this world, and, in the manner of the rhetoricians and poets, he makes a προσωποποιΐαν (personification), saying: O man, since after death there is nothing, and death itself is nothing, hear my counsel: while you live in this short life, enjoy pleasure, make use of feasts, drown your cares in wine, and understand that these things have been given you by God for use. Go about adorned in white garments, let your head breathe of perfumes; whichever of women pleases you, rejoice in her embrace, and run through this vain and brief life with a vain and brief pleasure. For beyond these things you will have nothing more to enjoy; whatever can delight you, seize it in haste, lest it perish. For you ought not to fear trifling fictions, that among the dead below you must render an account of each of your works, whether good or evil. For there is no wisdom in death; there is no sensation after the dissolution of this life. Let some Epicurus say these things, he says, and Aristippus, and the Cyrenaics, and the rest of the philosophers' cattle. But I, going over it carefully with myself, find, not, as some wrongly suppose, that all things are done by chance and that a changeable fortune plays in human affairs, but that all things come about by the judgment of God. For neither should one who is swift of foot think the race his own if he runs; nor should the strong man trust in his own strength; nor should the wise man suppose that riches and wealth are gathered by prudence; nor the eloquent and learned man that through eloquence and learning he can find favor with the people; but that all things come about by God's disposing. And unless he rules all things by his own will and builds the house, they have labored in vain who build it. Unless he guards the city, they watch in vain who guard it. There is not, therefore, as those men think, one event and an uncertain state of this life, because, when they do not expect it, they will be carried off by a sudden death and come to judgment. And as fishes are caught by the hook and by nets, and birds that are free through the air are bound by a snare while they do not know it, so men will be led away to eternal punishments according to their desert, when death and judgment come suddenly upon those who thought that all things were tossed about in an uncertain state. This much according to the sense in which we wished briefly to take in the whole. Now the particulars must be interpreted as though he were speaking not in another's person but in his own: Go, eat your bread in gladness, and drink wine with a good heart; for your works have well pleased God. Since you have learned that all things are ended by death, and that in hell there is no repentance, nor any return to the virtues, while you are in this world, hasten, strive, repent while you have time, labor. For God gladly receives the penitent. Otherwise: taken simply, too, it is profitable, according to that saying: Whether you eat, or drink, or whatever you do, do all things in the name of the Lord. And in another place: Drink wine with counsel. For he who abuses created things beyond measure does not have true gladness and a good heart. But it is better to think thus: he whose works have well pleased before God will never be able to lack the true bread, and the wine that has been trodden out from the vine of Sorek. So a precept has been given us which says: You have desired wisdom: keep the commandments, and the Lord will supply it to you. Let us keep the commandments, and we shall be able to find the spiritual bread and wine. But to him who does not keep the commandments, and boasts in the abundance of bread and of wine, it is said through Isaiah: Do not say also: I know it; you have neither known, nor do you know, nor from the beginning did I open your ears. For I know that, despising, you despise. Further, what is said in the Seventy translators: Come, eat your bread in gladness, is the voice of that Ecclesiastes who also speaks in the Gospel: Whoever thirsts, let him come to me and drink. And in Proverbs: Come, eat my loaves and drink my wine. At all times let your garments be white, and let oil not be lacking from your head. Have, he says, a clean body, and be merciful. Or thus: let there be no time in which you do not have white garments; beware of ever putting on polluted clothes. For the sinful people is described as having mourned in dark garments. But you, put on light, and not the curse that is written over Judas: Let him be clothed with cursing as with a garment. Put on bowels of mercy, kindness, humility, meekness, patience. And when you have stripped off the old man with his works, put on the new, who is renewed from day to day. As for his saying: And let oil not be lacking from your head, it must be known that the nature of oil is this: it both feeds light and relieves the labor of the weary. There is a spiritual oil, the oil of exultation, of which it is written: Therefore God, your God, has anointed you with the oil of exultation above your fellows. With this oil our face is to be made cheerful. With this the head of one who fasts is to be anointed, an oil which sinners cannot have, to whom it is said: There is no plaster to apply, nor oil, nor bandages. For they have a contrary oil, which the just man detests, saying: Let not the oil of the sinner fatten my head. This oil the heretics have, and with it they long to drench the heads of those they deceive.

9:9

See life with the woman whom you have loved, all the days of your vanity which have been given you under the sun, all the days of your vanity; for this is your portion in life and in the labor in which you labor under the sun. Follow wisdom and the knowledge of the Scriptures, and join her to yourself in marriage, she of whom it is said in Proverbs: Love her and she will keep you: embrace her and she will encircle you. The days of vanity signify the days of this wicked world. Of this the Apostle too is not silent. As for his saying: See life with the woman whom you have loved, it is said ambiguously: either see and contemplate life, both you yourself and your wife with you (for without such a wife you will not be able to see life alone), or consider both, and see both life and the woman in the days of your vanity. And he has finely commanded that in the days of our vanity we seek the true life with wisdom as our wife. For this is our portion and this the fruit of our labor, if in this shadowy life we are able to find the true life.

9:10

All that your hand finds to do, do it in your strength, for there is no work, and thought, and knowledge, and wisdom in hell, where you are going there. Do whatever you can now, and labor, because when you have gone down to hell there will be no place for repentance. Something like this is also commanded by the Savior: Work while it is day; night will come, when no one will be able to work. As for his saying: In hell, where you are going there, note it, so that you may believe that Samuel too was truly in hell, and that before the coming of Christ all, however holy, were held by the law of hell. Further, that after the resurrection of the Lord the saints are in no way held in hell, the Apostle testifies, saying: It is better to be dissolved and to be with Christ. But he who is with Christ is certainly not held in hell.

9:11

I turned and saw under the sun that the race is not of the swift, nor the battle of the strong, nor bread of the wise, nor riches of the prudent, nor grace of the knowing; for time and event befall all these. He who is bound with iron fetters and is weighed down by the heavy bonds of lead: For iniquity sits upon a talent of lead; and in the psalm he says: Like a heavy burden they have weighed heavy upon me. He is not fit for that race of which it is said: I have finished the race, I have kept the faith. But he who is light, and whose soul is not weighed down, even he nevertheless cannot reach the goal without God as his helper. And also when there is a battle against the opposing powers, of which it is written: Sanctify war, though a man be strong, he will not be able to conquer by his own strength. Even the one who is perfect among the sons of men, and wise, will not be able to have the living and heavenly bread except through Wisdom exhorting: Come, eat my loaves. And since there are riches of which the Apostle says: To become rich in good works; and elsewhere: You have been made rich in all speech and in all knowledge, it must be known that the prudent man cannot gather these riches unless he has received them from God, whose these riches are. Of them it is also said elsewhere: The redemption of a man's soul, his own riches. Grace also, unless it has accompanied knowledge and been granted by God, a man, however learned, will not be able to find. Paul too knew this: More, he says, than all have I labored; yet not I, but the grace which is with me. And again: His grace in me was not void. And finally, it is not known by man when the time will come in which a varied event and the end of all things follow. These things according to ἀναγωγήν (the higher, anagogical sense).

But, to discuss it more simply, the epistle to the Romans fits this passage: That it is not of him who wills nor of him who runs, but of God who shows mercy. As for his saying: There is no bread for the wise, it is proved daily by the example of many who, though they are most wise, lack necessities. And: There is no grace for the knowing. For you may see in the Church all the most unskilled flourishing; and because they have nourished a bold brow and acquired a voluble tongue, while they do not reflect on what they say, they consider themselves prudent and learned, especially if they have had the favor of the crowd, which is more delighted and moved by lighter sayings. And on the contrary, you may see the learned man lying hidden in obscurity, suffering persecutions, and not only having no favor among the people but wasting away in want and poverty. These things happen because all things are borne along in an uncertain state, and the recompense of merits is not in the present but in the future.

9:12

And indeed man does not know his time, like fishes which are held in a most evil capture, as birds which are bound together by a snare; likewise the sons of man will fall in an evil time, when it falls upon them forthwith. We have already said above that, while men do not know it, either distress or destruction comes upon them in this way. Further, according to allegory, it must be known that the kingdom of heaven is like a dragnet cast into the sea; and that, on the contrary, the heretics have a dragnet by which they catch fishes for destruction. Their dragnet is affable speech, flattering eloquence, feigned or forced fasts, humble clothing, an imitation of the virtues. But if they have begun also to dispute about higher things, and to raise their mouth on high, and to search out the heights of God, they set a snare in lofty places. As, therefore, fishes and birds are quickly caught by such a dragnet and by a snare of this kind, so it is when iniquity has been multiplied and the love of many has grown cold, and signs and wonders have been done, so that even the elect, if it is possible, are led astray. It must be known that men of the Church too, who are called sons of men and are of little faith, can quickly fall. It must be noted also that throughout the whole book, wherever sons of men is said, the Hebrew has sons of man, that is, sons of Adam. And almost all of Scripture is full of this idiom, calling the whole human race sons of Adam.

9:13-15

But this wisdom too I saw under the sun, and it is great with me. A small city, and few men in it; and a great king came against it and surrounded it and built against it a great engine. And he found in it a poor and wise man, and this man saved the city by his wisdom; and man did not remember that poor man. While others say that all things are uncertain and that the just man has nothing more than the unjust, I have proved wisdom to be greatest even in this: that it often happens that a city is small and its inhabitants few, and it is girded by an army of countless enemies, and the people within are being killed by siege and famine; and suddenly, against everyone's expectation, a humble and poor man is found who, because he has wisdom greater than all the rich, while the great, the powerful and the proud are placed in danger and are terrified at the siege, thinks, inquires and finds how to rescue the city from its evils. And, O the ungrateful forgetfulness of men! after they have been freed, and the captivity loosed, and liberty restored to their country, no one remembers that poor wise man, no one gives thanks for their deliverance, but all honor the rich, who in the danger could give no help. The Hebrew interpreted this passage otherwise, thus: The small city is man, who even among the philosophers is called a lesser world. And the few men in it are the members of which man himself is woven together. And when the great king, the devil, has come against it and has sought a place through which he can break in, there is found in it the humble, wise and quiet thought of the inner man, and it preserves the city, which was encircled and besieged by enemies. And when the man has been rescued from the danger, whether of persecution, or of distress, or of any adverse thing and sin, that outer man, who is the enemy of that poor and wise man, does not remember the inner man nor submit himself to his counsels, but again enjoys his own liberty. Otherwise: The small city, and the few men in it, in comparison with the whole world, is the Church; against it the great king, the devil, often rises up, not because he is great, but because he boasts that he is great, and he surrounds it with a siege, either of persecution or of some other kind of distress. He finds in it a poor and wise man, our Lord Jesus Christ, who became poor for us and is wisdom itself; and that poor man frees the city by his wisdom. How often have we seen the lion who sits in ambush with the rich, that is, with the senators and princes of this world, and schemes against the Church, fall by the wisdom of this poor man? And when this poor man has conquered and the city has been restored to peace, scarcely anyone remembers him, scarcely anyone heeds his commandments; but, giving themselves wholly to luxury and pleasures, they seek riches, which do not free them in need.

9:16

And I said: Better is wisdom above strength, and the wisdom of the poor man, which is despised, and his words, which are not heard. Although no one remembers that poor wise man when all things are joyful, but all admire power and riches, yet I, according to all the interpretations above, honor the more the despised wisdom and the words which no one deigns to hear.

9:17

The words of the wise are heard in quiet, more than the shout of one who has power among fools. Whomever you see in the Church as a shouter, stirring up applause with a certain allurement and charm of words, provoking laughter, rousing the hearers to feelings of gladness, know that it is a sign of foolishness, both of him who speaks and of those who hear. For the words of the wise are heard in quiet and in measured silence; but he who is foolish, however powerful he may be, and though he has the shouting either of his own voice or of the people acclaiming him, speaks among the foolish.

9:18

Better is wisdom above the instruments of war, and one sinning will destroy much goodness. Now too he prefers wisdom to strength, and says that it avails more in battles than the arms of the fighters; and that if there is one foolish man, however small and of no account, yet often through his folly great wealth and riches are overthrown. But since in the Hebrew it can be read: And he who sins in one thing will destroy much goodness, it must be understood in this way too: that on account of one sin many past acts of justice perish, and that the virtues follow one another, and he who has one has all, and he who has sinned in one is subject to all the vices.

10:1

Flies of death pollute the oil of compounding: a little folly is more precious than wisdom and glory. He has given an example of the preceding thought, in which he says that through one fool many good things can be overthrown: that a bad man mixed with the good contaminates very many, in the same way as flies, if they die in ointment, destroy both its odor and its color. And because wisdom is often mixed with cunning, and prudence has malice in it, he commands us to seek a simple wisdom, and that it be mixed with the innocence of doves; that we be prudent for good, but simple toward evil. And the sense is: It befits the just man to have a little simplicity and, because of his great patience, while he reserves vengeance to God, to seem a fool, rather than to avenge himself at once and practice malice under the veil of prudence. Otherwise: The flies which, according to Isaiah, rule over part of the river of Egypt destroy the sweetness of the oil, leaving in each one of the believers the odor and the traces of their uncleanness. From these flies the prince of the demons too has been called Beelzebub, which means either idol of flies, or man of flies, or having flies.

10:2-3

The heart of the wise man is in his right hand, and the heart of the fool in his left. But also on the way, when the fool walks, his heart is diminished; and he says, everyone is foolish. In the Gospel too it is commanded that the left hand should not know what the right hand of the wise man does. And when we are struck on the right cheek, we are not bidden to offer the left cheek to the one who strikes us, but the other. For the just man has no left in him, but all in him is right. And when the Savior comes to judge, the lambs will stand on the right, but the kids on the left. And in the prophet it is written: The Lord knows the right ways, but those which are perverse are from the left. He therefore who is wise always thinks of the world to come, which leads to the right; but he who is foolish thinks of the present one, which is set on the left. Following these things, the same philosopher and poet says: "The right hand, which runs beneath the walls of great Dis: this is our road to Elysium; but the left exacts the punishments of the wicked and sends them to impious Tartarus."

Our Firmianus too, in the excellent work of his Institutes, makes mention of the letter, and has discussed most fully the right and the left, that is, the virtues and the vices. Nor let us think that this little sentence is contradicted by the one in which it is said: Do not turn aside to the right, nor to the left. For here the right side is taken for good; but there it is not so much the right that is accused as the turning aside of the right: that we should not be wise more than we need to be wise, because the virtues are in the middle and every excess is in vice. As for the following little verse, in which he says: But also on the way, when the fool walks, his heart is wanting, and he says: all is folly, or foolish, this is the sense: The fool, as he himself sins, expects that all sin alike, and judges everyone by his own nature. Indeed Symmachus translated thus: But also on the way, when the fool walks, he himself, being foolish, suspects of all that they are fools. But the Seventy made another sense, so as to say: All things that the foolish man thinks are most vain.

10:4

If the spirit of him who has power rises up over you, do not leave your place, because healing makes great sins to rest. Scripture here signifies the prince of this world, the ruler of darkness, the one working in the sons of unbelief, whom the Apostle too mentions. If he rises up into our heart and the mind has received the wound of an evil thought, we ought not to give him place any longer, but fight against the very evil thought and be freed from the greatest sin, namely, that we not complete the thought in deed; because it is one thing to sin in thought, another to sin in deed. Of this great sin it is also written in the psalm: If they have not had dominion over me, then I shall be spotless and shall be cleansed from the greatest offense. The Hebrew word marphe, which all the others likewise translated ἴαμα, that is, healing or cure, Symmachus interpreted according to the sense, and says: If the spirit of the prince rushes upon you, do not withdraw from your place; because chastity represses great sins. That is, if the devil tickles your mind and stirs you to lust, do not follow the very evil thought and the flattering pleasure, but stand strong and unbending, and with the cold of chastity put out the flame of pleasure. The Hebrew conjectured something of this sort in this place: If you have received some dignity in the world and have been ordained greater among the peoples, do not abandon your former works, and do not begin to forget your ancient virtues and to give up your former labor, because the remedy for sins is born of good conduct, and not of a swollen and superfluous dignity.

10:5-7

There is an evil which I have seen under the sun, as it were ignorance going forth from the face of the powerful one: that a fool is set in great heights and the rich sit in a low place. I have seen slaves on horses and princes walking like slaves upon the earth. In place of what we have put: As it were ignorance going forth from the face of the powerful one, Aquila and Theodotion and the Seventy translated: As it were something not voluntary, that is, ὡς ἀκούσιον, from the face of the prince. Joining on to this, Symmachus says: A fool set in great height; but the rich sitting humble. And so he records that he has observed this iniquity too in the world, which makes the judgment of God seem unjust: that, whether through ignorance or apart from his will, it happens that, either in the powers of the world or in the government of the Church, those who are rich in speech and wisdom, rich also in good works, often sit without honor, while any imprudent man holds the government in the Church. And this happens from the countenance of him who has power in the world, while he presses down all men of power and learning and does not allow them to appear among the peoples, but makes those whom he knows to be imprudent greater in the churches, so that the blind are led by the blind into the pit. To this sense also belongs what follows: I have seen slaves upon horses and princes walking like slaves upon the earth: that those who are slaves of vices and of sin, or so lowly that they are reckoned slaves by men, puffed up by the devil with sudden dignity, wear down the public roads with their ponies; and every noble or prudent man, weighed down by poverty, goes on foot, in the manner and office of slaves. The Hebrew turned to the powerful one and prince, from whose face ignorance seems to go forth. God, he explained, is that one, because men think that, in this inequality of things, he does not judge justly and as is fair. Others, moreover, think it should be joined with the preceding, so that the powerful one is he of whom it is said before these verses: If the spirit of him who has power rises up over you, do not give place. Let us not, then, be sad if in this world we seem to be lowly, knowing that it is from the face of the devil that both fools are raised up and the rich cast down? That slaves have the insignia of masters, and princes go about in the vileness of slaves. It should be noted, moreover, that here too the horse is taken in a good sense, as there: And your riding is salvation.

10:8

He who digs a pit will fall into it, and he who breaks down a hedge, a serpent will bite him. The understanding is partly simple and partly mystical. For elsewhere too Solomon himself says: He who sets a snare will be caught in it. And in the seventh psalm: He opened a hole and dug it out, and he will fall into the pit which he made. As for the tearing down of the hedge and the wall: these are the doctrines of the Church and the institution founded by the apostles and prophets; whoever loosens them and chooses to pass beyond them is struck by the serpent in that very thing which he neglects. Of this serpent it is also written in Amos: If he goes down into hell, I will command the serpent and it will bite him.

10:9

He who removes stones will be pained by them; and he who splits wood will be endangered by it. In Zechariah too holy stones are rolled upon the earth. For they do not stand firm in that station, but pass on, and, always striving toward higher things, hasten to depart from here. Of these living stones the city of the Savior is built in the Apocalypse too, and the Apostle does not keep silent that the Church is built of them. If, then, someone perverted by heretical art has taken these stones away from the building of the Church – whence Aquila and Symmachus too pointedly said, in place of what we have put: He who removes stones will be pained by them: He who transfers stones will be torn apart by them – he will afterwards suffer torments. But because Scripture says absolutely, he who removes stones, or, he who transfers stones, and did not add good or bad, it must therefore also be understood the other way round: that a man of the Church, a bishop, namely, or a presbyter, if according to the commandment of Leviticus he has taken out of a leprous house a stone to be ground into dust and ashes, will be pained by that very fact, that he is compelled to remove a stone from the Church of Christ, saying with the Apostle: To weep with those who weep, to mourn with those who mourn; and: Who is weak, and I do not burn? Splitting wood too, he will be endangered by it. Heretics are unfruitful wood, and woodland with no benefit of fruit. Hence in the temple of God too a grove is forbidden to be planted, and the empty shades of leaves, that is, of words that merely make a sound, are spurned. However prudent and learned, then, the man may be who cuts down this wood with the sword of his speech, he will be endangered by it unless he takes careful heed; above all if what follows befalls him: If the iron is blunted and has troubled its face, that is, if his argument has been found weaker, and has not had the edge with which to cut down every contrary thing, but the ruling part of his heart is dulled; he will pass over to the opposite side, and a perverse strength will confirm him. For this is what the Seventy translators rendered: And with strength he will be strengthened; and superfluous wisdom of the strong man he will begin, he says; he will begin to have strength, a wisdom which is superfluous and does not help its possessor.

10:10

If the iron is blunted, and it is not as before, but will be troubled, it will be strengthened with powers; and the remainder of strength is wisdom. If anyone, he says, sees that through negligence he has lost the knowledge of the Scriptures, and the edge of his mind has been blunted; and, troubled, he has by no means remained such as he had already begun to be. For it sometimes happens that when someone has had a little knowledge, he is lifted up into pride and ceases to learn and to read, and little by little, since nothing is added to him, he loses something, and his breast remains empty of learning, and the iron that had been sharp grows dull. For idleness and sloth are a kind of rust on wisdom. If, then, anyone has suffered this, let him not despair of the remedy of health, but let him go to a teacher and be instructed again by him, and after labor and diligence and much sweat he will be able to recover the wisdom which he had lost. And this is what is said more expressively in the Hebrew: With strengths he will be strengthened, that is, by labor and sweat and diligence and daily reading he will attain wisdom, and his strength will have this end, that he receives wisdom.

10:11

If a serpent bites in silence, there is nothing more to him who has a tongue. The simple sense here is: The serpent and the detractor are equal. For as the one, biting secretly, injects venom, so the other, detracting in secret, pours out the poison of his breast upon his brother, and has nothing more than the serpent. For whereas the tongue of man was created for blessing and for the edification of his neighbor, he makes it equal to the serpent, while he abuses its powers for perversity. Otherwise: If the serpent, the devil, has bitten someone secretly and, with no one aware of it, has infected him with the venom of sin; if he who has been struck keeps silent and does not show repentance, and is not willing to confess his wound to his brother and teacher, then the teacher and brother, who have a tongue for healing, will not easily be able to help him. For if the sick man is ashamed to confess his wound to the physician, medicine does not cure what it does not know.

10:12

The words of the mouth of the wise man are grace, and the lips of the foolish man will cast him headlong. Folly, if it were content with its own rusticity, would have less evil in it. But as it is, it wages war against wisdom, and whatever prudence it sees in a learned man, goaded by jealousy, it does not accept. For the wise man speaks words of knowledge, words of grace, which can bring profit to the hearers; and the lips of the fool do not receive what is said as it was said, but on the contrary try to trip up the prudent man and make him like themselves. And truly the wise man is cast headlong when he speaks into the ear of an imprudent man, and his words perish in a deep whirlpool, so to speak. Hence blessed is he who speaks into an ear that hears.

10:13-14

The beginning of his words is foolishness, and the end of his mouth is the worst error. And the fool multiplies words. Man does not know what it is that has been made; and what will be after him, who will announce to him? The discussion still concerns the fool, whose lips cast the wise man headlong, or, according to another interpretation, make the fool himself fall. For the beginning and the end of his speech are folly and the worst error; or, as Symmachus translated: tumult, and a kind of inconstancy of words; since he does not abide by a judgment, but thinks that by multiplying speech he can escape sin. For since he neither remembers things past nor knows things to come, and wallows in ignorance and darkness, promising himself a false knowledge, he thinks himself learned in this, and wise in this: if he multiplies words. This can also be taken of the heretics, who do not grasp the sayings of prudent men, but, preparing themselves for contrary disputations, wrap up both the beginning and the end of their speaking in vanity, tumult and error; and though they know nothing, they speak more than they know.

10:15

The labor of fools will afflict those who do not know how to go into the city. Join these little verses too with the preceding; he is discussing either generally all fools who are ignorant of God, or specifically the heretics. Read Plato; unroll the subtleties of Aristotle; look more carefully at Zeno and Carneades, and you will prove true what is said: The labor of fools will afflict them. They indeed sought the truth with all zeal; but because they did not have a leader and guide on the way, and thought that with human senses they could grasp wisdom, they by no means came to the city of which it is said in the psalm: Lord, in your city you will scatter their image. For all the shadows and the various images and masks which they put on themselves in their various doctrines, the Lord will scatter in his city. Of it it is written elsewhere too: The rush of the river gladdens the city of God. And in the Gospel: A city set on a mountain cannot be hidden. And in Isaiah: I am a strong city, a city which is besieged. For this city of truth and wisdom, strong and sturdy though it is, all try to attack, both the wise of the world and the heretics. And what we have said of the philosophers must be held likewise of the heretics: that they labor in vain and are afflicted in the study of the Scriptures, since they walk in the desert and cannot find the city. Of their error the psalmist too makes mention, saying: They wandered in the desert and in a waterless place; they did not find the way of the city and of its habitation.

10:16-17

Woe to you, land, whose king is a youth, and your princes eat in the morning. Blessed are you, land, whose king, your own, is the son of the freeborn, and your princes eat in due time, in strength and not in confusion. He seems indeed to reprove the rule of the young and to condemn luxurious judges, because in the one wisdom is weak by reason of age, and in the others even a mature age is enervated by delights. And on the contrary to approve a prince of good character and liberal education; and to praise those judges who by no means put pleasure before the affairs of the citizens, but take food after much labor and administration of the commonwealth, as if compelled by necessity. But to me something more sacred seems to lie hidden in the letter: that those are called young men in Scripture who fall away from the old authority and despise the gray-haired precepts of their parents; who, neglecting the commandment of God, desire to establish the traditions of men. Concerning them, in Isaiah too, the Lord threatens Israel, because it would not have the water of Siloam, which goes in silence, and turned away from the old pool, choosing for itself the streams of Samaria and the floods of Damascus. And I will give, he says, young men as their princes, and mockers shall rule over them. Read Daniel and you will find God the Ancient of days. Read the Apocalypse of John and you will find the head of the Savior white as snow and like white wool. Jeremiah too, because he was wise and his gray hairs were reckoned in his wisdom, is forbidden to say that he is a youth. Woe, then, to the land whose king is the devil, who, ever greedy for revolution, rebels even in Absalom against his parent; the land which has as judges and princes those who love the pleasures of this world, who, before the day of death comes, say: Let us eat and drink; for tomorrow we shall die. On the contrary, blessed is the land of the Church, whose king is Christ, the son of the freeborn, descending from Abraham and Isaac and Jacob, and from the stock of the prophets also and of all the saints, over whom sin did not have dominion; and therefore they were truly free. From them was born a virgin freer still, holy Mary, having no shrub, no shoot from her side; but all her fruit burst forth into a flower, which speaks in the Song of Songs: I am the flower of the field and the lily of the valleys. Its princes too are the apostles and all the saints, who have as king the son of the freeborn, the son of the free woman, begotten not of the handmaid Hagar, but of the freedom of Sarah. Nor do they eat in the morning, nor in haste. For they do not seek pleasure in the present world, but they will eat in their own time, when the time of retribution has come, and they will eat in strength, and not in confusion. Every good of the present world is confusion; every good of the world to come is everlasting strength. Something of this kind is said in Isaiah too: Behold, those who serve me will eat; but you will be hungry. And again: Behold, those who serve me will rejoice; but you will be ashamed.

10:18

In slothfulness the timberwork will be brought low, and in weakness of hands the house will drip. Our house, which was raised up along with the standing of man, and the dwelling which we have in the heavens, will be brought low if we are slothful and slower to good works. And all the timberwork which ought to bear a lofty roof will collapse to the ground and crush its inhabitant. And when the help of the hands and of the virtues has grown torpid, every storm from above and the whirlwind of the rain-clouds bursts in upon us. Moreover, what we have interpreted of one man can better be taken of the Church: that through the negligence of its princes all its height collapses; and that the allurements of vices are there where the covering of virtues was thought to be.

10:19

In laughter they make bread and wine, that the living may feast. And all things obey silver. I think that what follows depends on the preceding. For we explained above that through the sloth and indolence of the teachers the Church is brought low and its roof falls in, and the beams are rained through. Now, then, he speaks of the same teachers. He had seemed to accuse them for keeping silent and not using the office of teachers: that, appointed bishops and presbyters in the Church, they did not labor in word and doctrine, which Titus too is admonished to do, and Timothy is commanded not to neglect the grace which he had received through the laying on of hands; but thought themselves presbyters and bishops in this, that they received salaries, and many teachers sought the double honor which is owed to those who labor in doctrine and word. Now, on the contrary, he accuses those who do indeed speak in the Church and teach the peoples, but teach what the people delight to hear, so that it strokes the sinner in his vice and stirs up the applause of the hearers. When in the Church the discourse runs wanton and promises blessedness and the kingdoms of heaven to the multitude, does it not seem to you to make bread in laughter and to mix wine for the gladness of the drinkers? Either because those who teach acquire riches and food and wealth through delightful promises; or because they make the bread of the Church, which is the bread of those who mourn and not of those who laugh – for blessed are those who mourn, since they themselves shall laugh – in gladness and joy. But what follows: All things will obey money, or silver, must be taken in two ways: either that the teachers themselves, after they have been enriched by flattery, exercise rule over the peoples; or certainly, because silver is always taken for speech: The words, indeed, of the Lord are pure words, silver tried by fire, of earth, purified sevenfold. He asserts this: that the ignoble crowd readily yields to eloquence and to a discourse composed of the foliage of words. Otherwise: Those who have the bridegroom with them, and are therefore forbidden to mourn and fast, make bread in laughter. From this laughter Isaac too received his name; and they prepare wine in the gladness of the drinkers. Every holy man, therefore, who, as Christ commanded, is a teacher of the Church, makes bread in laughter and in gladness, and serves cups of wine in joy. The silver too, which all things will obey, is those five talents of the householder from the Gospel, and the two, and the one, and the ten minas which are entrusted to the servants for trading.

10:20

In your mind do not curse the king, and in the secret of your bedchamber do not curse the rich man; because the birds of heaven will carry the voice, and that which has wings will announce the word. The simple precept too edifies the hearers: that we should not, overcome by anger and fury, burst out into cursing and detraction of kings and princes, because it sometimes happens, against all expectation, that it is reported to those whom we have cursed, and we incur danger through the intemperance of the tongue. But what he says: A bird of heaven will carry away the voice, and that which has wings will announce the word, is to be understood hyperbolically, as we are accustomed to say that even the very walls, which are privy to what we say, will not conceal what they have heard. But it is better to hear the precept thus, so that we may know that we are commanded not only to say nothing rashly against Christ, but also, even in the secret places of the heart, however much we are hemmed in by various tribulations, to think nothing blasphemous, nothing impious. And because the love which we show to Christ we owe also to our neighbor – You shall love, indeed, the Lord your God, but also your neighbor as yourself – this too is commanded: that after the king we should not readily detract from the saints as well, nor gnaw with a biting tongue those whom we see enriched with knowledge, wisdom and virtues; because the angels who go around the earth and are ministering spirits, and in Zechariah say: We have gone around the earth, and behold, all the earth is inhabited and is silent, carry our words and thoughts to heaven like birds; and what we think in secret does not escape the knowledge of God.

11:1

Send your bread upon the face of the water, for in the multitude of days you will find it. He exhorts to almsgiving: one must give to everyone who asks and do good without discrimination. For just as he who sows upon well-watered ground looks for the fruit of his sowing, so he who bestows on the needy sows not a grain of seed but the bread itself, awaiting its multiplication as with a kind of interest; and when the day of judgment has come, he will find much more than he had given. Otherwise: In whatever man you see that water of which it is said: Rivers of living water will flow from his belly, do not be reluctant to supply the bread of wisdom, the rational bread, the bread of the word. For if you do this often, you will find that you have not cast the seed of teaching in vain. Something of the same kind, I think, is said in Isaiah too: Blessed is he who sows upon the water, where the ox and the ass tread. That is, that teacher is held worthy of blessedness who sows upon the well-watered breast of his hearers, a people gathered from the Jews as well as from the Gentiles.

11:2

Give a portion to seven and also to eight, for you do not know what evil is to come upon the earth. In Ezekiel too seven and eight steps are read of for the ascent to the temple. And after that ethical psalm, that is, the hundred and eighteenth (119th), come the fifteen psalms of the steps, through which we are first instructed in the law, and then, the number seven being completed, we afterwards climb through the ogdoad to the Gospel. We are commanded, therefore, to believe in both Testaments, the Old, that is, as well as the New, with equal reverence. The Jews gave a portion to seven, believing in the sabbath, but they did not give one to eight, denying the resurrection of the Lord's day. Conversely the heretics, Marcion and Mani and all who tear the old law to pieces with rabid mouth, give a portion to eight, accepting the Gospel; but they do not grant the same to the number seven, spurning the old law. Let us therefore believe both Testaments. For we cannot even now grasp with the mind the torments they deserve and the deserved punishment that is laid up for those who dwell on the earth, the Jews and the heretics, who each deny one of the two. The Hebrews understand this passage thus: Keep both the sabbath and circumcision, lest, if perhaps you do not do this, an unexpected evil come upon you.

11:3

If the clouds are filled, they will pour out rain upon the earth. And if the tree falls toward the south or toward the north; in the place where the tree falls, there it will be. Keep the commandments that were enjoined on you above, so that the clouds may pour their rain upon you. For wherever you have prepared a place for yourself and a future seat, whether toward the south or toward the north: there you will remain when you have died. Otherwise: This is why we said above: Send your bread upon the face of the water and give to everyone who asks you; because the clouds too, when they are full, bestow their riches on mortals, and you, like a tree, however long-lived you may be, will not last forever, but, overthrown by a sudden tempest, of death as of the winds, wherever you fall, there you will remain perpetually, whether your last hour finds you hard and fierce or gentle and merciful. Otherwise: It is said to God in the psalms: Your truth reaches even to the clouds; and in Isaiah God threatens the sinning vineyard: I will command the clouds not to rain rain upon it. The clouds, then, are the prophets and every holy man, who, when he has gathered many teachings in his heart, will then be able to rain down the precepts of doctrine and to say: Let speech be awaited like the rain, and they will pour out rain upon the earth; to which it was said: Let the earth hear the words of my mouth. But as for what follows: And if the tree falls toward the south or toward the north: in the place where the tree falls, there it will be; let us take the example from Habakkuk, in which it is written: God will come from Teman, which other interpreters have rendered: God will come from the south. And so far as I judge, the south is always taken in a good sense. Hence in the Song of Songs too it is said: Arise, north wind, that is, withdraw and go away; and come, south wind. The tree, then, that has fallen in this life and been cut down by the condition of mortality either sinned before while it stood, and is afterwards placed in the quarter of the north; or, if it bore fruits worthy of the south, it will lie in the southern region. Nor is there any tree that is not either toward the north or toward the south. The same thing is signified also by that which is written: I will say to the north wind: Bring; and to the south-west wind: Do not hold back. For the south wind and the east wind are never commanded to bring, because those who are afterwards to be led to the east and the south must be in other regions. The north wind, therefore, brings its inhabitants to the south, and the south-west wind brings its own to the east. For they cannot make progress if they remain in their former seats.

11:4

He who watches the wind does not sow; and he who looks at the clouds will not reap. He who considers to whom he should do good, and does not give to everyone who asks him, often passes over the one who deserves to receive. Otherwise: He who preaches the word of God only at the time when the people listen gladly and the favorable breath of popular report blows is a negligent sower and a lazy farmer. For in prosperity itself, while we are unaware, adversities arise. But in season, out of season, at its own steady pace, the word of God must be preached; and in the time of faith the storm of adverse clouds is not to be considered. Of it it is said in Proverbs: Like a violent and useless rain, so are those who forsake wisdom and praise impiety. Without regard, then, for the clouds and without fear of the winds, we must sow in the midst of storms. Nor must we say: That time is convenient, this one useless, since we do not know what is the way and what the will of the Spirit who disposes all things.

11:5

As you do not know what is the way of the spirit, and as the bones in the womb of a pregnant woman, so you will not know the works of God, who will make all things. Just as you do not know the way of the spirit and of the soul entering into the little child, and are ignorant of the varieties of the bones and veins in the belly of the pregnant woman: how from a cheap element the body of man is diversified into different shapes and limbs, and from the same seed one part grows soft in the flesh, another is hardened in the bones, another throbs in the veins, another is bound in the sinews: so you will not be able to know the works of God, who is the maker of all things. From this he teaches that adversities are not to be feared, nor should one judge rashly about the winds and clouds of which we spoke above; since the sower ought to go on at his own steady pace and course and leave the event to the sentence of the Lord. For it is not of him who wills, nor of him who runs, but of God who shows mercy.

11:6-8

In the morning sow your seed, and toward evening do not slacken your hand: for you do not know which may please, this or that. And if both, it is good as though one; and the light is sweet, and it is good for the eyes to see the sun; for if a man lives many years, let him rejoice in all of them, and let him remember the days of darkness, for they will be very many. All that is to come is vanity. Do not choose to whom you should do good; but also, when you have done good, never cease from good work. Let the evening find your morning justice, and let the rising sun heap up more upon your evening mercy. For it is uncertain which work pleases God more, and from which the fruit of justice is being prepared for you. And it can happen that not one but both please God. Otherwise: In youth and in old age alike let your labor be equal. And do not say: While I could, I labored; in old age I ought to rest. For you do not know whether you please God in young manhood or in advanced age. Nor does frugality in youth profit, if old age is passed in luxury. For on whatever day the just man goes astray, his old acts of justice will not be able to free him from death. But if, according to both interpretations, you always do good and keep an equal course at every age, you will see God the Father, the sweetest light; you will see Christ, the sun of justice. Furthermore, even if you live many years and have all good things, or have performed good works, and know that you are always on the point of dying, and the coming of darkness is always before your eyes: you will despise present things as fleeting, fragile and perishable. Symmachus rendered the end of this sentence thus: If a man lives many years and has been glad in all of them, he ought to remember also the days of darkness, for they will be many, in all of which there will come a ceasing to be. Otherwise: In another place of Scripture God promises, saying: I will give you the early and the late rain, that is, the Old Testament and the New, and I will water you with both showers. Hence we too are now admonished to read the old law in such a way as not to despise the Gospel, and to seek the spiritual understanding in the Old Testament in such a way as not to think that in the evangelists and in the apostles nothing sounds but what is read. For we do not know in which of the two knowledge and grace are granted to us more by God; and happy is he who has joined both together in common and made them as it were one body. Whoever has attained this will see the light, will see Christ, the sun of justice. And if he lives many years, he will dwell in the highest joy and delight, possessing the knowledge of the Scriptures, and he will be driven the more to this labor by the remembrance of the judgment to come; because an everlasting time of darkness will come, and eternal punishments will be prepared for those who did not sow in the morning and toward evening and in no way joined the two together, and who saw neither the light nor the sun, from which light itself goes forth.

11:9-10

Rejoice, young man, in your youth, and let your heart be in what is good in the days of your young manhood, and walk in the ways of your heart and in the sight of your eyes. And know this, that for all these things God will bring you into judgment. And drive anger from your heart and put away malice from your flesh, for youth and folly are vanity.

12:1

And remember your Creator in the day of your youth, before the days of malice come and the years draw near in which you will say: I have no will in them. On this passage the explanation of everyone has been different, and there are almost as many opinions as there are men. Hence, since it is a long task to review the opinions of all, and to unfold the arguments by which they wished to prove their views is almost the business of a whole volume, let it suffice for the prudent that I have indicated what they thought, and that, as on some small tablet, I have painted the positions of the lands and have shown in a narrow compendium the vastness of the whole globe and the circuit of the ocean.

The Hebrews think that the command pertains to Israel, who is instructed to enjoy his riches before the time of captivity comes upon him and he exchanges his youth for old age. Whatever appears delightful or pleasant, to the heart as much as to the eyes, let him use it to the full while it is at hand, yet in such a way that he knows he must be judged in all things; and let him flee evil thoughts as much as lusts, knowing that folly is coupled with youth, and let him always remember his Creator, before the days of the Babylonian and the Roman captivity come, in which he will not be able to have any will. And this whole passage, from where he says: Before the sun is darkened, and the moon and the stars, up to the place in which Scripture records: And the dust will be turned into its earth as it was, and the spirit will return to God, who gave it, they explain of the state of their own condition. Since these things, as we said above, are long-winded and prolix, they will be touched on by us briefly and in passing. Rejoice, therefore, in your youth, O Israel, and do this or that, of which we have already spoken, before the captivity comes, and your honor and glory depart from you, and your judges and your holy ones, whom they want to be understood by the sun, the moon and the stars, are taken away; before Nebuchadnezzar comes, or Titus the son of Vespasian, summoned by the prophets, and their prophecies are fulfilled. In the day in which the angels who preside over the temple will withdraw, and all the strongest men in your army will be troubled, and the discourses of the teachers will be idle, and the prophets, who used to receive from heaven the light of their visions, will be darkened. When the doors of the temple will be shut, and Jerusalem will be humbled, and the Chaldean will come, called forth by the cries of Jeremiah as by the song of a bird, and the daughters of song, the choirs of those singing psalms in the temple, will fall silent. At that time when the enemies themselves too, coming to Jerusalem, will dread the greatness of God, and, wavering on the way, will fear the destruction of Sennacherib. For they think this is said: And they will fear from on high and will dread on the way. In those days the almond will flower, that staff and rod which Jeremiah saw at the beginning of his prophecy, and the locust will grow fat, Nebuchadnezzar with his army, and the caper will be scattered, the friendship of God with Israel. But what the caper means will be explained more fully when we begin to speak of each point. And all this will befall Israel because man is about to go away into the house of his eternity, and God, his protection, is about to return to the heavenly places; and as he departs into his tabernacle, they will go about in the street weeping and lamenting, and hemmed in by the siege of their enemies. Rejoice, therefore, Israel, in your youth, before the silver cord is broken, that is, while your glory is with you, before the golden band runs back. That is, before the ark of the covenant is taken away; before the pitcher is crushed at the fountain and the wheel is rolled up over the cistern; that is, while the precepts of the law are in the holy of holies, and the grace of the Holy Spirit is there, and before you return to Babylon, from which you went forth in the loins of Abraham, and begin to be crushed in Mesopotamia, from which you once set out. And let all the grace of prophecy with which you had once been inspired return to its giver. These things the Jews expound to this day, and they draw the understanding of this passage to their own person.

But we, returning to the order of our earlier discussion, will try to explain each point: Rejoice, young man, in your youth, and let your heart be in good in the days of your youth, and walk in the ways of your heart and in the sight of your eyes. And know that over all these things God will bring you into judgment. He had said that the light of this world is most sweet, and that a man ought to rejoice in the days of his life and pluck pleasure with all eagerness. For the eternal night of death is coming on, when one may not enjoy what has been gathered, and all that we have had passes away like a shadow. Now, therefore, he exhorts man and says: O young man, before old age and death come upon you, rejoice in your youth, and whatever seems good to you in heart and pleasant in sight, take it; enjoy the things of the world as you please. Again, lest he should be thought by saying this to provoke man to luxury and to fall into the doctrine of Epicurus, he removed this suspicion by adding: And know that over all these things God will bring you into judgment. Use the things of the world to the full, he says, in such a way that you know you are to be judged at the last. And drive anger from your heart and put away malice from your flesh, for youth and folly are vanity. In anger he includes all the perturbations of the mind; by the malice of the flesh he signifies all the pleasures of the body. Enjoy, therefore, he says, the goods of this world in such a way that you do not sin either by desire or by the flesh. Leave the old vices by which in your youth you served vanity and folly, for youth is coupled with foolishness. And remember him who created you in the day of your youth, before the days of malice come and the years draw near in which you will say: I have no will in them. Always remember your Maker, and so walk the road of youth that you remember your final death, before the time comes upon you in which every sorrowful thing follows.

12:2

Before the sun is darkened, and the light, and the moon, and the stars, and the clouds return after the rain. If we take it of the general consummation of the world, this passage agrees with the words of the Lord, in which he says: There will be tribulation and distress such as has not been from the beginning of creation, nor will be. For the sun will be darkened and the moon will not give its light and the stars will fall from heaven, and the powers of the heavens will be moved. These are the keepers of the house: so that the house is understood as this world, while the strong men are those deceived by error, namely the opposing powers that are to be scattered. But if the particular consummation of each one is meant, it refers to the end of this life; and the sun and the moon and the stars and the clouds and the rains will cease to be for him who has died. Otherwise: Rejoice, young man, O Christian people, and enjoy the good things granted to you by God, and know that in all these you are to be judged by the Lord. And do not think that, because the former branches were broken off and you were grafted into the root of the good olive, you are therefore secure. But put away anger from your heart and pleasures from your body, and, leaving the other vices behind, remember your Creator, before the day of malice comes upon you, the incurable day, in which punishments are prepared for sinners; lest, when you have sinned, the sun of justice set for you at midday and the light of knowledge perish, and the splendor of the moon, that is, of the Church, be withdrawn, and the stars set, of which it is written: Among whom you shine like lights in the world, having the reason of life. And elsewhere: Star differs from star in glory. Before the clouds return after the rain; lest the prophets, who have watered the hearts of believers with their speech as with their rains, after they have perceived that you are unworthy of their shower, return to their seat; that is, to him by whom they were sent.

12:3

In the day when the keepers of the house have been moved and the men of strength have perished. The keepers of the house are understood either as the sun and the moon and the rest of the choir of stars, or as the angels who preside over this world. The men of strength, or the strong, as Symmachus translated, who will perish, or, as Aquila rendered, will go astray, are taken to be the demons, who have themselves also received the name of the strong from the strong devil. Him the Lord overcomes and binds, according to the parable of the Gospel, and lays waste his house. Otherwise: those who refer everything that is written to the body of man think that the keepers of the house signify the ribs, because by them the intestines are walled in and the whole softness of the belly is kept safe. The strong men they take to be the legs; the sun and the moon and the stars they fit to the eyes, the nostrils and the ears, and the senses of the whole head. And they do this because further on they are forced by necessity to understand what follows, not of angels and demons, sun and moon and stars, but of the members of man.

And the grinders will cease because they are diminished; and those that see in the holes will be darkened. At the consummation of the world, when the charity of many has grown cold, and the souls of the teachers, who can supply heavenly food to believers, have been diminished and transferred to the heavenly places, then those who in this world beheld the light of knowledge in part will begin to be wrapped in darkness. For if it is said to Moses: I will put you in the hole of the rock and so you will see my back parts, how much more does each soul behold the truth through a hole and through certain dark caverns! Otherwise: there are two women grinding, of whom the Gospel does not keep silent that one is taken and the other left. When these have been diminished and have ceased, all the light of knowledge must be taken away from the eyes. Otherwise: they think that the grinders ceasing because they are diminished is said of the teeth. For when extreme old age has come, the teeth too, by which food is ground fine and passed into the belly, are either worn down or fall out. And those that see in the holes being darkened they take to be the eyes, because in those worn out with age the sight grows dim and the gaze is obscured.

12:4

And they will shut the doors in the street in the lowness of the voice of the grinder; and he will rise at the voice of the bird; and all the daughters of song will grow dumb. When the voice of the grinder has been weakened and the teaching of the teachers has ceased, all things will consequently cease. Among them the doors too will be shut in the street, so that, as with the foolish virgins of the Gospel, each one may have the doors of his own street shut, and they may not be able to buy oil. Or certainly, while the foolish virgins go about in the street, those who have entered with the bridegroom will shut his chamber. For if narrow and strait is the way that leads to life, and wide and spacious the one that leads to death, rightly, when the charity of many has grown cold, the door of teachings is shut in the streets. The following little verse, however, in that he says: And he will rise at the voice of the bird, or sparrow, we shall use at the right time, if ever we see a sinner rising up through repentance at the voice of a bishop or a presbyter. This can also be taken, out of the ordinary course, even though we do not follow the context of the passage, of the proper resurrection, when at the voice of the archangel the dead will rise again. Nor is it to be wondered at if we compare the angel's trumpet to a sparrow, since every voice compared with Christ is thin. And as far as I can recall in my mind, I know that I have never read of the sparrow in a bad sense. The righteous man speaks in the tenth (11th) psalm: In the Lord I trust; how do you say to my soul: Move away to the mountain like a sparrow. And elsewhere: I have watched and become like a solitary sparrow on the roof. And also in another place: For the sparrow has found itself a house. Otherwise: they want the doors shut in the streets to be taken as the feeble steps of the old man, because he always sits and cannot walk. The lowness of the voice of the grinder they interpret of the jaws, because he cannot grind his food and, his breath being constricted, his thin voice is scarcely heard. Further, his rising at the voice of the bird shows that, the blood now growing cold and the moisture dried up, the materials by which sleep is nourished, he wakes at a slight sound, and in the middle of the night, when the cock has crowed, he gets up in haste, being by no means able to turn his limbs over often on the bed. The daughters of song growing dumb (obmutescere), too, or, as the Hebrew has it better, growing deaf (surdescere), signifies the ears, because the hearing of the old becomes duller and they can tell no difference between voices, nor take delight in songs. This indeed Barzillai also says to David, when he does not wish to cross the Jordan.

12:5

But they will also fear from the heights and will dread on the way. That is, they will not be able to go up steep places, and with weary knees and trembling step, wavering even on a level road, they will dread a stumble of their steps.

And the almond will flower, and the locust will grow fat, and the caper will be scattered, because man will go into the house of his eternity and they will go about in the street lamenting. By metaphor the discourse of Ecclesiastes is even now about the members of man: that when old age has come, the hair has turned white, the feet have swollen, lust has grown cold, and the man has been dissolved by death, then he will return to his earth and to the house of his eternity, the tomb, and, the funeral rites having been duly celebrated, a crowd of mourners will go before the bier. The flower of the almond (amygdalum), which we have set down for gray hairs, some interpret as the sacred spine, because as the flesh of the buttocks wastes away the spine grows out and flowers. Further, in that he says: The locust will grow fat, it must be known that where in our copies locust (locusta) is read, in the Hebrew is written aagab, a word which is ambiguous among them. For it can be translated both ankle (talus) and locust. So too at the beginning of Jeremiah the word soced, if the accent is varied, signifies both nut (nux) and watchings (uigiliae). And it is said to him: What do you see, Jeremiah? And he answered: A nut. And the Lord said to him: You have seen well, for I will watch over my word, to perform it. Or rather this: it also has the etymology of the nut, in that God is going to watch and to repay the people what it deserves; this is the occasion of the saying. So now too the ambiguity of the word, through its etymology, indicates the swelling legs of old men, weighed down with the humors of gout. Not that this happens to all old men, but because it happens for the most part, and συνεκδοχικῶς (by synecdoche) what is the whole is named from a part. Where we have caper (capparis), the Hebrew has abiona; which is itself also ambiguous, and is interpreted love, desire, lust, or caper. And what is signified, as we said above, is that the lust of old men grows cold and the organs of coition are scattered. This is so because these ambiguous words, while in their own terms they sound almond and locust and caper, signify something else when derived, and are derived by a figure to senses that suit an old man. It must also be known that where now the Seventy translators have put almond, the word itself is soced, which is at the beginning of Jeremiah; but there it was turned into nut, here into almond. Symmachus, thinking I know not what in this place, translated it very differently. For he says: And above these things they will also see from on high, and there will be error on the way, and the watchful one will fall asleep, and the strength of the spirit will be dissolved. For man will go into the house of his eternity, and they will go about in the street lamenting. The Laodicean, following his interpretation, can please neither the Jews nor the Christians, since he is both far from the Hebrews and disdains to follow the Seventy translators.

12:6-8

Before the cord of silver is broken, and the golden fillet runs back, and the pitcher is crushed upon the fountain, and the wheel is broken upon the cistern. And the dust returns into its earth as it was, and the spirit goes back to God, who gave it. Vanity of vanities, said Ecclesiastes, everything is vanity. He returns to what came before, and after a great hyperbaton – which he had interposed from the place where he says: And remember your Creator, in the day of your youth; before the days of malice come, and before the sun and moon are darkened, and the rest, in the day in which the keepers of the house will be moved –. Now he concludes the sentence he had begun with a similar ending, saying: Before the cord of silver is broken, and this or that happens. By the cord of silver he shows this bright life, and the breath that is given us from heaven. The running back of the golden fillet signifies the soul, which runs back thither whence it had come down. Further, the two remaining things that follow, the crushing of the pitcher upon the fountain and the breaking of the wheel upon the cistern, are, by metaphor, riddles of death. For as a pitcher that is crushed ceases to draw, and when the wheel by which water is raised from cisterns and wells is broken, rolled up, as the Seventy translators would have it, in its own cord, the use of the water is cut off; so too, when the cord of silver has been broken and the stream of the soul has run back to its fountain, the man will perish. And as follows more plainly: The dust will return into its earth, from which it was taken, and the spirit will be turned to God, who gave it. From this those are quite laughable who think that souls are sown with bodies and are begotten not by God but by the parent of the bodies. For since the flesh returns into the earth, and the spirit goes back to God, who gave it, it is manifest that God is the parent of souls, not men. After the description of human death, beautifully repeating the opening of his book, he says: Vanity of vanities, said Ecclesiastes, all is vanity. For since all the labor of mortals, which has been discussed in the whole volume, comes to this, that the dust returns into its earth and the soul goes back thither whence it was taken, it is a matter of great vanity to labor in this world and to gather what will profit nothing.

12:9-10

And further, because Ecclesiastes became wise, he still taught the people knowledge, and made them hear; and searching he composed proverbs. Ecclesiastes sought much, to find words of will and to write rightly words of truth. The wisdom by which Solomon surpassed the whole race of men he now also professes at the end of his little work: that he was not content with the instruction of the old law, but of his own accord plunged himself into deep questions, and, to teach the people, composed proverbs and parables that have one thing in the marrow and promise another on the surface. For that proverbs do not sound what is written we are taught in the Gospels too, since the Lord spoke to the people in parables and proverbs, but privately solved them for the apostles. From this it is manifest that the book of Proverbs too does not, as the simple suppose, contain plain precepts, but, as gold is sought in the earth, the kernel in the nut, the fruit hidden in the bristly husks of chestnuts, so in them the divine sense must be searched out more deeply. Beyond these things he adds also that he wished to know the causes and natures of things, and the disposition and prudence of God; that he wished to know why each thing was made, or how; so that what David hopes he will see after the dissolution of body and soul, on his return to the heavens, saying: I will see the heavens, the works of your fingers, this Solomon now strove to find in the present, so that the human mind, walled in by the enclosure of the body, might comprehend the truth known to God alone.

12:11

The words of the wise are as goads and as nails fixed deep, to those who have assemblies, given from one shepherd. Lest he should seem, after the law of God, to burst forth suddenly as a rash teacher and to claim for himself a teaching which Moses had taken up not so much of his own will as with God first angry and then inspiring him, he says that his words are the words of the wise, which in the likeness of goads correct those who sin and stir the sluggish steps of mortals with a pricking point; and that they are as firm as nails fixed deep and solid, and are put forth not by the authority of one but by the council and consensus of all the teachers. And lest human wisdom be despised, he says that it is granted by one shepherd. That is, although very many teach, yet the one author of the teaching is the Lord. This passage tells against those who think that the God of the old law is one and the God of the Gospel another, because one shepherd has instructed the council of the prudent. And the prudent are the prophets as much as the apostles. At the same time this too is to be noted, that the words of the wise are said to prick, not to stroke, nor to handle wantonness with a soft hand, but to fix the pain and wound of repentance in those who stray and, as we said above, in the slow. If, therefore, someone's speech does not prick, but is a delight to the hearers, that is not the speech of a wise man. For the words of the wise are as goads. And since they call the sinner to conversion and are firm, and are given by the council of the saints and granted by one shepherd, they are also founded on a solid root. With this goad, I think, not yet Paul but still Saul, pierced through on the way of error, heard: It is hard for you to kick against the goad.

12:12

And further than these, my son, beware. Of making many books there is no end; and much meditation is a labor of the flesh. Apart from these words which are given by one shepherd and put forth by the council and consensus of the wise, do nothing, claim nothing for yourself; follow the footsteps of the elders, do not depart from their authority. Otherwise, as you seek many things, an endless number of books will meet you, to draw you into error and make you labor in vain as you read. Or certainly he teaches that brevity is to be pursued and that meanings rather than words must be followed, against the philosophers and the teachers of this world, who try to assert the falsehoods of their doctrines by the variety and multiplication of speech. Divine Scripture, on the contrary, is confined within a short circle, and as much as it is expanded in meanings, so much is it compressed in speech. For the Lord has made a finished and shortened word upon the earth, and his word is near, in our mouth and in our heart. Otherwise: frequent reading and daily meditation is usually a labor of the soul rather than of the flesh. For as whatever is done with hand and body is accomplished by the labor of hand and body, so what pertains to reading is rather a labor of the mind. From this it seems to me that what was said above about the multitude of books should be understood otherwise than most people suppose. It is the custom of the Scriptures, although there are several books, if they do not disagree among themselves and write about the same matter, to call them one volume; since both the Gospel and the law of the Lord, unspotted, converting souls are named in the singular, although there are several Gospels and many commandments of the law. So too the volume sealed in the speech of Isaiah is the whole of divine Scripture, and Ezekiel and John eat one chapter of a book. The Savior also, prophesied by the voices of all the saints of the past, says: In the chapter of the book it is written of me. According to this sense, then, I think it is now commanded that many books should not be made. For whatever you say, if it is referred to him who in the beginning was with God, God the Word, is one volume, and innumerable books are called one law, one Gospel. But if you discourse on diverse and discordant things, and with excessive curiosity draw your mind hither and thither, even in one book there are many books. Hence it is said: From much speaking you will not escape sin. To such books, therefore, there is no end. For every good thing, and truth, is enclosed by a fixed end; but malice and falsehood are without end, and the more they are sought out, the greater the series of them that is born. Study and meditation on this matter is a labor of the flesh. Of the flesh, I say, not of the spirit. For the spirit too has its own labor, according to what the Apostle says: But I labored more than all of them; yet not I, but the grace of God which is with me. And the Savior: I have labored crying out.

12:13-14

The end of the whole discourse is very easy to hear: Fear God and keep his commandments. For this is the whole man, that God will bring every deed into judgment concerning every hidden thing, whether it be good or whether it be evil. The Hebrews say that, since among the other writings of Solomon, which have become obsolete and have not lasted in memory, this book too seemed fit to be blotted out, because it asserted that the creatures of God are vain and thought the whole to be as nothing, and preferred food and drink and passing delights to all things, it earned its authority from this one passage, so that it was placed in the number of the divine volumes: because it compressed its whole discussion and all its catalogue into this, as it were, ἀνακεφαλαιώσει (recapitulation), and said that the end of its discourses is most readily heard and has nothing difficult in it, namely that we should fear God and do his precepts. For man was born for this, that, understanding his Creator, he should venerate him with fear and honor and the work of the commandments. For when the time of judgment has come, whatever has been done by us will stand before the judge and long await an uncertain sentence, and each one will receive according to his work, whether he has done any evil or any good. In place of what we have set down: Concerning every hidden thing, whether it be good or whether it be evil, Symmachus and the Seventy translated, concerning every contempt, or at least concerning every thing unknown, because we shall render account in the day of judgment even for an idle word, uttered not by will but through ignorance. Otherwise: since fear belongs to servants, and perfect love casts out fear, and in divine Scripture a twofold fear is spoken of, of beginners as much as of the perfect, I think it is now said of the fear perfected in virtues, according to that: Nothing is lacking to those who fear him. Or at least, because he is still a man and has not yet received the name of God, he has this account of his substance, that, placed in a body, he should fear God. For God brings every deed, that is, all men, into judgment over all the things which they have thought otherwise than as they were ordained and spoken by him, whether in this direction or in that. For Woe indeed to those who call evil good and good evil.
