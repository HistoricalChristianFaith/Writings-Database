On the End of the World

No public-domain English translation of this work is available. The only complete English translation is a draft by Cameron Rhoades, made at the request of Grant Jeffrey and Thomas Ice and printed in Grant R. Jeffrey, “A Pretrib Rapture Statement in the Early Medieval Church”, in Thomas Ice and Timothy J. Demy (eds.), When the Trumpet Sounds: Today’s Foremost Authorities Speak Out on End-Time Controversy (Eugene, OR: Harvest House, 1995; ISBN 978-1-56507-313-5), pp. 105–126, 454–455, an essay in a multi-author volume; transcriptions of this translation that circulate online are said to be inaccurate. A partial translation, made from Caspari’s Latin text, is in Bernard McGinn, Visions of the End: Apocalyptic Traditions in the Middle Ages (Columbia University Press, 1979), pp. 60–61; the book is available from the publisher (revised edition 1998; ISBN 978-0-231-11257-4). The work (CPL 1144) is a short Latin sermon on the last days, entitled in the oldest manuscripts Scarpsum de dictis Sancti Efrem prope fine mundi et consummatione saeculi et conturbatione gentium (“Extract from the sayings of St Ephrem on the end of the world, the consummation of the age and the confusion of the nations”), and known as De fine mundi; it survives in a few manuscripts from the eighth century onward, one of which ascribes it to Isidore, and has been edited by C. P. Caspari and by D. Verhelst. It is not by Ephrem the Syrian but belongs to the Latin texts under his name (“pseudo-Ephraem Latinus”): Pearse argues that it is an original Latin composition whose author drew on the Greek Ephrem corpus and copied a key sentence from the old Latin translation of the sermon De beatitudine animae. It treats the troubles of its own time, among them Persian wars and the decline of Rome, as signs of the approaching end and the coming of the Antichrist, and its statement that the saints and elect are gathered and taken to the Lord before the tribulation, so that they do not see the confusion that is to overwhelm the world, has made it a subject of controversy in modern debates over a pre-tribulation “rapture”. (Noted by Roger Pearse, 27 October 2018, in a study of the manuscripts, the editions and the sources of the passage; see also his posts of 13 October and 22 October 2018.)
