Letter on his misfortunes

No public-domain English translation of this work is available. A complete English translation of the letter, as Ibn Abī Uṣaybiʿa quotes it in his history of physicians, ʿUyūn al-anbāʾ fī ṭabaqāt al-aṭibbāʾ, is Michael Cooperson’s “Epistle on the Trials and Tribulations Which Befell Ḥunayn ibn Isḥāq”, in Dwight F. Reynolds (ed.), Interpreting the Self: Autobiography in the Arabic Literary Tradition (University of California Press, 2001; ISBN 978-0-520-22667-8), pp. 107–118, with a short introduction; it is one of thirteen autobiographical texts translated in that volume, which is available from the publisher and can also be read in the publisher’s UC Press E-Books Collection. Ḥunayn ibn Isḥāq (d. 873 or 877), a Nestorian Christian of al-Ḥīra, was the leading physician-translator of ʿAbbāsid Baghdad, who put Galen and much other Greek science into Syriac and Arabic. In the letter he complains of the envy of his fellow Christian physicians at court and tells how one of them, Bakhtīshūʿ, tricked him into spitting on an icon of the Virgin and Child in front of the caliph al-Mutawakkil; he was flogged, his property was seized, and he spent six months in prison under repeated torture, until the caliph fell ill, recalled him, and on his recovery heaped wealth and honours on him. He ends by urging the reader never to despair of God’s providence in adversity. Some scholars doubt that Ḥunayn wrote the letter himself, but it is among the earliest Arabic prose works written as autobiography. (Noted by Roger Pearse, 21 June 2011; he quotes much of the translation in his post of 23 June 2011.)
