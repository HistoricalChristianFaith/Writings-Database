Letter of Tiberius to Pilate

No public-domain English translation of this work is available. The complete letter is translated by Bart D. Ehrman and Zlatko Pleše in The Apocryphal Gospels: Texts and Translations (Oxford University Press, 2011; ISBN 978-0-19-973210-4), p. 529, with the Greek text, available from the publisher; the same translation is reprinted in their The Other Gospels: Accounts of Jesus from Outside the New Testament (Oxford University Press, 2014; ISBN 978-0-19-933522-0), pp. 285–288, available from the publisher. Both books are anthologies of apocryphal texts. M. R. James gives only an abridged version, partly in summary, in The Apocryphal New Testament (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1924), pp. 156–157. The Epistola Tiberii ad Pilatum is a short Greek piece of the Pilate cycle, dated on linguistic grounds to the eleventh century at the earliest; the Greek text was edited by James in Texts and Studies 5 (1893), pp. 78–81, from the earlier printings of Birch and Fleck. Unusually for a Greek text it takes the Western view of Pilate as a criminal. Tiberius, told by Mary Magdalene of the healings of Jesus, rebukes Pilate for the unjust sentence and sends the courier Rahab with two thousand soldiers to bring him and the Jewish leaders to Rome. The soldiers kill the Jews, Caiaphas dies on the way at Crete, and Annas is sewn into an ox-hide; Pilate is walled up in a cave so that he cannot see the emperor’s face and so be spared, and is killed by an arrow that the emperor, out hunting, shoots at a deer standing at its mouth. (Noted by Roger Pearse, 3 October 2016.)
