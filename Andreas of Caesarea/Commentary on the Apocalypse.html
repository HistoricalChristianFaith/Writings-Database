Commentary on the Apocalypse

No public-domain English translation of this work is available. A complete English translation by Eugenia Scarvelis Constantinou, the first in any modern language, forms the second part of her doctoral thesis Andrew of Caesarea and the Apocalypse in the Ancient Church of the East: Studies and Translation (Université Laval, 2008), which is free online in the university’s repository, CorpusUL; the thesis PDF formerly at the address Pearse gave is preserved by the Internet Archive’s Wayback Machine. Its licence is not stated. Andrew, archbishop of Caesarea in Cappadocia, wrote the commentary early in the seventh century (Constantinou dates it to 611), dividing Revelation into twenty-four discourses of three chapters each. Drawing together earlier interpretations by the Fathers and by unnamed teachers, he reads the visions both spiritually and as prophecy still to be fulfilled, and does not think the end is at hand despite the troubles of the empire. The work became the standard Greek commentary on the book, shaped the text of Revelation handed down with it, and helped win the book its place in the canon of the Eastern churches. Other English translations: Constantinou’s translation as published in Andrew of Caesarea, Commentary on the Apocalypse (The Fathers of the Church 123; Catholic University of America Press, 2011; ISBN 978-0-8132-0123-8), available from the publisher; William C. Weinrich, in Greek Commentaries on Revelation: Oecumenius and Andrew of Caesarea (Ancient Christian Texts; IVP Academic, 2011; ISBN 978-0-8308-2908-8), available from the publisher.
