Exposition on the Song of Songs

No public-domain English translation of this work is available, and there is no complete English translation; only short excerpts have been published. Three passages, including a long one from the Epilogue (89–93) on the “five cities in the land of Egypt” of Isaiah 19:18, read as the five languages of the Church, are translated in Steven A. McKinion (ed.), Isaiah 1–39 (Ancient Christian Commentary on Scripture, Old Testament 10; IVP Academic, 2004; ISBN 978-0-8308-1480-0), pp. 142–3 and elsewhere in the volume, available from the publisher. Further short passages on Song 2:2, “as the lily among thorns” (3.26, 3.29, 3.30), are translated in Michael Maher, “A Lily among Thorns: Canticles 2:2 in the Latin Exegetical Tradition”, in Carmel McCarthy and John F. Healey (eds), Biblical and Near Eastern Essays: Studies in Honour of Kevin J. Cathcart (JSOT Supplement 375; T&T Clark, 2004; ISBN 978-0-8264-6690-7), pp. 227–239 (Google Books); and excerpts appear among the selections in Richard A. Norris Jr., The Song of Songs: Interpreted by Early Christian and Medieval Commentators (The Church’s Bible; Eerdmans, 2003; ISBN 978-0-8028-2579-7), available from the publisher. Apponius (or Aponius) is an obscure Latin writer known only from this work, in twelve books, which is usually dated to Italy, probably Rome, in the early fifth century, though a seventh-century Irish origin has also been proposed. Following Origen, he reads the Song as the story of Christ and the Church from the creation to the last judgement, passing through Israel under the Law, the Incarnation and Passion, the calling of the Gentiles, persecution and heresy, the conversion of Rome and of the barbarians, and ending with the conversion of the Jews; he also reads it of the union of Christ with the faithful soul. The Latin text is edited by B. de Vregille and L. Neyrand in Corpus Christianorum Series Latina 19 (1986), with a French translation in Sources Chrétiennes 420, 421 and 430; the 1843 Rome edition of Bottino and Martini is online at Archive.org. (Noted by Roger Pearse, 25 June 2015, who quotes the passages translated in the first two of these books.)
