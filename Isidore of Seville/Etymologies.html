Etymologies

No public-domain English translation of this work is available. There are two complete modern translations. The first is by Stephen A. Barney, W. J. Lewis, J. A. Beach and Oliver Berghof, with the collaboration of Muriel Hall, The Etymologies of Isidore of Seville (Cambridge University Press, 2006; ISBN 978-0-521-83749-1; paperback 2010, ISBN 978-0-521-14591-6), in one volume with an introduction and the letters exchanged between Isidore and Braulio of Saragossa; it is available from the publisher. The second is by Priscilla Throop, Isidore of Seville’s Etymologies: Complete English Translation, in two volumes, self-published through Lulu (2005–2006; revised 2013): Volume I (Books I–X; ISBN 978-1-4116-6523-1) and Volume 2 (Books XI–XX, with an index of Latin words; ISBN 978-1-4116-6526-2). The Etymologies (or Origins), which Isidore was still working on when he died in 636 and which his friend Braulio, bishop of Saragossa, divided into twenty books, is an encyclopaedia of the learning of late antiquity that explains each thing it treats through the origin, real or supposed, of its name. It runs from the liberal arts (grammar, rhetoric and dialectic, arithmetic, geometry, music and astronomy) through medicine, law and the reckoning of time, the books and offices of the Church, God, the angels and the heresies, to languages and peoples, the human body, animals, the world and its regions, cities and buildings, stones and metals, agriculture, war and games, ships, clothing, food and household tools. It was one of the most widely copied and consulted books of the Middle Ages, and preserves much from classical sources now lost. (Noted by Roger Pearse, 29 June 2012; see also his post of 15 August 2019, which quotes the Barney translation of Book I, chapter 22, on shorthand signs.)
