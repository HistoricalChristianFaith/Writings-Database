Gospel of Judas

No public-domain English translation of this work is available. The complete Coptic text, with an English translation, is published in The Gospel of Judas, Together with the Letter of Peter to Philip, James, and a Book of Allogenes from Codex Tchacos: Critical Edition, with introductions, translations and notes by Rodolphe Kasser, Gregor Wurst, Marvin Meyer and François Gaudard (National Geographic, Washington, 2007; ISBN 978-1-4262-0191-2), which also reproduces colour photographs of the papyrus pages; no publisher's page for it is still online (see its Open Library record). An earlier popular edition, The Gospel of Judas, ed. Rodolphe Kasser, Marvin Meyer and Gregor Wurst, with commentary by Bart D. Ehrman (National Geographic, 2006; ISBN 978-1-4262-0042-7), gives the English translation alone; it is available from Penguin Random House. A newer critical edition and translation, which uses fragments of the codex that came to light after 2007, is The Gospel of Judas, ed. Johanna Brankaer with an introduction by Bas van Os (Oxford Early Christian Gospel Texts; Oxford University Press, 2019; ISBN 978-0-19-967262-2), available from the publisher. A preliminary transcription and translation of the fragments recovered in Ohio is given by Herbert Krosney, Marvin Meyer and Gregor Wurst, "Preliminary Report on New Fragments of Codex Tchacos", Early Christianity 1 (2010), pp. 282–294 (Mohr Siebeck). The Gospel of Judas is a Gnostic work, probably composed in Greek in the second century (Irenaeus already knew of a book of that name), which survives only in a Coptic translation in Codex Tchacos, a papyrus book, probably of the fourth century, found in Middle Egypt. It presents a series of conversations between Jesus and his disciples in the days before the Passover, in which Judas alone is shown to understand who Jesus is and receives a private revelation about the heavenly world and the creation, and the disciples' worship is condemned. The codex passed through the hands of several dealers before it was published, and parts of it were lost or broken up on the way; Pearse's notes on its history are here.
