Liber Pontificalis

No public-domain English translation of the complete work is available. The lives from Peter to Pelagius II (d. 590) were translated by Louise Ropes Loomis as The Book of the Popes (Liber Pontificalis). I: To the Pontificate of Gregory I (Records of Civilization: Sources and Studies; Columbia University Press, 1916), with an introduction and explanatory notes; it is in the public domain (published 1916) and can be read as a scan at the Internet Archive, but it has not yet been transcribed here. Loomis keeps the narrative in full but leaves out part of the lists of names and figures, especially of ordinations, and sets out the divergent readings of the earlier and later recensions in parallel columns. The standard modern English translation, by Raymond Davis, is in three volumes (Translated Texts for Historians; Liverpool University Press): The Book of Pontiffs (Liber Pontificalis): The Ancient Biographies of the First Ninety Roman Bishops to AD 715 (TTH 6; first published 1989; revised third edition 2010; ISBN 978-1-84631-476-6), The Lives of the Eighth-Century Popes (Liber Pontificalis) (TTH 13; first published 1992; revised edition 2007; ISBN 978-1-84631-154-3), covering the nine lives from 715 to 817, and The Lives of the Ninth-Century Popes (Liber Pontificalis) (TTH 20; 1995; ISBN 978-0-85323-479-1), covering the ten lives from 817 to 891; together they translate the whole of the ancient text, with introductions and notes. The Liber Pontificalis (“Book of the Pontiffs”) is a collection of biographies of the bishops of Rome, first compiled at Rome in the first half of the sixth century and then continued by later writers, life by life, down to the end of the ninth century; it is prefaced by two forged letters in which Jerome asks Pope Damasus for a history of his see and Damasus sends it. Each life gives the pope’s origin and the length of his pontificate, then his decrees, the churches he built or repaired and the gifts of plate, lamps and estates he made to them, his ordinations of bishops, priests and deacons, and the place and date of his burial; the earliest lives are largely legendary, but the later ones were written by contemporaries and are a major source for the history of Rome and the Papacy. The Latin text was edited by Louis Duchesne (1886–1892) and, for the lives to 715, by Theodor Mommsen (Monumenta Germaniae Historica, 1898). (Noted by Roger Pearse, 17 February 2015, who quotes Loomis’s version of the life of Symmachus, and in his post of 17 February 2022, which quotes Davis’s version of the lives of Theodore and Benedict II.)
