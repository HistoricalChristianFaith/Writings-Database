Poems of the Months

Translated from the Latin by Roger Pearse, who published the poems one month at a time on his blog in 2022, in a series opened by An Introduction to the Poems of the Chronography of 354 (21 February 2022); the text is taken from the posts for January, February, March, April, May, June, July, August, September, October, November and December. In the copies of the Chronography of 354 each month of the calendar faces a picture, and two short Latin poems go with it: a four-line poem (tetrastich), written down the side of the picture, which often describes what the picture shows and which also circulated on its own in the manuscripts of the Latin Anthology; and a two-line poem (distich), a hexameter under the picture and a pentameter under the facing calendar page, which A. E. Housman edited as a single poem of twenty-four lines. Neither set appears to have belonged to the original book of 354: the distichs are perhaps of the first century, the tetrastichs are said to be of the fifth. Pearse made use of the Latin text and German translation of J. Divjak and W. Wischmeyer, Das Kalenderhandbuch von 354: Der Chronograph des Filocalus (Vienna: Holzhausen, 2014), which is free to download from OAPEN (vol. 1, vol. 2). Only his English is given here; the Latin text he prints with it, his descriptions of the manuscript illustrations and the images themselves are omitted, and where he struck out earlier wording in his drafts only his final wording is kept. Pearse called some of the renderings drafts, invited corrections, and revised several posts in response to readers. Words in round brackets are the translator’s; the daggers in August mark words he gives as corrupt. The notes are the translator’s comments from the posts, slightly abridged. No licence is stated for the translations.

January

Tetrastich

This month is sacred to Janus; Lo! See on the altars
How the incense glitters, how the Lares accept the pious incense.
It is the start of years and time, the birthday of the offices
Which the nobles enumerate in their purpled calendars.

Distich

The first month is sacred to you, Janus, like everything;1
From both sides it is possible for him always to see everything.

February

Tetrastich

And he whom the cerulean cloak wraps (ties up) with a knot,
And who delights to chase the marsh-dwelling fowl,
He whom the skilful Iris/Rainbow pelts with a rain shower;
This month by the Romulean ritual has the feast of purification.

Distich

The second is of the ghosts, in which month it is believed,
That, after sacrifice has been made, earth is accessible to wandering spirits

March

Tetrastich

Know the month clothed with the wolf’s pelt;
Its name is Mars, and Mars gave us the skins.
The springtime brings the unruly kid, and the chattering swallow,
And the pail of milk, and the greening grass.

Distich

Rome was founded under the great name of Mars
There is no mistake. Romulus will be the founder.

April

Tetrastich

April worships a Venus robed with myrtle,
He has the light of spring, in which nurturing Thetis blooms,
And the waxen candle on the right diffuses the scents of flame;
Nor is balsam wanting, of which the Paphian (Venus) is redolent.

Distich

This is the month of Caesar’s Venus, in which the fields are green,
resplendent with flowers, in which every wood resounds with birdsong.

May

Tetrastich

All the treasures of spring, and the roses coloured like gems,
Behold! May has them, wrapped in linen in a basket.
The month is named after Maia, the daughter of Atlas,
Which Urania rightly loves most.

Distich

Blessed May in now follows these (months) with all its strength,
Which (it is said) Jove has assigned to Mercury, son of Maia.2

June

Tetrastich

June unclad then views the sundial’s time,
And shows that the sun is changing its course.
The Lamp-festival marks Ceres’ ripe ears of corn,3
And the scattered lily-petals show the fading of the flower.

Distich

June itself gives you the reason for its name,
Extolling having brought forth abundant fruitfulness.

July

Tetrastich

Look! July shows off his tanned limbs,
Whose reddish hair a garland of corn ties.
The glad mulberry, loaded down with fruit, offers blood-red berries,
It is green in the middle star of Cancer.4

Distich

How rightly, Quintilis, you changed your name!
A pious motive assigned you to the honour of Caesar.5

August

Tetrastich

Look for spring waters and transparent cups in glass,6
So that a thirsty man may drink with submerged mouth.
By the immortal name of a reign is the month designated,
In which, they maintain, that Hecate was born from Latona.

Distich

You also, venerable Sextilis, in every year,
(Under) the7 name of the divinity of Augustus †in the year you come†.

September

Tetrastich

The swelling berries and the different coloured grapes,
September cuts them down; beneath him lie the ripe fruit.
Delighted to have tied up the captive lizard with a string;
Which suspended from a hand plays an active game.8

Distich

September, temples girded with ripe grapes;
Concealed, you will be recognised by your number.

October

Tetrastich

The captured hare and with the vine itself the fruits,
October gives; it gives the fat birds of the country.
Now Bromios appears, to fill with foam the basins, and the fermenting juice make sounds;9
See, the cask is warming up from the new wine!

Distich

In October the harvester happily carries the grapes,
Every field celebrates in sound the gifts of Bacchus.

November

Tetrastich

After this, limbs clad in a linen cloak,
He worships the sacred things and goddess of ancient Memphis:
The greedy goose is hardly kept in check by him with a sistrum,
And, just enough, the ‘dweller’ devoted to the gods, O Memphis.10

Distich

Gone are the leaves, and its frosts reclaim the month,
When Sagittarius shakes off the lofty Plough.11

December

Tetrastich

Mixing the furrowed earth with the annual seed-sowing,
Winter lets them grow; all things are soaked by the rain from Jove.
Now let December bring back the golden feast days of Saturn;
Now, slave, you are permitted to joke with your master.

Distich

I leave the subjects of the month to you, O December;
You can end the year however you like.12

1 The first line is different in two of the manuscripts, R1 and R2. Instead the first line reads: Ianus adest bifrons primusque ingreditur annum… “Two-faced Janus is here, and first begins the year…” It seems to be taken for granted in the literature that the illustration and the hexameter in R1 and R2 are not genuine; but renaissance compositions.
2 Housman noted that the second line was clearly corrupt and suggested that Mercurio is a gloss. To me the obvious accusative and infinitive Jovem … tribuisse indicate reported speech, and therefore that the missing text must have a sense something like “it is said”. Divjak and Wischmeyer thought the same in their German version.
3 “Phoebus” here means the sun. The reference to “Lampas”, a festival with torches on the solstice is also attested in the ps.Chrysostom, De solstitiis et aequinoctibus, the “dies lampadarum” or “day of torches”, or, more briefly, “lampas”, “the torch”.
4 I.e. in the heat of summer.
5 I can’t work out the syntax for the second line: honori is dative, of course, not nominative. The sense is that the motive for the change of name is to honour Caesar.
6 “vitro” is ablative singular, so I am not sure how that fits with the rest of the first line. The reign mentioned in line 3 is that of Augustus. On line 4, the Roman goddess Diana in one of her aspects took on the role of the Greek Hecate as goddess of the underworld. Her birthday was celebrated at Nemi on August 13th.
7 The last words are those in the manuscripts, but Divjak and Wischmeyer suggest that they are corrupt; apparently the editions give various suggested emendations. I don’t see how the nominative “nomen” should be understood – “under” is a guess.
8 September 5 is Vindemia, the start of the grape harvest. The lizard can be a pest (Pliny, Natural History 30, 89), but on a thread in a container it produces medicine (NH 30, 52).
9 “Bromios” is Bacchus. The heat of fermentation is referred to in line 4.
10 The “dweller” is perhaps the sacred snake in the temple, depicted in the image.
11 I’m not sure of the sense of the second line here.
12 The second line is, apparently hopelessly corrupt and is variously emended. Housman suggested instead: Argumenta tibi mensis concedo Decemb<ris>, / Qui squamis annum claudere piscis <amas>.
