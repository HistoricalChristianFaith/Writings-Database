Carmen contra Paganos

No public-domain English translation of this work is available. The first English translation, by Brian Croke and Jill Harries, is printed complete, with notes, as “Anonymous, Carmen contra paganos (Poem against the Pagans)” in their anthology of sources Religious Conflict in Fourth-Century Rome: A Documentary Study (Sources in Ancient History; Sydney: Sydney University Press, 1982; ISBN 978-0-424-00091-6); see the National Library of Australia catalogue record. A prose translation, with the Latin text, is also given in an appendix to Alan Cameron, The Last Pagans of Rome (Oxford University Press, 2011; ISBN 978-0-19-974727-6), available from the publisher. The Carmen contra paganos (“Poem against the Pagans”, also called Carmen adversus paganos or, after Mommsen, Carmen adversus Flavianum) is an anonymous Latin poem of 122 hexameters, preserved only in a single Paris manuscript of Prudentius (Paris, lat. 8084). It attacks a pagan prefect of Rome, lately dead, who had led a short-lived revival of the old rites in the city: the poet ridicules the scandals of the Roman gods and gives a sharp picture of the cults of the Great Mother, Serapis and Mithras, including the bull’s-blood initiation of the taurobolium. The prefect is usually identified with Virius Nicomachus Flavianus, who died in 394 after the defeat of the pagan party at the Frigidus, as Croke and Harries take it; Cameron argues instead for Vettius Agorius Praetextatus (d. 384), with Pope Damasus as the author.
