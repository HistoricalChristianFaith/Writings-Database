Death as a Good

Latin source: Ambrose's book on death as a good, a sequel to On Isaac and the Soul, probably worked up from two sermons preached at Milan (about 387-391), from the critical text of C. Schenkl, Corpus Scriptorum Ecclesiasticorum Latinorum 32/1 (Vienna, 1897), pp. 703–753, verified against the page images and collated with the Benedictine edition as reprinted in Migne, PL 14, with the edition's misprints corrected (among them the words of Matthew 8:22 that his print repeats in Chapter IX §39); in two places the text departs from Schenkl with the other manuscripts and the Benedictine edition (Chapter I §2, priora without his supplement contraria; Chapter VII §28, adiectio for his abiectio). Scripture is translated as Ambrose quotes it, from his own Old Latin text; italics mark the Scripture he quotes, and the half-line of a poet that Paul quotes in Acts 17:28 (Chapter IX §38). The section numbers are the Maurist ones shared by all editions. The two Greek words Ambrose cites (Chapter VIII §34; Chapter X §45) are kept as printed, with a transliteration in parentheses. Schenkl's supplements are read into the text, the word he brackets as spurious is omitted, his three lacunae (Chapter VIII §33, Chapter IX §39, Chapter X §47) are marked with dots, and his critical apparatus is not translated. This English translation is released into the public domain.

Chapter I.

1. Since in the previous book we have woven together some discourse on the soul, we think the way is easier for composing something on death as a good. If death harms the soul, it can seem an evil; but if it does the soul no hurt at all, it is not even an evil. And what is not evil is also good, since what is faulty is evil, but what is free of fault is good; and therefore good things are contrary to evil things, and evil things to good. Then, innocence is where there is no will to harm, and he is called harmful who is not harmless, merciful who forgives, unmerciful who knows not how to pardon and be moved.

2. But perhaps someone may assert: 'What is so contrary as life to death? If, then, life is thought a good, how is death not an evil?' And so let us consider what life is, and also what death is. Life is to enjoy the gift of breathing; death, to be deprived of it. Now this gift of breathing is counted by most people among good things. This, then, is life: to enjoy good things; death, on the contrary, is to be stripped of good things. And Scripture says: Behold, I have set before your face life and death, good and evil, calling life a good and death an evil, or else pairing the first-named with each other and the latter with each other. Then, to use an example from the divine reading, man was placed in paradise to eat of the tree of life and of the other trees of paradise, but not to eat of the tree in which was the knowledge of good and evil; and on the day he ate, he would die the death. He did not keep the commandment, and he lost the fruit, and, cast out of paradise, he tasted death. Death, then, is an evil, which is inflicted as the price of condemnation.

Chapter II.

3. But there are three kinds of death. One is the death of sin, of which it is written: the soul that sins, it shall die. Another is mystical death, when someone dies to sin and lives to God, of which the Apostle likewise says: for we were buried together with him through baptism into death. The third is the death by which we complete the course and the task of this life, that is, the parting of soul and body. We observe, then, that one death is evil, if we die because of sins; another death is good, by which he who has died is justified from sin; the third death is in between, for it both seems good to the just and is to be feared by most, and though it releases all, it delights few. But this is not the fault of death but of our weakness, for we are captivated by the pleasure of the body and the delight of this life, and we tremble to complete this course, in which there is more bitterness than pleasure. But not so holy and wise men, who groaned at the long duration of this sojourning, judging it a finer thing to be dissolved and to be with Christ: they even cursed the day of their birth, as one says: let the day perish on which I was born.

4. For what is there in this life to delight us, full as it is of hardships and anxieties, in which there are countless false accusations and many troubles and many tears of those who are afflicted by troubles, and there is none, he says, to comfort them? And therefore Ecclesiastes praises the dead more than the living, and better, he says, than these two is he who has not yet been born, who has not seen this evil. And elsewhere the same Ecclesiastes declared that one whom his mother cast out in a miscarriage is better than a long-lived man, because he has not seen these evils which happen in this world, nor come into this darkness, nor walked in worldly vanity, and therefore he who has not come into this life will have more rest than he who has come. For what good is there for a man in this life, who lives in a shadow and cannot be filled with his desires? And if he is filled with riches, he loses the fruit of repose, because he is forced to guard what he has sought with wretched greed, so that he possesses the more wretchedly riches that will be of no profit to him. For what is more wretched than to be tormented by guarding riches whose abundance profits nothing?

5. Therefore, if life is full of burden, surely its end is a relief. Now relief is a good, and death is the end: death, then, is a good. For in no other way did Simeon also rejoice, who had received the answer that he would not see death until he had first seen the Lord's Christ. And when his parents brought him into the temple, he took him up in his hands and said: now you dismiss your servant in peace, as if he were held in this life by a kind of necessity, not by his will. So he asks to be dismissed, as though he were hastening from certain bonds to freedom. For there are, as it were, certain bonds of this body and, what is heavier, bonds of temptations, which bind us and tie us down by a kind of law of sin to the injury of captivity. Then, in the very departure, we see how the soul of one who is dying looses itself little by little from the bonds of the flesh and, sent forth from the mouth, flies away, as though stripped of the prison-hovel of this body. Then holy David too was hastening to go out from this place of sojourning, saying: I am a stranger with you on the earth, and a sojourner, like all my fathers. And therefore, as a sojourner, he was hastening to that homeland common to all the saints, asking that his sins be forgiven him for the defilement of this stay before he departed from life. For he who has not received forgiveness of sins here will not be there; for he will not be who has not been able to come to eternal life, because eternal life is the forgiveness of sins. And therefore he says: forgive me, that I may be refreshed before I go, and I shall be no more.

6. Why, then, do we so greatly desire this life, in which the longer one has been, the heavier the load he is burdened with? The Lord himself says: sufficient for the day is its own evil, and Jacob said: the days of the years of my life which I have are a hundred and thirty, few and evil, not because the days are evil, but because with the coming on of days the increases of evil are heaped up for us;

7. for no day passes without sin of ours. Hence the Apostle excellently says: for to me to live is Christ and to die is gain, referring the one to the necessity of life, the other to the advantage of death; for to us to live is Christ, whom we serve, to whom obedience must be rendered by his saints in the preaching of the gospel. Then Simeon too, who said: now you dismiss your servant, was waiting because of Christ. For Christ is our king: therefore we cannot abandon and despise what the king commands. How many does the emperor of this earth command to stay in foreign places, either for the sake of honour or on account of some office! Do these depart without consulting the emperor? And how much greater a thing it is to please divine things than human ones! For the saint, then, to live is Christ and to die is gain. For as a servant he does not flee the obedience of life, and as a wise man he embraces the gain of death; for it is gain to have escaped the increases of sin, gain to have fled worse things, to have passed over to better. And he added: to be dissolved and to be with Christ; for it is much better; but to remain in the flesh is more necessary for your sake. The one is better, the other necessary: necessary for the sake of the fruits of work, better for the sake of the grace and the union of Christ.

Chapter III.

8. Since, then, the Apostle has taught that he who has escaped from this body will be with Christ, provided that he has deserved it, let us consider what death is, and also what life is. And so, with Scripture teaching us, we have learned that death is the release of soul and body and a kind of separation of man. For we are loosed from this tie of soul and body when we depart. Hence David too says: you have burst my bonds; to you I will sacrifice an offering of praise. That the bonds of this life are meant, that is, of our conjunction, which consists of soul and body, the earlier little verse of his psalm teaches: precious in the sight of the Lord is the death of his saints. And therefore, as a prophet foreseeing that he would be with the saints and with those who for devotion laid down their souls in Christ, he rejoices, because he too offered himself faithfully for the people of God, when he fought against Goliath in single combat and alone repelled the common danger and reproach, or when he readily exposed himself to death to appease the Lord's displeasure, or when he offered himself, ready, to the divine vengeance for the safety of the suffering people. For he knew that it was more glorious to die for Christ than to reign in this world; for what is more excellent than to become Christ's offering? And so, although we frequently read that sacrifices were offered by him to the Lord, yet in this place he added: to you I will sacrifice an offering of praise. Not 'I sacrifice', he says, but 'I will sacrifice', signifying that the sacrifice is perfect when each one, released from the bonds of this body, stands before the Lord and offers himself as an offering of praise, because before death there is no perfect praise, nor can anyone in this life be proclaimed with definite commendation, since what comes after for him is uncertain. Death, then, is the loosing of soul and body. Then too we have shown that it is read in the Apostle: to be dissolved and to be with Christ is much better. And what does this loosing do but that the body is dissolved and rests, while the soul turns to its rest and is free, and, if it is godly, will be with Christ?

9. What else, then, do the just do in this life but strip themselves of the contaminations of this body, which bind us like bonds, and strive to separate themselves from these troubles, renounce pleasures and luxury, and flee the flames of lusts? Does not each one, then, placed in this life, imitate the likeness of death, if he is able so to conduct himself that all the delights of the body die to him and he himself also dies to all desires and to the allurements of the world, as Paul had died, saying: to me for the world is crucified, and I to the world? Then, that we may know that there is a death in this life, and a good death, he exhorts us to carry about the death of Jesus in our body; for he who has the death of Jesus in himself will also have the life of the Lord Jesus in his body. Let death, then, work in us, that life may also work, a good life after death, that is, a good life after victory, a good life when the contest is finished, so that the law of the flesh may no longer know how to fight against the law of the mind, so that we may no longer have any strife with the body of death, but there may be victory in the body of death. And I myself do not know whether this death is not of greater virtue than life. Certainly I am moved by the authority of the Apostle, who says: so death works in us, but life in you. How many peoples' life did the death of one build up! And so he teaches that this death too is to be sought by those placed in this life, that the death of Christ may shine out in our body, and that blessed one by which our outer man is corrupted so that our inner man may be renewed, and our earthly house is dissolved so that a heavenly dwelling may be opened to us. He, then, imitates death who withdraws himself from the communion of this flesh and looses himself from those bonds of which the Lord says to you through Isaiah: but loose every binding of injustice, dissolve the obligations of violent exchanges, let the broken go free in remission, and burst asunder every unjust fraud.

10. He imitates it who strips himself of pleasures and raises and lifts himself up from earthly delights and places himself in that heavenly dwelling in which Paul, while he was still alive, used to live. Otherwise he would not say: but our way of life is in heaven, which can be referred equally to the confident expectation of merit and to meditation. For there was his meditation, there the way of life of his soul, there his understanding, which certainly was not wont to cling within the narrow confines of this flesh. For the wise man, when he seeks that divine thing, releases his soul from the body and sends away its close company, when he pursues that knowledge of the truth which he desires to be shown to him as it were naked and open, and therefore he seeks to strip himself of the nets, as it were, and the mists of this body. For we cannot grasp that supernal truth with these hands or eyes and ears, since the things that are seen are temporal, but the things that are not seen are eternal. Then, we are often deceived by sight and see most things otherwise than they are; we are deceived also by hearing; and therefore let us contemplate not the things that are seen but those that are not seen, if we do not wish to be deceived. When, then, is our soul not deceived, when does it attain the throne of truth, except when it separates itself from this body and is not deceived and mocked by it? For it is mocked by the sight of the eyes, it is mocked by the hearing of the ears; and therefore let it leave the body and forsake it. Hence the Apostle too cries out: do not touch, do not defile, do not taste, things which all tend to corruption; for the things that serve the indulgence of the body tend to corruption. And therefore, showing that he had found what is true not through indulgence of the body but through the elevation of the soul and humility of heart, he added: but our way of life is in heaven. There, then, let a man seek what is true, what is and abides, and let him gather himself into himself and assemble the whole keen edge of his strength, and not entrust and commit himself to others, but know and understand himself, and recognize that what seems to him to be true is to be followed; what he judges is to be chosen by fleshly delight, let him know to be false, let him flee and withdraw from it, because it is full of deceit.

11. Rightly, then, did he depreciate and dishonour this body, calling it the body of death. For who has been able to see the splendour of the virtues with the eyes, who to grasp justice with the hands, who to behold wisdom with the gaze of the eyes? Then, when we are thinking about something, we want no one to come in our way, no one to make a noise in our ears, and we so concentrate with the mind that very often we do not see what is before us. Indeed, at night we think more purely, and then we meditate better in the heart on the things that move us. Hence the prophet too says: what you say in your hearts, be pierced with sorrow for it on your beds. Often, too, some close their eyes if they wish to bring out something by the effort of deep thought, avoiding the hindrances of the eyes. Often, too, we seek out solitude, so that no one's talk may whisper into our ears and, like some byway, lead the soul that is intent on its thought away from the truth and turn it aside from its purpose.

12. The necessity of this body, then, begets many occupations for us, and habit brings them in, by which the vigour of the soul is hindered and its attention called away. Hence holy Job beautifully says: remember that you fashioned me of clay. If, then, the body is clay, it certainly smears us, it does not wash us clean, and it defiles the soul with the defilement of intemperance. With skin and flesh, he says, you clothed me, with bones and sinews you wove me together. And so our soul is bound and stretched by the sinews of this body, and therefore at times it grows stiff, often it is bent. And he added: you did not make me innocent of iniquity. For if I am ungodly, woe to me! But if I am just, I shall not be able to recline; for I am full of confusion. You have brought temptations upon me. For what is this life but full of snares? We walk among snares, we move among very many temptations. Then the same man says above: is not the life of man on earth a temptation? He did well to add on earth, because there is a life of man in heaven too. Like a hireling's wage, he says, is his life, in toil, that is, and in warmth, lighter than tales, wavering and swimming in words, his habitation in houses of clay and his very life in clay. No firmness of judgement, no constancy. In the day night is longed for, in the night day is sought; before food, groaning; at meals, weeping, tears, pains, fears, anxieties; no rest from disturbances, no reclining from toils. The movement of anger and indignation is rather fierce. Many desire death and do not obtain it; but if they obtain it, they rejoice, because death alone is rest for a man.

Chapter IV.

13. But someone will say that it is written that God did not make death. Life was in paradise, where the tree of life was, and the life was the light of men. Death, then, is evil, since it befell us and crept in. But how is death evil if either, according to the pagans, it lacks sensation, or, according to the Apostle, the gain is Christ, with whom it is much better to be? How, then, is death an evil for us, if there is no sensation after death? For where there is no sensation, there is certainly no pain of injury, because pain is a sensation. Or, since there is sensation after death, there is certainly life after death, and the soul survives death, since it uses sensation and enjoys life. But since life and the soul remain after death, the good remains and is not lost by death, but increased; nor is the soul held back by any hindrance of death, but rather it acts the more, because it does what is its own without any fellowship of the body, which is more a burden than a use to the soul. What evil, then, is there for the soul that has guarded its purity and kept the discipline of the virtues? Or if it has not kept it, death is not an evil, but life is, because it was not life: for what kind of life is that which is overgrown with vices and sins? Why, then, do we accuse death, which either pays the price of life or abolishes its pain and torment? And so death either enjoys the good of its own repose or labours under an evil not its own.

14. Now look at this: if life is a burden, death is a release; if life is a punishment, death is a remedy; or if there is a judgement after death, there is also life after death. This life, then, is not a good; or, if life here is good, how is death there not good, since there no fear of a terrible judgement remains? But if this very life here is good, by what things is it good? Certainly by virtue and good conduct. It is good, then, not according to the union of soul and body, but because through virtue it drives off what is its evil and obtains what is the good of death, so that it accomplishes what belongs to the soul rather than what belongs to the close company and conjunction. But if life is good, which is the mirror of a soul separating itself from the body, and if the soul is good, which raises itself up and withdraws from the close company of the body, then death too is certainly a good, which releases and frees the soul from fellowship with this flesh.

15. In every way, then, death is a good: both because it divides things that fight against each other, so that they may not assail one another; and because it is a kind of harbour for those who, tossed on the great sea of this life, seek the anchorage of a sure repose; and because it does not make one's state worse, but such as it has found it in each, such it keeps it for the judgement to come, and cherishes it with repose itself, and withdraws it from the envy of present things, and settles it in the expectation of things to come. Add to this that men fear death in vain, as if it were the end of nature. For if we recall that God did not make death, but that after man had fallen into the shameful deed of transgression and deceit, the sentence laid hold of him that earth should return to its earth, we shall find that death is the end of sin, lest, the longer life was, the more numerous guilt should become. The Lord, then, allowed death to creep in, so that guilt might cease; but lest, again, the end of nature should lie in death, the resurrection of the dead was given, so that through death guilt might fail, but through the resurrection nature might be made everlasting. And therefore this death is the passage of all. You must pass over steadfastly: and it is a passage from corruption to incorruption, from mortality to immortality, from disturbances to tranquillity. Do not, then, let the name of death offend you, but let the benefits of a good passage delight you. For what is death but the burial of vices and the raising up of virtues? Hence he too says: may my soul die among the souls of the just, that is, may it be buried together with them, that it may lay aside its vices and take on the grace of the just, who carry about the mortification of Christ in their body and soul. And the mortification of Christ is the forgiveness of sins, the abolition of crimes, the forgetting of error, the taking up of graces. What can we say more fully about the good of death than that it is death which redeemed the world?

Chapter V.

16. But let us speak of the death common to all. Why should we fear it, when it is not wont to harm the soul? For it is written: do not fear those who kill the body, but cannot kill the soul. Through this death the soul is set free, while it is separated from the close company of the body and stripped of the wrappings of disturbance. Hence let us too, while we are in the body, imitating the practice of death, lift up our soul from the couch of this flesh and rise, as it were, from this tomb. Let us withdraw ourselves from the tie of the body, let us leave all things whatever that are earthly, so that when the adversary comes he may find nothing of his own in us. Let us strive toward that eternal thing, let us fly up to that divine thing on the wings of love and with the oars of charity. Let us rise from here, that is, from worldly and earthly things. For the Lord said: rise, let us go from here, commanding that each one should rise from the earth, lift up his soul lying on the ground, raise it to the things above, rouse his eagle, that eagle of which it was said: your youth shall be renewed like the eagle's. This was said to the soul. Let our soul, then, like an eagle, seek the heights, fly above the clouds, shine with its plumage renewed, carry its flights into heaven, where it cannot fall into snares. For a bird that comes down from on high, or that cannot raise itself on high, is often either caught in snares or deceived by birdlime or entangled in some ambush or other. So too let our soul beware of coming down to these worldly things. There is a snare in gold, birdlime in silver, a tie in landed property, a nail in love. While we seek gold, we are strangled; while we seek silver, we stick in its birdlime; while we seize on landed property, we are bound. Why do we seek empty gain at the loss of a precious soul? The whole world is little to you against the loss of one soul; for what does it profit a man if he gain this world, but suffer the loss of his own soul? Or what exchange will you give for your soul? It is not redeemed with gold, not with silver; rather, it is lost by gold. Indeed, even a woman's beauty, when it is pursued, binds tight. Lust is a nail, sadness is a nail, anger is a nail, all the passions are nails, which pierce our soul as with a kind of spit and fix themselves in the body and fasten it to its inward parts.

17. Let us flee, then, these evils and lift up our soul to that image and likeness of God. Flight from evils is likeness to God, and the image of God is acquired by virtues. Therefore he who painted us painted us, as our maker, with the colours of the virtues: behold, I, he says, O Jerusalem, have painted your walls. Let us not wipe away with the brush of negligence the painted wall-defences of our soul. Therefore he said I have painted walls, by which we may turn back the enemy.

18. The soul has its walls, from which it looks out and of which it says: I am a fortified city, I am a besieged city. With these walls it is fortified; by these, when besieged, it is defended. And truly the soul is a wall, which stands out in front of the camp. Hence it too says in the Song of Songs: I am a wall, and my breasts are like a tower. A good wall, which the Lord has painted, as he himself says: on my hands I have painted your walls, and you are always in my sight. A good soul, which has God as its watchman and is in his hands, like the prophetic soul, which is commended into the hands of the Lord as a spirit, and which is in the sight of God; for the eyes of the Lord are upon the just, as it says itself: I was in his eyes as one who finds peace. She has good towers who has both the word concerning intelligible things and the discipline concerning moral things. And therefore this soul, having the grace of her breasts, enters into the gardens, and finding there the Bridegroom sitting and discoursing with his friends, she says: you who sit in the gardens, make your voice heard to me. To me, she says, not to the friends. Flee, my brother: she urges the Bridegroom to flee, because now she herself too can follow him as he flees earthly things. She tells him to be like the little roe that escapes from the nets; for she too wishes to flee and fly away above the world.

19. From here Plato composed that garden of his. He called it in one place the garden of Jupiter, in another the garden of the mind; for he called Jupiter both god and the mind of the universe. Into this garden, he says, the soul, which he names Venus, entered, to fill herself with the abundance and riches of the garden, in which Porus lay filled with drink, pouring out nectar. This, then, he composed from the book of the Song of Songs, on the ground that the soul cleaving to God entered into the garden of the mind, in which there was an abundance of various virtues and flowers of words. And who does not know that he thought the abundance of virtues should be transferred from that paradise which we read of in Genesis, having the tree of life and the tree of the knowledge of good and evil and the other trees, and planted in the garden of the mind, which Solomon in the Song of Songs signified as the garden of the soul, or as the soul itself? For thus it is written: a garden enclosed is my sister, my bride, a garden enclosed, a fountain sealed; your sendings forth are a paradise. And below the soul says: arise, north wind, and come, south wind, blow through my garden, let my ointments flow down. Let my brother come down into his garden. How much more beautiful is this, that the soul adorned with the flowers of the virtues is a garden, or has in itself a paradise budding forth! Into this garden it invites the Word of God to come down, so that that soul, watered by the heavenly rain of the Word and by his abundance, may bear fruit. And the Word of God feeds on the virtues of the soul whenever he finds it obedient to him and rich, and he plucks its fruits and delights in them. And when the Word of God has come down into it, the ointments of healthful words flow down from it, and the fragrant breaths of manifold graces breathe out fragrance far and wide.

20. Hence the Bridegroom says — and the Bridegroom of the soul is God the Word, to whom the soul is united by a kind of lawful covenant of marriage —: I have entered into my garden, my sister, my bride, I have gathered my myrrh with my ointments, I have eaten my bread with my honey, I have drunk my wine with my milk. Eat, my neighbours, and drink, and be inebriated, my brothers. I sleep, and my heart keeps watch. Let us learn what fruits and foods God feasts on, or in what he delights. He delights in this, if someone puts his sin to death, blots out his guilt, buries and abolishes his iniquities. For myrrh is the burial of the dead, and sins are dead, since they can have no sweetness of life. And certain wounds of sins are bathed with the ointments of the divine discourse, and are healed by the stronger food of the Word as by bread and by the sweeter discourse as by honey. That words are foods Solomon teaches elsewhere too, saying: good words are honeycombs. In that garden, then, there are good words: one to restrain guilt, another to rebuke iniquity, another to make arrogance die and, as it were, bury it, when someone who has been rebuked renounces his errors. There is also a stronger word, which strengthens the heart of man with the more solid nourishment of heavenly Scripture. There is also a persuasive word, sweet as honey and yet pricking the conscience of the sinner in its very sweetness. There is also a word of more fervent spirit, which inebriates like wine and gladdens the heart of man; there is also a milky word, pure and white. The Bridegroom tells his neighbours to feast on these foods of sweet and profitable words; and his neighbours are those who follow him and are present at his wedding. Filled with this food and drink — for each one drinks water from his own vessels and from the springs of his own wells — and inebriated, the soul was asleep to the world, awake to God; and therefore, as what follows teaches, God the Word asked that her door be opened to him, that he might fill her by his entering.

21. From here, then, those Platonic banqueters were transferred, from here that nectar from the prophetic wine and honey, from here that sleep, from here that everlasting life on which he said his gods feast, because Christ is life. And therefore the womb of her soul was filled with the seeds of such words, and she herself went out in the Word. And the soul that goes out from this servitude and raises itself from the body follows the Word.

Chapter VI.

22. But there are principalities of the air and powers of the world, which seek either to cast us down from the wall of the soul, or to hinder those who walk aright, or to bring down those who strive toward higher things and to call them back to earthly things. But let us all the more raise our mind to the heights, following the Word of God. Those principalities pour worldly things over us, with which to bend your mind. Then, O soul, direct your steps all the more to Christ. They cast in the desire for gold, for silver, for a neighbouring property, so that for the sake of acquiring it you may excuse yourself from the supper of him who invited you to the wedding of the Word. Beware of excusing yourself; rather, put on the wedding garment and enjoy the banquet of the rich man, lest the rich man who had invited you, when you have excused yourself while you are taken up with worldly things, invite others and you be shut out. The powers of the world also cast in the craving for honour, that you may exalt yourself like Adam and, while you wish to make yourself equal to God by a likeness of power, may despise the divine commands and begin to lose what you had. For he who does not have, even what he has shall be taken away from him.

23. How often in prayer, in which we draw nearest to God, things full of some disgrace or crime are poured over us, by which they may turn us away from our zeal for prayer! How often the enemy tries to insert into our heart something by which he may turn us back from our purpose of holiness and from devout vows! How often he inflames bodily fires, how often he makes the eyes of harlots meet us, with which to tempt the chaste feeling of the just man, so as to strike him unprepared with the unforeseen dart of love! How often he puts into your mind a wicked word and hidden thoughts of the heart! Of this the Law says to you: Take heed to yourself, lest there be a hidden wicked word in your heart, and lest Jesus say to you: 'Why do you think evil things in your heart?' And lest, when you abound in gold and silver and the rich fruits of your fields or in honours, you say: 'My strength has given me these things', and forget the Lord your God.

24. By these things, then, the soul that longs to fly away is brought down. But you, wrestle against them like a good soldier of Christ Jesus, and, despising lower things and forgetting earthly things, strive toward heavenly and eternal things. Lift up your soul, lest the bait of snares entice it. The pleasures of the world are a kind of bait and, what is worse, bait of evils, bait of temptations. While you seek pleasure, you run into snares. For 'the eye of a harlot is the snare of a lover'. The eye of a harlot, then, is a snare; the word of a harlot is also a snare, which sweetens your throat for a time and afterwards makes it harsh with the bitterness of a sinful conscience. Another's possession, full of pleasantness, is a snare. The whole road of this life is full of snares. Hence the just man says: In this way in which I walked they have hidden snares for me. In this way he says they have hidden. Therefore walk in that way which says: I am the way and the truth and the life, so that you may say: He has turned back my soul; he has led me upon the paths of justice for his name's sake.

25. Let this world, then, die to us; let the wisdom of this flesh, which is hostile to God, die to us. Let us subject our soul to Christ alone, so that each one may say: Is not my soul subject to God? Not subject to the world, not subject, he says, to the world. The moneyed man cannot say this, the greedy man cannot say it; but the just and self-controlled man says it. The greedy man, however, says: Soul, you have many good things laid up for many years; rest, eat, drink and feast, because the soul of the greedy man is subject to bodily luxury, whereas the soul of the just man uses the body as an instrument or organ. Like an excellent artist, the soul leads the service of the body wherever it will, and fashions from it the form it has chosen, and makes whatever virtues it wishes resound in it, playing now the melodies of chastity, now the melodies of temperance, the song of sobriety, the sweetness of integrity, the delight of virginity, the gravity of widowhood. Sometimes, however, the player suffers along with his instrument: and therefore play honourable strains, so that the suffering-along may be honourable. For he who sees is often affected by seeing, and he who hears by hearing. And therefore Scripture says: Let your eyes see right things, and below: Do not be much with a strange woman. Do not fix your gaze on the eyes of a young girl, do not fix your attention on the words of a harlot.

Chapter VII.

26. And why do I speak of snares from outside? It is our own snares that we must beware of. In this very body of ours snares are spread around us, which we ought to avoid. Let us not entrust ourselves to this body, let us not mingle our soul with it. With a friend he says mingle your mind, not with an enemy. Your body is an enemy to you, for it fights against your mind, and its works are enmities, dissensions, quarrels and disturbances. Do not mingle your soul with it, lest you confound the two. For if it is mingled, then the flesh, which is lower, comes off better than the soul, which is higher, because the soul hands on life to the body, but the flesh pours death over into the soul. So the working of both is confounded; almost the very substance of both is confounded. Thus the soul takes upon itself the insensibility of a dead body, and the body discharges all the virtues of the soul. And lest perhaps, because the soul is poured into the body, it should be thought to be also confounded with it, let the grace of this light be an example to us, since light too is poured into an earthly place and yet is not confounded with it. Let there not, then, be a confounded working of things whose substance is unlike; rather, let the soul be in the body so as to give life to the body, to govern it and to enlighten it.

27. Yet we cannot deny that it suffers along with its body. For it is also saddened, since Jesus says: My soul is sorrowful even unto death, expressing in himself the feeling of a man, and elsewhere: My soul is troubled. For as a player suffers along with his melodies, whether of the flute or the lyre or the organ, in voice, gesture and feeling, sadder in sad sounds, gladder in glad ones, more roused in the sharper ones, milder and gentler in the milder ones themselves, so that he himself commends the sounds of the songs and in a manner sets the feelings to measure, so also the soul that is sober, in this body as on musical strings, yet strikes the passions of this flesh with its fingertips, so to speak, like the sounds of strings, so as to render a consonant and harmonious concord of morals and virtues, so that in all its thoughts, in all its works it keeps this one thing: that all its counsels and deeds are in tune with one another. It is the soul, then, that uses, and the body that is for use; and thus the one is in command, the other in service; the one is what we are, the other what is ours. If someone loves the beauty of the soul, he loves us; if someone loves the comeliness of the body, he does not love the man himself but the beauty of the flesh, which nevertheless quickly withers and fades away.

28. Therefore turn your attention to him of whom the prophet says: who has not received his soul in vain. He has received his soul in vain (to speak now of the troubles of this life) who builds up worldly things and constructs bodily things. Every day we rise to eat and drink, and no one is filled so as not to hunger and thirst a moment later. Every day we seek gain, and no limit is set to greed. The eye he says shall not be satisfied with seeing, nor the ear with hearing. He who loves silver shall not be satisfied with silver. There is no end of labour and there is no fruit of abundance. Every day we long to know new things, and what is knowledge itself but an addition of daily pain? All things that are have already been, and there is nothing new under the sun, but all is vanity. I hated the whole of life said Ecclesiastes. He who hated life certainly proclaimed death. Indeed, he praised the dead more than the living, and judged him blessed who has not come into this life nor taken up this empty labour. My heart went round he says to know the gladness of the ungodly, and to consider and seek wisdom and number, and to know through the ungodly man gladness and trouble and tossing, and I find it more bitter than death: not because death is bitter, but because it is bitter to the ungodly. And yet life is more bitter than death. For it is heavier to live for sin than to die in sin, because the ungodly man, as long as he lives, increases sin; if he dies, he ceases to sin.

29. Many rejoice at the acquittal of their crimes. If they mean to amend, they do rightly; if they mean to persist in them, foolishly, because condemnation would have profited them far more, so that they should not heap up increases of their sins. On this there is the lofty judgement of the Apostle, who says that not only those who do shameful things but also those who approve them are worthy of death; and also that those who condemn in others such things as they themselves do are held inexcusable, as condemned even by their own sentence; for when they condemn others, they condemn themselves. Nor ought they to flatter themselves because for a time they seem exempt from punishment and free of guilt, since they pay heavier penalties within themselves and are guilty before themselves, though they do not seem so to others, and twist against themselves a heavier sentence of conscience when they judge the sins of others. But do not, he says, O man, despise the treasures of the divine goodness and patience; for the goodness of God calls you to repentance, invites you to correction; but your hardness, by which you persist in the obstinacy of error, increases the severity of the judgement to come, so that you receive a fitting retribution for your offences.

30. Death, then, is not an evil. For death is neither among the living nor among the dead: with the one it is not, because they are still alive; the others have passed over. And so it is not bitter to those who do not yet know it, by the very fact that they do not know it, nor to those who now both feel nothing according to the body and have been set free according to the soul.

Chapter VIII.

31. But if it is reckoned terrible among the living, it is not death itself that is terrible but the opinion about death, which each one interprets according to his own feeling or dreads according to his own conscience. Let each one, then, blame the wound of his own conscience, not the harshness of death. Indeed, for the just death is a harbour of rest; for the guilty it is thought a shipwreck. Certainly for those to whom the fear of death is heavy it is not heavy to die, but it is heavy to live under the fear of death. It is not death, then, that is heavy, but the fear of death. And fear belongs to opinion, opinion to our weakness, and it is contrary to the truth; for through the truth comes virtue, through opinion weakness. Now opinion certainly belongs not to death but to life. So that heaviness is found to belong rather to life. It is clear, then, that the fear of death is to be referred not to death but to life. For we have nothing to fear in death if our life has committed nothing that should be feared. For to the prudent the punishments of offences are a terror, and offences are the acts not of the dead but of the living. Life, then, concerns us, since its conduct is in our power; but death is nothing to us. For it is the separation of soul and body: the soul is released, the body is dissolved. That which is released rejoices; that which is dissolved into its own earth feels nothing. What feels nothing is nothing to us.

32. Yet if death is an evil, how is it that the young do not fear to become old, nor dread the age that borders on death, and that he who passes away with his death foreseen passes away more patiently than he who meets it unforeseen? To those also who think death an evil I consider this a fitting answer: that through life there is a passage to death, but through death a return to life; for none can rise again except those who have died. The foolish dread death as the greatest of evils; the wise seek it as rest after labours and the end of evils.

33. The foolish fear death for two reasons. One is that they call it destruction. But there can be no destruction of a man, since the soul survives the body, not to mention that resurrection awaits the body itself. The other reason is that they dread punishments, frightened, that is, by the fables of the poets: the barking of Cerberus and the gloomy whirlpool of the river Cocytus, Charon gloomier still, the troops of the Furies or sheer Tartarus, then . . . . where a fiercer hydra has its seat; the entrails of Tityus too, fertile for torments ever renewed, which a monstrous vulture feeds on without any end; also the perpetual whirling of Ixion's wheel under the cruelty of punishment; then the impending fall of a stone hanging above the heads of those reclining at their banquets. These things are full of fables; and yet I would not deny that there are punishments after death. But what has that which is after death to do with death? And if the things that are after death are referred to death, then the things that are after life are likewise referred to life. There will therefore be no punishments to be referred to death. For death, as we said above, is the release and separation of soul and body; and loosing is not evil, for to be dissolved and to be with Christ is far better. Death, then, is not evil. Indeed, the death of sinners is very evil: not, certainly, death in general, but specifically that of sinners is very evil. And then: precious is that of the just. From this it is clear that the harshness belongs not to death but to guilt.

34. Beautifully did the Greeks call death an end; for they call death τελευτήν (teleutē), because it is the end of this life. But Scripture also names death sleep, as in this saying: Lazarus our friend sleeps; but I go to awaken him. And sleep is good, since it is repose, as it is written: I slept and rested and rose again, for the Lord will take me up. Sweet, then, is the repose of death. Indeed, the Lord awakens those who repose, because the Lord is the resurrection.

35. This too is excellent, what Scripture says: Before death praise no one. For each one is known in his last days and judged in his children, whether he has trained his sons well and taught them with fitting disciplines, since the dissoluteness of sons is referred to the negligence of the father; and also because each one, as long as he lives, is liable to falling, nor is old age immune from crime. Therefore you read that Abraham died in a good old age, because he remained in the goodness of his purpose. Death, then, is the testimony of life. For if a helmsman cannot be praised before he has brought his ship into harbour, how will you praise a man before he has come into the anchorage of death? He himself is his own helmsman, and he himself is tossed on the deep of this life: as long as he is on this sea, so long is he among shipwrecks. A general himself does not take the laurel unless the battle is finished, nor does a soldier lay down his arms or obtain the wage of his pay unless the enemy is overcome. Death, then, is the fullness of service, the sum of the wage, the grace of discharge.

36. And how much holy Job granted to death, when he said: May the blessing of one who is about to die come upon me! For even though Isaac blessed his sons as he was dying, and Jacob blessed the patriarchs, yet the grace of that blessing could be ascribed solely to the merits of those who blessed, or to fatherly love. Here, however, there is no prerogative of merits, none of fatherly love, but the privilege of death alone, since the blessing of anyone at all who is about to die has so much virtue that the holy prophet desired it for himself. And therefore let us always think on this little verse and hold it in our heart.

37. If we see a poor man about to die, let us help him with our means; let each one of us say: May the blessing of one who is about to die come upon me. If we see someone weak, let us not desert him; if we see someone at his last, let us not abandon him: let it occur to us too to say: May the blessing of one who is about to die come upon me. May everyone about to die praise you too, everyone laid low by age, everyone worn out by a grievous wound, everyone consumed by disease and already near to death. How many this little verse has caused to be blessed! How often it has struck me with shame, if I passed by one about to die, if I did not visit one gravely ill, if I disdained a needy man, if I did not redeem a captive or despised an old man! Let it be always in the heart, then, to spur on the harder and to admonish the readier. Let the last words of one about to die echo you, and let the soul as it goes out of the body carry with it a blessing on you. Rescue also him who is being led to death, who was about to perish unless you had come to his help, so that you may say: May the blessing of one who was about to perish come upon me.

Chapter IX.

38. Who, then, would doubt the good of death, when that which is restless, that which is shameful, that which is hostile to us, that which is violent, that which is stormy and alluring to every vice, comes to rest and lies still and is shut up like a wild beast in the cage of the tomb; when its madness is left lifeless and the dead framework of the entrails is dissolved into the earth; while that which is akin to the virtues, a friend to disciplines, eager for glory, a follower of the good, subject to God, flies away to that sublime place and abides with that pure, everlasting and immortal good, cleaves to it and is with it, from which it draws its kinship, as someone says: for we are also his offspring? For it is plain that the soul does not die with the body, because it is not from the body. And that it is not from the body Scripture teaches in many ways. For Adam too received the spirit of life from the Lord our God and became a living soul, and David says: Return, my soul, to your rest, for the Lord has done good to me. And hear in what he has done good: For he has delivered he says my feet from falling. You see that he gives thanks for the remedy of this death, because it has become the end of error, because it was guilt, not nature, that failed.

39. Then, as one stripped and free, he says: I will please the Lord in the region of the living. For that is the region . . . . Then he says that that rest of souls is the land of the living, where sins do not penetrate, where the glory of the virtues lives. But this region is full of the dead, because it is full of offences, and rightly was it said: Leave the dead to bury their own dead. But above, too, he says likewise: His soul shall dwell among good things, and his seed shall inherit the land; that is, the soul of him who fears God shall abide among good things, so as to be always in them and in accordance with them. This can also be taken of one who is in the body: that he too, if he fears God, may abide among good things and be in heavenly things, and possess his body and rule over it as over a thing brought into servitude, and possess the inheritance of glory and of the heavenly promises.

40. Hence we too, if we wish to be among good things after the death of this body, should take care that our soul is not glued to this body, not mixed with it, not clinging to it, not dragged by the body, and does not, as if drunk with its disturbances, stagger and waver; nor should it entrust itself to the body and to its delights so as to commit itself to its senses. For its eye too is error and fraud, because the sight is deceived, and its ear is deception, because the hearing too is mocked, and its taste is deception. Indeed, it was not said idly: Let your eyes see right things and: Let your tongue not speak perverse things. This would not have been said unless they often erred. You saw a harlot, you were captivated by her face and thought her form comely: your eyes erred, they saw perverse things, they reported something other than it was. For if they had seen truly, they would have seen the harlot's ugly feeling, the bristling wantonness, the unseemly shamelessness, the withering lusts, the foul filth, the wounds of the mind, the scars of the conscience. He who has looked he says on a woman to lust after her. You see that he sought what was false who sought not truth but adultery. For he sought to see in order to lust, not in order to know what is true. The eye errs, then, where the feeling errs. Feeling, then, is a deception: a deception of the sight (and therefore it is said to you: Nor be caught by the eyes, that is, let not your soul be caught; for a woman captures the precious souls of men), a deception of the hearing. Indeed, the fornicating woman has often seduced, deceived and mocked a young man's heart with much flattery of speech.

41. Let us not, then, trust these snares and nets, which deceive and mock, because hearts are tempted and thoughts are entangled. They are entangled by sight, entangled by hearing, smell, touch, taste. Let us not follow what is alluring and seductive, but let us follow that which is good, let us cleave to it, let us imitate it; let its presence, its communion make us better and colour our morals; let a certain fellowship with it shape us. For he who cleaves to the good takes on from it what is good, because it is written: With the holy you will be holy, and with the perverse you will be overturned, and with the innocent you will be innocent; for by constant companionship and imitation a kind of image of likeness is formed. And therefore he added: For you light my lamp, O Lord. For he who draws near to the light is the sooner lit up, and the splendour of the eternal light shines more brightly in him from close at hand. So the soul which cleaves to that invisible good, God, the immortal, itself also flees these bodily things and abandons earthly and mortal things, and becomes like that which it desires and in which it lives and is fed. And because it is intent on the immortal, it is not itself mortal. For the soul that sins dies, not indeed by any dissolution of itself, but it deservedly dies to God, because it lives to sin. So the soul that does not sin does not die, because it abides in its own substance, abides in virtue and glory.

42. For how can its substance perish, since it is surely the soul that pours in life? Into whatever the soul is poured, life is poured; from whatever the soul departs, life departs. The soul, then, is life. How can it receive death, since death is its contrary? For just as snow does not receive heat, for it at once melts, and light does not receive darkness, for it at once dispels it (for when light is poured in, the dread of darkness is taken away, just as when fire is brought near the stiffness of snow ceases), so also the soul, which creates life, does not receive death, does not die. But the soul does not receive death: therefore the soul does not die.

Chapter X.

43. We have, then, the reasoning. But these things are human; that is divine which the Lord says: I have power to lay down my soul, and I have power to take it up again. You see, then, that that which is both laid down and taken up again, and is commended into the hands of God the Father, does not die with the body. But perhaps you may say: 'Specially: that is, what is Christ's.' And although he took up the things that are man's, yet, because establishing this too is a matter for another occasion, so as not to waste time, hear him who says: How do you know whether this night your soul be demanded back from you? Did he say: Let your soul die in you? No, but: be demanded back from you. What has been given is demanded back, or they demand it back from you. For the soul is demanded back, not destroyed. What is demanded back remains; what is destroyed does not remain. For how is that destroyed of which the Wisdom of God said that no one is to be feared who can kill the body but cannot kill the soul? Of which the prophet says: My soul is in your hands always. Always he says, not for a time.

44. And you too, commend your soul into the hands of the Lord. Not only when it withdraws from the body, but also when it is in the body, it is in the hands of the Lord, because you do not see it, whence it comes or whither it goes. It is both in you and with God. Indeed, the heart of the king is in the hand of the Lord, and is ruled and governed by him. The heart is filled with mind, because the mind is the ruling part of the soul and is the strength of the soul. I do not mean the strength that is in the arms, but that which is in counsels, in temperance, piety and justice. If the heart of man is in the hand of the Lord, much more the soul. If the soul is in the hand of God, surely our soul is not shut up in the tomb together with the body, nor held by the grave mound, but enjoys a holy repose. And therefore men build costly tombs in vain, as if they were receptacles of the soul and not of the body alone.

45. That the dwellings of souls are on high is strongly proved by the testimonies of Scripture, since in the books of Esdras too we read that, when the day of judgement comes, the earth will give back the bodies of the dead, and the dust will give back those that rest in the tombs, the remains of the dead. And the dwellings he says will give back the souls that were entrusted to them, and the Most High will be revealed upon the seat of judgement. These are the habitations of which the Lord says that there are many dwelling places with his Father, which, as he went to the Father, he would prepare for his disciples. But I have made use of the writings of Esdras so that the pagans may know that the things they admire in the books of philosophy have been carried over from ours. And would that they had not mixed in with them superfluous and useless things, so as to say that the souls of men and of beasts are alike common, and that their highest prize would be if the souls of great philosophers migrated into bees or nightingales, so that those who had before fed the human race with discourse should afterwards soothe it with the sweetness of honey or the charm of song! It would have been enough for them to have said that souls freed from bodies made for Ἅιδην (Hades), that is, a place that was not seen, which place we call in Latin the underworld (infernum).

46. Indeed, Scripture too has named those dwellings of souls storehouses; and, meeting the complaint of men that the just who have gone before seem to be cheated of the reward due to them until the day of judgement, through a very long time, that is, it says wonderfully that the day of judgement is like a crown, in which as there is no slowness of the last, so there is no swiftness of the first. For the day of the crown is awaited by all, so that within that day both the vanquished may blush and the victors may obtain the palm of victory. Nor did it leave this hidden either: why those who were born earlier seem superior and those born later weaker. For it compared the births of this age to the womb of a woman, since those who were born in the youth of strength are stronger, and weaker those who were born in the time of old age. For this age has failed through the multitude of its generation, like the womb of a woman who bears, and, like a creature growing old, it lays aside the vigour of its youth, as the force of its powers now withers.

47. So while the fullness of time is awaited, the souls await the reward due to them. Punishment awaits some, glory others: and yet neither are the former meanwhile without injury nor the latter without fruit. For those too . . . . seeing that a reward of glory is laid up for those who keep God's law, that their dwellings are kept by angels, but that for themselves there are to come the punishments of neglect and stubbornness, and shame and confusion, so that, beholding the glory of the Most High, they blush to come into the sight of him whose commands they have violated. For as was the transgression of Adam, so also is the confusion; since, just as he fell through carelessness about the heavenly commands and hid himself for shame at his falling, not daring, through the bashfulness of a sinful conscience, to come under the brightness of the divine presence, so also the souls of sinners will not bear the splendour of his flashing light, by whose witness they will remember that they went astray.

Chapter XI.

48. But the joy of the souls of the just is set out through certain orders. First, that they have overcome the flesh and have not been bent by its allurements. Next, that as the price of their diligence and innocence they obtain security, and are not entangled, as the souls of the ungodly are, in certain errors and disturbances, nor tormented by the memory of their vices and driven about by certain surges of cares. Third, that they are upheld by the divine testimony that they have kept the Law, so that at the final judgement they do not fear an uncertain outcome of their deeds. Fourth, that they begin to understand their rest and to foresee their future glory, and, soothing themselves with that consolation, they will rest in their dwellings in great tranquillity, surrounded by the guard of angels. The fifth order, moreover, holds the sweetness of the most abundant exultation, that from this prison of the corruptible body they have come into light and liberty and possess the inheritance promised to them. For there is an order of repose, because there is also one of resurrection. For as in Adam all die, so also in Christ, he says, shall all be made alive, but each in his own order: Christ the firstfruits, then those who are Christ's, who have believed in his coming, then the end. There will therefore be a diverse order of brightness and glory, just as there will be an order of merits. The progression of the orders, too, expresses a progression of brightness. Then, in the sixth order, it will be shown to them that their face begins to shine like the sun and to be compared to the lights of the stars, yet that this radiance of theirs can no longer feel corruption. The seventh order will be this: that they exult with confidence, trust without any hesitation and rejoice without trembling, hastening to see the face of him to whom they rendered the obedience of diligent service, from whom, recalling their guiltless conscience, they confidently expect the glorious wage of their small labour; and as they begin to receive it, they have come to know that the sufferings of this time are not worthy, since so great a glory of eternal reward is paid back for them. 'This is the order,' he says, 'of the souls which are of the just', whom he did not hesitate even to call immortal in the fifth order, 'because,' he says, 'they begin to receive spaciousness, enjoying it and immortal'. 'This is,' he says, 'their rest through seven orders and the first experience of future glory, before they fully enjoy in their habitations the gift of a quiet gathering. Whence the prophet says to the angel: So time will be given to the souls, after they have been separated from the bodies, to see these things as you have said. And the angel said: Their liberty will be for seven days, that in seven days they may see the words that were foretold, and afterwards they will be gathered in their dwellings'. These things have been set out more fully concerning the orders of the just than concerning the sufferings of the ungodly for this reason, that it is better to know how the innocent are saved than how the wicked are tormented.

49. Since, then, the just have this reward, that they see the face of God and that light which enlightens every man, let us from now on put on such zeal that our soul may draw near to God, our prayer may draw near, our desire may cling to him, and we may not be separated from him. And while we are placed here, let us be united to God by meditating, reading, seeking; let us know him as we can; for here we have known in part, because here all things are imperfect, there perfect; here we are little children, there strong. We see, he says, now through a mirror in an enigma, but then face to face. Then, with unveiled face, it will be granted to behold the glory of God, which now souls wrapped in the dense entrails of this body and overshadowed by certain stains and filth of this flesh cannot see clearly. For who, he says, shall see my face and live? And rightly. For if our eyes cannot bear the rays of the sun, and if anyone gazes too long straight at the sun he is said usually to be blinded; if a creature cannot look upon a creature without injury and hurt to itself, how can one covered with the husk of this body behold without danger to himself the flashing face of the eternal Creator? For who is justified in the sight of God, when even an infant of one day cannot be clean from sin, and no one can glory in the integrity and chastity of his heart?

50. Let us not fear, then, to be taken up from among men; let us not dread that end which is owed to all, in which Esdras found the reward of his devotion, when the Lord said to him: for you will be taken up from among men, and you will live for the rest of time with my Son and with those like you. But if for him it was glorious and delightful to live with those like him, how much more glorious and delightful will it be for us to go on to better ones and to live with those whose deeds we admire!

51. Who, indeed, was earlier, Esdras or Plato? For Paul followed the sayings of Esdras, not of Plato. Esdras revealed, according to the revelation granted to him, that the just will be with Christ, will be with the saints. Hence that Socrates too says that he is hastening to those gods of his, to those best of men. So the things that excel in the writings of the philosophers are ours. And he set down things for which he had no testimony of his own; we have the authority of a divine precept. Moses and Elijah appeared with Christ; Abraham received two others with God as guests; Jacob beheld the camp of God; Daniel declared, as the Holy Spirit revealed it to him, that the just shine like the sun and the stars in heaven.

Chapter XII.

52. Relying, then, on these things, let us go fearlessly to our Redeemer Jesus; fearlessly to the council of the patriarchs, fearlessly to Abraham our father let us set out, when the day has come; fearlessly let us go to that assembly of the saints and gathering of the just. For we shall go to our fathers, we shall go to those teachers of our faith, so that, even if works are lacking, faith may help us and the inheritance be defended. We shall go also where holy Abraham spreads open his bosom to receive the poor, as he received Lazarus too, in which bosom rest those who in this world have endured hard and harsh things.

53. But now, father, again and again stretch out your hands to receive the poor man from here, open your lap, spread out your bosom, so that you may receive more, because very many have believed in the Lord. But nevertheless, although faith has grown, iniquity abounds, charity grows cold. We shall go to those who recline in the kingdom of God with Abraham and Isaac and Jacob, since, when invited to the supper, they did not make excuses. We shall go there, where there is a paradise of delight, where Adam, who fell among robbers, no longer knows how to weep for his wounds, where the robber himself also rejoices in a share in the heavenly kingdom, where no clouds, no thunders, no lightnings, no storm of winds, nor darkness nor evening nor summer nor winter will vary the changes of the seasons; there will be no cold, no hail, no rains, no use of this sun or of the moon, nor the globes of the stars, but the brightness of God alone will shine. For the Lord will be the light of all, and that true light which enlightens every man will shine for all. We shall go there, where the Lord Jesus has prepared dwelling places for his little servants, that where he is we too may be; for so he willed. What those dwelling places are, hear him saying: In my Father's house there are many dwelling places; what his will is: Again, he says, I come and summon you to myself, that where I am you too may be.

54. But you say that he was speaking to the disciples alone, that to them alone he promised many dwelling places; so he was preparing them for eleven disciples only. And where then is that saying, that they will come from all parts and recline in the kingdom of God? Why do we doubt the accomplishment of the divine will? But for Christ, to will is to have done. Then he showed both the way and the place, saying: And where I go you know, and my way you know. The place is with the Father, the way is Christ, as he himself says: I am the way and the truth and the life. No one comes to the Father except through me. Let us enter this way, hold fast the truth, follow the life. It is the way that leads, the truth that confirms, the life that is given back through itself. And so that we may know his true will, he added in later words: Father, those whom you have given me, I will that where I am they too may be with me, that they may see my glory, Father. This repetition is a confirmation, like that saying: Abraham, Abraham! and elsewhere: I am, I am he who blots out your iniquities. And it is beautiful that what he promised above, here he asked for. And because he promised first and so asked, and did not ask first and so promise, he promised as the arbiter of the gift, conscious of his power; he asked of the Father as the interpreter of piety. He promised first, so that you may recognize his power; he asked afterwards, so that you may understand his piety. He did not ask first and so promise, lest he should seem to have promised what he had obtained rather than to have fulfilled what he had promised. Nor should you think it superfluous that he asked, since it expresses to you his sharing in the Father's will, in which there is a sign of unity, not an increase of power.

55. We follow you, Lord Jesus; but summon us, that we may follow, because without you no one will ascend. For you are the way, the truth, the life, the possibility, the faith, the prize. Receive your own as the way, confirm them as the truth, give them life as the life. Lay open that good of yours which David, dwelling in the house of the Lord, desired to see, and therefore he said: Who will show us good things? and elsewhere: I believe that I shall see the good things of the Lord in the land of the living. For there are good things where there is perpetual life, life without guilt. Elsewhere too he says: We shall be filled with the good things of your house. He repeated this often so that you might know that the philosophers transferred from here that good which they declare to be the highest. Lay open, then, that truly good thing of yours, that divine good, in which we both live and are and move. We move as in the way, we are as in the truth, we live as in eternal life. Show us that which is good, like itself, always indissoluble and unchangeable, in which we may be eternal in the knowledge of every good, as Paul, the vessel of your election, testified, saying: For perhaps he departed for an hour for this reason, that you might receive him as eternal. So he called the minister of God eternal, writing to Philemon, whose faith, in the knowledge of every good that is in the saints, he asked to become more evident in Christ Jesus. In this good there is pure rest, immortal light, perpetual grace, the inheritance of souls, a holy and secure tranquillity, not subject to death but rescued from death, where there are no tears, there is no weeping — for whence weeping there, where there is no falling? — where your saints are released from errors and anxieties, from folly and ignorance, from fear and dread, from desires and all bodily filth and passions; where the region of the living is; and, that we may add authority to this assertion, concerning this good the prophet says: Return, my soul, to your rest, for the Lord has done good to me, because he has rescued my soul from death, my eyes from tears, my feet from falling. I will please the Lord in the region of the living. I will please, he said, not I please; that is, he flatters himself about the time to come. But future things are contrary to present ones, and eternal things to temporal ones. And therefore, since there is the region of the living, here surely is that of the dead.

56. Or is this not the region of the dead, where there is the shadow of death, where the gate of death, where the body of death? Then it is granted to Peter that the gates of hell shall not prevail against him. These gates of hell are earthly ones, whence he too says: You who lift me up from the gates of death. For just as there are gates of justice, in which the saints confess to the Lord, so there are gates of crimes, in which the ungodly have denied the Lord. Hear that this region is that of the dead: If anyone, then, touches a dead man, he will be unclean; and every unjust man is unclean in the sight of the Lord. If anyone, then, touches iniquity, he will be unclean; if anyone lives in pleasures, he is dead, since she who lives in pleasures is dead. And those who are unbelievers go down alive into hell: even if they seem to live with us, yet they are in hell. If anyone takes usury, commits robbery, he does not live by life, as you have it in Ezekiel. But if anyone who is just keeps the ordinances of the Lord so as to do them, 'by life,' he says, 'he shall live and he shall live in them'. He, then, is in the region of the living, in that region where life is not hidden but free, where there is no shadow but glory; for here not even Paul himself lived in glory. Indeed, he groaned in the body of death. Hear him saying: for now our life is hidden with Christ in God; but when Christ, our life, appears, then we too shall appear with him in glory.

57. Let us hasten, then, to life. If anyone touches life, he lives. Indeed, that woman touched it who touched his fringe and was set free from death, to whom it is said: Your faith has saved you. Go in peace. For if he who touches a dead man is unclean, without doubt he who touches the living one is saved. Let us seek, then, the living one. But again let us see that we do not seek him among the dead, and it be said to us as to those women: Why do you seek the living one with the dead? He is not here, but he has risen. The Lord himself too showed where he wished to be sought, saying: Go to my brothers and say to them: I ascend to my Father and your Father, to my God and your God. There, then, shall we seek him, where John sought and found him. He sought him in the beginning and found him living with the living one, the Son with the Father. Let us seek him at the end of the times, and embrace his feet and worship him, so that he may say to us too: Do not fear, that is, do not fear the sins of the world, do not fear the iniquities of the world, do not fear the waves of bodily passions: I am the forgiveness of sins. Do not fear the darkness: I am the light. Do not fear death: I am the life. Whoever comes to me shall not see death for ever, since he himself is the fullness of divinity, and his are honour, glory and everlastingness from the ages, both now and always and for all the ages of ages.
