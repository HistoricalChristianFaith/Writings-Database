On Jacob and the Blessed Life

Latin source: Ambrose's two books on Jacob and the blessed life, reworked from sermons he preached at Milan (about 386-388), from the critical text of C. Schenkl, Corpus Scriptorum Ecclesiasticorum Latinorum 32/2 (Vienna, 1897), pp. 3–70, verified against the page images and collated in part with the Benedictine edition as reprinted in Migne, PL 14, with the edition's misprints corrected; in two places the text departs from Schenkl with the other manuscripts and the Benedictine edition (Book One, Chapter VIII §39, without his non; Book Two, Chapter VII §33, sonum for his somnum). Scripture is translated as Ambrose quotes it, from his own Old Latin text; italics mark the Scripture he quotes. The section numbers are the Maurist ones shared by all editions and restart in Book Two; the Maurist chapter summaries are not translated. The one Greek word (Book One, Chapter II) is kept as printed, with an English rendering in parentheses; Psalms keep the Latin (LXX) numbering, with the Hebrew number in parentheses. Schenkl's supplements are read into the text, the words he brackets as spurious are omitted, his two lacunae (Book One, Chapter I §2; Book Two, Chapter VIII §36) are marked with dots, and his critical apparatus is not translated. This English translation is released into the public domain.

Book One

Chapter I.

1. Good speech, full of prudence, is necessary to all for instruction, and a mind intent on reason runs ahead in the virtues and restrains the passions; for virtue can be taught. Indeed, it is sought by study and by learning, and it is lost by neglect. Otherwise, unless good speech were necessary for correction, the Law would never say: You shall not commit adultery. But because bare speech is useful for admonishing but weak for persuading, therefore the consideration of right reason must be applied, so that what good speech has prescribed, reason, more fully worked out, may persuade. For we are not constrained to obey by a slave's necessity, but by the will as judge, whether we lean towards virtue or incline towards guilt. And so either a free disposition draws us into error, or the will, following reason, calls us back. Now the most grievous passion of guilt is concupiscence, which reason softens and represses. For it can soften it; it cannot root it out, since the soul that is capable of reason is not the master of its passions but their restrainer. For it cannot be that one who is quick to anger should not be angry, but only that he should temper himself by reason, check his indignation and hold himself back from punishing, as the prophet too teaches us, saying: Be angry and do not sin. He granted what belongs to nature; he forbade what belongs to guilt.

2. Every . . . . . . , then, takes its origin from other things, not from itself, and is therefore secondary. For it is taken up either from natural things or from useful things. And so either it tempers natural things or it is the champion of utility. Indeed, it does not cut out the concupiscence of the soul, but brings it about that we do not serve concupiscence. For who is so great as to be able to take away a bodily movement, except him alone who could say of the unfruitful fig tree, that is, of the malice of the Jews: Behold, it is three years since I came seeking fruit on this fig tree, and I do not find it: cut it down, then. The slave answered him: Leave it this year also, until I dig around it and put in a basket of dung. And if indeed it bears fruit: but if not, in the future cut it down. Rightly did he refer it to the Lord, because he could not claim for himself the power of cutting out the lapse of the body, but reserved it for the Lord.

3. Indeed, whom among men shall we take as better and braver than holy David, who could not take away from himself the desire with which he had longed for the water from the cistern of Bethlehem, cut off by the enemy army, yet could mitigate it? For since we do not find that water was lacking to the others, that is, to so great a number in the army, and since water from the other springs could certainly have been much less lacking to the king, he suffered a certain irrational concupiscence and desired that water which was walled about by the encircling enemy, from where it could not easily have been brought without the greatest danger. And so he says: Who will give me a drink from the cistern that is in Bethlehem at the gate? And when three men were found to break through the enemy's camp and bring the water which he had desired with the greatest longing, recognizing that this same water had cost him the danger of others, he poured it out to the Lord, so that he might not seem to drink the blood of those who had brought it. This is a sign that concupiscence indeed comes before reason, but that reason resists desire. David, then, suffered something human, in that he desired irrationally; but this is praiseworthy, that he cheated an irrational concupiscence with a remedy rationally prepared. While I praise the men who blushed at their king's longing and preferred to put an end to his shame even at the peril of their own safety, I praise him more, who in his own longing blushed at another's danger and likened to blood the water sought at the price of an uncertain lot. At the same time, like a victor with his desire repressed, he poured out the water to the Lord, to show that he was quenching his concupiscence with the consolation of the word.

4. The sober mind, then, can bridle and repress the impressions of passions, however grievous, and cool all the heat of the most burning desire, divert its movements elsewhere, and spit out the passions by the reasoning of right reason. For when God established man and planted in him character and senses, he then set over his movements the royal rule of the mind, so that all the senses and movements of man might be governed by its vigour and power. To the grace of creation he added this: that he should form the mind itself with divine precepts and instruct it with the disciplines of wisdom, by which it might know what is to be avoided and recognize what is to be chosen. The mind, therefore, holding the discipline of wisdom by right reason, so as to know things divine and human, is instructed in the Law, through which it learns which passions it ought to subject to itself.

Chapter II.

5. Now the natural leaders, as it were, of the passions are pleasure and pain, and the rest follow them. For these two embrace all of them, and each of them is a passion not only of the body but also according to the soul. And since we have said that other passions lie beneath these: before pleasure comes concupiscence, after pleasure rejoicing; before pain comes fear, after pain sadness; while agitation of the soul is a passion common to both pleasure and pain. I shall pass over the others, that is, pride, avarice, ambition, contentiousness and envy, which are passions according to the soul; I shall pass over also the insatiable lust for devouring and the outpouring of luxury and wantonness, vices which are bound up with the body and work according to it. And rightly does temperance above all quench the heat of these passions: first it tempers the soul with sobriety and moderation and forms the mind, then also, by abstinence from delicacies, it draws tight the reins of bodily fierceness. Therefore the Law cut back licence in foods and abundance in feasts, not only to prune away luxury, but also, through the contemplation of a restraining precept, to open a way for the reasoning of reason, which might cut back the incitements of gluttony and the other desires and check the bodily passions and movements. Temperance, then, is the forerunner of correction, the mistress of discipline.

6. Setting out from this, holy Jacob received from his brother the rights of the firstborn, which he did not have, and, preferred with his brother's assent, taught for the future that the intemperate are worthless even in their own judgment. Setting out from this, Joseph both tamed the heat of youth and strengthened by the application of right reason a soul tempted by adulterous enticements. Indeed, although he was strong and vigorous, yet he preferred to support himself by the reasoning of reason, saying to his master's wife: If my master does not know of anything in his house because of me, and has given all that he has into my hands, and nothing has been withheld from me except you, because you are his wife, how then shall I do this evil thing and sin before God? This, then, is the reasoning of right reason, which the Greeks call λογισμόν (reasoning), by which the mind intent on wisdom is made firm. For it is a fine reason: that he ought not to be ungrateful for his master's kindnesses, and that a sin which he would commit with God as witness, from whom he could not hide, could not be hidden.

7. Good, then, is reason, which often both strips off a hostile disposition and sets aside the pain of an injury. Indeed, even in battle it often softens the victor, holds back the sword of one about to strike, and snatches from death the one who begs, because just reason persuades him to spare the subdued. For as to shutting out or softening the pain of an injury, who is a better master than the patriarch Jacob, who, rebuking his own sons Simeon and Levi, said: You have made me hateful, so that I seem cruel. And indeed they had avenged the injury of their sister, who, against the customs of their fathers, had been defiled by the violation of her chastity. Yet Jacob, the teacher of discipline, the guardian of chastity, could not approve the outrage that had been committed, but preferred that the insolent be restrained by reason, knowing that reason could temper indignation.

8. It is temperance, then, that prunes away desires. God commanded the first human beings to hold to it, saying: But of the fruit of the tree that is in the middle of paradise you shall not eat, nor shall you touch it, lest you die. And because it was not held to, therefore the transgressors of an excellent virtue were made exiles from paradise and without a share in immortality. This the Law teaches and pours into the dispositions of all.

Chapter III.

9. Now Scripture testifies that temperance is taught, and wisdom and discipline as well: temperance in the Law, the others in the book of Job, in which it is written: Is it not the Lord who teaches understanding and discipline? And in the Gospel the Lord himself says: Learn from me, for I am meek and humble of heart. And elsewhere he says to the disciples: Go, teach the nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit. And from what were they called disciples, or what else did they learn from Christ but to carry out the precepts of the virtues? Indeed, David says: Come, sons, hear me; I will teach you the fear of the Lord. Certainly the fear of God is of the number of the virtues, because the beginning of wisdom is the fear of God, through which the form of devout teaching is acquired, of which Paul says: But thanks be to God, that you were slaves of sin, but you obeyed from the heart that form of teaching into which you were handed over; and, freed from sin, you have become slaves of justice. Teaching, then, brings it about that we can attain to justice. Justice, therefore, can be acquired by learning. Let us therefore apply ourselves with zeal to the form of the Gospel teaching. Very little zeal is often reckoned as very much. For all things lie in zeal, through which obedience is applied, and obedience, whichever way it has leaned, adds either guilt or grace. It was this that drew us to death in the first Adam; it was this that called us to life in the second Adam.

10. There is no reason for us to ascribe our hardship to anyone but to our own will. No one is held to guilt unless he has turned aside by his own will. Things inflicted on those who resist carry no crime; the odium of offences follows only deeds voluntarily committed, odium which we would shift onto others. Christ chooses for himself a voluntary soldier; the devil buys at auction for himself a voluntary slave. He possesses no one bound by the yoke of slavery unless that one has first sold himself to him for the coin of sins. Why do we accuse the flesh as weak? Our members are weapons of injustice and weapons of justice. You saw a poor man suffering injury, you protected him: your members are weapons of devotion, with which you defended the poor man from injury. You saw a needy man, you presented him with gifts: with your right hand you drove death from your own breast. You saw one who was being led to death, you rescued him, because it is written: Rescue him who is led to death: your members are weapons of justice, if you did not allow a man to perish unjustly. You saw a woman, you chastised your body, you mortified your lusts, you turned away from the wanton eyes of a harlot and forsook them: your members are weapons of chastity. But on the contrary, if your eye has seen a woman so as to desire her, you have opened a wound, you have driven a dart into your body: your members are weapons of sin. You saw the possession of orphans and drove them from their fathers' homes, you moved the boundaries which your fathers set: your members are weapons of iniquity. The disposition, then, not the flesh, is the author of guilt, while the flesh is the handmaid of the will.

11. Let our will, then, not sell us. The Apostle cries: Do you not know that to whomever you present yourselves as slaves for obedience, you are slaves of him whom you obey, whether of sin unto death or of obedience unto justice? Therefore, if we serve either sin or justice, let us consider on which side the slavery is more tolerable and the fruit richer. But what fruit can there be in death? For the wages of sin is death, and therefore there is no fruit in it, but only a loss of honour, when we blush at the things we have done. But to serve justice is freedom; for he who was called in the Lord as a slave is a freedman of the Lord; likewise he who was called as a free man is a slave of Christ. Both conditions are excellent, to be under Christ, under whom there is both a precious slavery and a glorious freedom: a precious slavery, as acquired at the price of such great blood, and a glorious freedom, which no slavery of guilt, no chains of sins constrain, which no burdens of shameful deeds, no traffic in crimes, consign to the bond of a degenerate slavery.

12. Learn humility, O man; recognize the force of the Apostle's teaching. If you call yourself a slave, you are a freedman; if you boast that you are free, you are a slave. For he who was redeemed as a slave has freedom, and as for him who was called as a free man, it is good for him to recognize that he is a slave of Christ, under whom slavery is safe and freedom secure. Who would claim that Paul was ignorant, as it were, even in the law itself? He knew how to distinguish between a freedman and a free man, and therefore he spoke not carelessly but precisely: For he who was called in the Lord as a slave is a freedman of the Lord; likewise he who was called as a free man is a slave of Christ. For in truth we are all Christ's freedmen, none of us a free man; for all were begotten in slavery. Why do you take on the arrogance of freedom in a slave's condition? Why do you usurp titles of nobility, you, the inheritance of a slave? Do you not know that the guilt of Adam and Eve made you over to slavery? Do you not know that Christ redeemed you, he did not buy you? You were redeemed not with gold and silver from your vain way of life of your fathers' tradition, but with the precious blood of the Lamb cries the apostle Peter. Therefore you have been redeemed by the Lord, and you are a slave because you were created, you are a slave because you were redeemed, and you owe slavery both to him as your Lord and to him as your redeemer. Nor should you think the status of a freedman under Christ inferior to freedom. In dignity it is equal, in protection more excellent, in grace on a par, more wary against falling, better sheltered against pride. You have received freedom on such terms that you ought to remember the one who set you free, and that you should know that lawful deference must be paid to your patron, lest freedom be revoked from one who is ungrateful. What is more blessed than you, who reign under the Lord and serve as a soldier under a patron?

Chapter IV.

13. But what is there that the Lord has not conferred on you? He gave the Law; he added grace. For the Law announced sin, but in a slippery condition it could not restrain it completely. For I came to know sin, which I did not know; I came to know that concupiscence is sin, and on this occasion of the knowledge of sin the debts were heaped up, because sin, which before had seemed dead through my ignorance, revived in me; but I died under the wound of sin, because the knowledge of guilt, which seemed likely to profit me, did me harm, so that I knew what I could not avoid. For it betrayed sin, and through the good of its announcement it heaped up the odium of sin itself. And so sin became beyond measure for me, because it was heaped up by the announcement of the commandment; for guilt grows when it is revealed and not guarded against. How then is the commandment good, which is death to me? Or how is it not death to me, since sin, which was to be shown up through the good of its signification, has wrought death in me? For it is certain that death came upon me when I recognize that what I do is sin, as the Lord himself says: If I had not come and spoken to them, they would not have sin.

14. Why then do you wonder if the Law is death to some, when even the saving coming of the Lord, through which we have been redeemed, is death? For the unbeliever seeks death for himself from grace, but the grace of the commandment remains. Indeed, just as the knowledge of poisoned cups is brought to completion for the discipline of medicine, so the divine commandment is for eternal life. But just as, for one who makes bad use of harmful potions, the knowledge of them is turned to evil and danger, and the more he has come to know poisons, the more he understands that he is in danger, so the form of the commandment is the author of death for those who interpret the Law badly, or who cannot avoid sins even when they have been shown and forbidden. Just as, then, an antidote is good, even if it is not good for the imprudent or the intemperate, so the commandment is good, even if it is not good for the intemperate. Therefore the commandment is good which is death to someone. Have, then, the answer to the first proposition: that the commandment which is death to me can be good: good through the nature of the saving precept, death through the intemperance of the flesh.

15. And since we have learned that the commandment which is death to me is good, let us now discuss how that which, though through the good, has wrought death for me is not death to me. For thus the Apostle put it: Is that which is good, then, death to me? By no means; but sin, that it might appear as sin, wrought death for me through the good. So let us look at each point. The commandment certainly belongs to the Law, and the Law is spiritual: I see its grace, I praise its beauty, I proclaim its form, I admire its precept; but because I am carnal and have been sold under sin, I am drawn unwillingly to guilt. For guilt lords it over me as over a slave. And so I hate the crime and I do it. The mind hates, the flesh desires; yet I am in both, I who with the mind consent to the Law and with the flesh do what I do not will. Good, then, is the commandment, to which I consent, and good is the mind, which chooses what is good. It is good for judging, but often weak for resisting, because the appetite of the body fights against it and drags it captive to the enticements of error.

16. In this danger there is one remedy: that the grace of God should free him whom the Law could not free. For thus it is written: Unhappy man that I am, who will free me from the body of this death? The grace of God through Jesus Christ our Lord. And so it comes about that, although the sin that lay hidden, that is, my concupiscence, which I did not think was sin, wrought death for me when it was revealed, and sin itself became beyond measure — for it was sin, even if it was not known, but by knowledge it was heaped up and took on, as it were, an increase of error, and the form of the commandment passed over into the nature of an offence — yet it is not death to me, for whom it is ready at hand to take refuge in Christ, through whom we are released from every danger of death. The second proposition, then, is also resolved: that the commandment of the Law is not death to me, even if it works death. For that we are thrown into confusion belongs to our frailty; that we escape belongs to Christ.

Chapter V.

17. Therefore, to return to the beginning of this discourse, the mind is good which has the reasoning of reason and is intent on the disciplines of wisdom; but it has a heavy struggle with the body of death, and often the enticement of the flesh overcomes the reason of the mind. Therefore the Lord first gave the Law, to which the mind of man gave itself up to obey, and it began to serve it, so as to be subject to it; but the flesh was not subject, because the wisdom of the flesh is not subject to the Law, and it fought against its precepts. For it could not obey virtue, given over as it was to desires and entangled in the allurements of the flesh. Therefore we must labour to hold fast the grace of God. The mind, then, is good, if it is intent on reason, but hardly perfect unless it has the helm of Christ. For the Lord Jesus came to fix our passions to his cross and to forgive sins. In his death we have been justified, so that the whole world might be cleansed by his blood. Indeed, we were baptized in his death.

18. If, then, our sins are forgiven us in his death, let the passions of sins also die for us in his death, let them be held fastened by the nails of his cross. If we have died in his death, why are we called back again, as though living, to the things of the world? What have we to do with the elements of the world, what with desires, what with luxury and wantonness, to which we have died in Christ? But if we have died in Christ, in Christ we have risen again; let us then dwell with Christ, let us seek with Christ the things that are above, not those that are corruptible and earthly. Christ, rising from the dead, left the old man fastened to the cross and raised up the new. Christ died for this reason, that we too might die to sin and rise again to God. Our flesh is dead: why does it come to life again for sin, why does it obey sin again? Why does sin reign again among the dead, when death is the end of sin? We have died in the flesh, we have been renewed in the spirit. Let us walk in the spirit, because we have received the Spirit of Christ. But if Christ is in us, then let the flesh be dead for us because of sin, but let the spirit live because of justification.

19. Thus what was impossible for the Law is resolved, if we walk in the spirit; thus we bury the passions, if we do not unfasten the cross of this body, if we do not write out again the bond of sin which was blotted out on the cross of Christ, if we do not put on again the garment of the old man which we have put off. For it is written in the Song of Songs: I have put off my tunic; how shall I put it on? I have washed my feet; how shall I defile them? The members of the body, then, have been mortified for us: why do its vices sprout? For this reason the Law did not prevail, because it did not mortify the flesh; for this reason it passed away like a shadow, because it gave no colour; for this reason too it overshadowed us from the sun of justice, because it heaped up crimes: therefore it also did harm.

Chapter VI.

20. What need was there, then, for the Law to be promulgated, if it was not going to profit? We already had the law of nature; each one was a law to himself, who had the work of the law written in his heart. That law we did not keep: why was a second one added as well, by whose works the flesh could not be justified? A bond was added, not a release; there was added the recognition of sins, not their remission. We have all sinned, we who could have put forward the excuse of ignorance: the mouth of all has been stopped.

21. Yet it profited me. I began to confess what I used to deny, I began to recognize my offence and not to cover up my injustice, I began to declare my injustice against myself to the Lord, and you forgave the impieties of my heart. But this too profits me, that we are not justified by the works of the Law. I have, then, nothing for which I could glory in my works, I have nothing for which to boast of myself, and therefore I will glory in Christ. I will not glory because I am just, but I will glory because I have been redeemed. I will glory not because I am empty of sins, but because my sins have been forgiven me. I will not glory because I have been of profit, nor because anyone has been of profit to me, but because Christ is an advocate for me with the Father, because the blood of Christ was shed for me. My guilt has become for me the reward of redemption, through which Christ came to me. For my sake Christ tasted death. Guilt is more fruitful than innocence. Innocence had made me arrogant; guilt has made me submissive.

22. You have, then, the ways in which the giving of the law has profited you. But you say that through the Law sin superabounded. But where sin superabounded, grace also superabounded. You are dead to sin, O man; therefore the Law no longer does harm. You rise again through grace; therefore the Law has profited, because it gained grace. You have received also the pledge of Christ's love, since he who died for you is your advocate and keeps in store the reward of his blood, and he who reconciled the sinner to the Father much more commends the innocent and protects the submissive, because he has taken him to himself as guiltless.

23. Do you, then, a debtor for so great a benefit, not pay back deference? He has made you an heir, he has made you a co-heir: an heir of God, a co-heir of Christ; he has poured into you the spirit of adoption. Count these things up, and apply them not so much to the bond of a debt as to the keeping of a gift received. You are a co-heir of Christ, if you suffer with him, if you die with him, if you are buried with Christ. Take up his passions, so that you may deserve to be with him above the passions. See how he has forgiven you your former sins, so as to make the fact that you sinned do you no harm. See how he exhorts you not to lose what you have received. Short is the goal of this labour, and the crown is of everlasting fruit; the suffering is bearable, the reward beyond reckoning. For what distresses you? The lowliness of humble birth? But you will have in the future a glorious nobility of devotion and faith. A slender fortune, a straitened living? But you will have the riches of an eternal recompense, in which you can lack nothing. The loss of children? You will receive back as everlasting those whom you had taken up as temporal. It will be said of you: Blessed is he who has seed in Zion and kinsfolk in Jerusalem. Unworthy it says are the sufferings of this time in view of the glory to come: so Scripture tells you.

24. Add that the blessed life is not diminished by these adversities of worldly troubles or bodily sufferings, but rather is proved. Add that it either does not feel the losses of its patrimony, or with a brave mind hides the losses of its kinsfolk and swallows up its grief. Add that he who is always in the harbour of tranquillity knows no shipwrecks. What of this, that your labour is shared with all creation, since for your sake the world itself endures the slavery of corruption, since you have a common partnership with the saints in this labour and expectation? The sun acknowledges its setting, the moon its waning, the lights of the stars their wandering, while the redemption of our whole body is awaited.

25. But do you fear the uncertain windings of life and the snares of the adversary, when you have the help of God, when you have from him such great condescension that he did not spare his own Son for you? Scripture has used a beautiful word to declare the loving purpose of God the Father towards you, who offered his Son to death. And the Son could not feel the bitterness of death; but, as far as lay with the Father, he kept back nothing for himself, he offered the whole for you, because in the fullness of the divinity he himself lost nothing, and he redeemed you. Consider a father's affection. As is the part of love, he took on the danger as of a son about to die, he drank in the grief as of bereavement, so that the fruit of redemption might not be lost to you. So great was the Lord's zeal for your salvation that he almost put at risk what was his own while he was gaining you. For your sake he took on our losses, to graft you into things divine and consecrate you to things heavenly. Wonderfully too he added: He handed him over for us all, to show that he so loves all that he handed over his most beloved Son for each one. Can it be, then, that for those for whom he gave what is above all things, he has not given all things in him? For he excepted nothing who gave up the author of all things.

26. There is nothing, then, that we should fear can be denied us, nothing in which we ought to distrust the perseverance of the divine munificence, whose abundance was so lasting and unbroken that first he predestined, then he called, and those whom he called he also justified, and those whom he justified he also glorified. Will he be able to desert those whom he has attended with such great benefits of his all the way to the rewards? Amid so many benefits of God, are any snares of the accuser to be feared? But who would dare to accuse those whom he sees chosen by divine judgment? Can God the Father himself, who conferred them, revoke his own gifts and banish from the grace of a father's affection those whom he has taken up by adoption? But there is the fear that the judge may be too severe: consider whom you have as judge. Surely the Father has given all judgment to Christ. Will he, then, be able to condemn you, whom he redeemed from death, for whom he offered himself, whose life he knows to be the reward of his own death? Will he not say: What profit is there in my blood, if I condemn the one whom I myself have saved? Then again, you consider the judge; you do not consider the advocate. Can he pronounce a more severe sentence, who does not cease to intercede that the grace of the Father's reconciliation may be bestowed on us?

Chapter VII.

27. But even if some grievous things were threatening, they ought in no way to separate us from Christ. Why should we not also endure hard and bitter things for him who for us took upon himself things so unworthy of him? And therefore there ought to be love in us, so that no dangers call us back from Christ. For it is written: much water will not be able to shut out love, and rivers will not close in upon it, because the soul of one who loves passes over the torrent. No storm, no deep danger, no terror of death or of punishment lessens the strength of love; for in these things we are proved, in these is the blessed life, even if it be flooded with many dangers.

28. For the wise man is not broken by the pains of the body nor troubled by misfortunes, but even in hardships he remains blessed. For bodily adversities neither lessen the gift of the blessed life nor take away anything of its sweetness, because the blessedness of life is not in the pleasure of the body, but in a conscience pure from every stain of sin, and in the mind of him who knows that what is good delights, even if it is harsh, while what is unseemly, even if it is sweet, does not soothe. Therefore the cause of living well is not bodily pleasure, but the prudence of the mind. For it is not the flesh, which is subject to passion, but the mind, which judges, because nothing delights more than honourable counsels and beautiful works; the mind, then, is the interpreter of the blessed life. For prudence, or reason, the arbiter of passion, is better than passion, and that which judges is more excellent than that which is subject to judgement. For it cannot be that what is irrational is better than reason. He, therefore, who follows Jesus has his reward in himself, and in his own disposition his prize and grace. Even if he endures hard things, he is nevertheless blessed in his character, blessed in the very dangers, as the Lord defined it, saying: blessed are those who suffer persecution for the sake of justice.

29. There is, then, a blessed life among men, but, to be sure, in those in whom the life has been perfect. Now the perfect life is not this life of the senses, but that rational life according to the reasoning of reason and the liveliness of the mind. In this there is not a portion of man but his perfection, which lies not so much in man's condition as in his activity; for it is this that makes him blessed. To this man, then, what is good except himself to himself? What he has, this good is both present to him and will be for him the cause of good things to come. Of this good Solomon said: drink water from your own vessels and from the springs of your wells. Let waters overflow for you from your own spring, and let them run about in your streets. Let them be established for you alone, and let no stranger share them with you. Let the spring of your water be your own. Use, then, your inner good.

30. Now the greatest testimony to this good is that he who has it does not seek other things. For what should he seek who despises lower things and clings to the most excellent, as it is written: let the stag of friendship and the foal of graces converse with you? And let friendship go before you and be one with you at every time; for good is the friendship of the virtues and the love of the highest good. Therefore that perfect man seeks nothing else but the sole and glorious good. Hence he also says: one thing I have asked of the Lord, this will I seek, that I may dwell in the house of the Lord all the days of my life and see the delight of the Lord. Nor should you disdain him as narrow and needy because he is content with the solitary company, as it were, of one good. For surrounded by its friendship he will be manifold. For it is abundantly sufficient to such a man for blessedness and for the possession of the good, and therefore he desires nothing else. For he who has all things seeks nothing as if it were new. For there is nothing of the good that he does not have, nor does he delight in superfluous things, but in necessary ones, and in that very necessity only in so far as it is necessary not to himself but to the flesh that clings to him; and he grants it what does not disagree with the purpose of the inner man, so that he may make both one and reconcile to God the inner man and the outer alike, so that in both there may be one spirit.

31. A man of this purpose, then, is neither diminished by losses, nor broken by adversities, nor held back by barriers, nor saddened by the loss of his own. For his instruction the Apostle says: we do not want you to be ignorant, brothers, about those who are asleep, so that you may not be sad, as also the others who have no hope. For he consoles himself with faith in the resurrection and with the grace of the reward to come, and he does not admit sadness into that mind by which he clings to God, and he rejoices in the delight of the divine presence. But he who is saddened by these things is not saddened according to God, and what is not according to God is full of folly. Far from the perfect man, then, is the sadness of this world, which is not according to God, and every anxiety over bodily hardship. For to such a disposition of mind and being the condition of the body and a certain use of outward nature are not reckoned, since an unconquered soul ought to keep a uniform constancy against the delights of the body and at the same time against the very frailty of nature, bereavements, losses, insults, so that he who desires to hold the palm of blessedness should cut away the body itself and strip off the senses of the flesh, for the fruit of blessedness lies not in the portion of a single virtue but in the fellowship of very many and, if it can be, of all the virtues.

32. The perfect man, therefore, does not know the troubles of this body or the adversities of the world, nor does he feel them, since he bears a soul estranged from fear of this kind, the fear that they may happen. For a life is not defined as more perfect if it is free of these things, but if it despises them. For if it were defined in such a way that the blessed life were that which could be found exempt and free from misfortunes of this kind, then certainly, when these occur, no one could be defined as blessed. These things, then, have been set aside, and this alone has been required in assessing the blessed life, that its definition should be held not in other things but in the possession of the true and the good, since he who has this spurns the rest and does not seek them.

Chapter VIII.

33. I now ask whether the wise man takes delight in the health of the body. We cannot deny that he takes delight in it according to nature, and that he would more gladly accept feeling no pain than feeling pain, unless perhaps for Christ, for whom, if the cause demanded it, he would readily accept even weakness of the body and offer his whole body to death. Yet the same man, even apart from the cause of faith and justice, if health were lacking, would not be affected in his soul nor broken by the pain of the body, since he can console himself with the perfection of the virtues.

34. I ask also whether he takes delight in children? Who would deny it? For it is not a hard man of iron that we are seeking, but a perfect one. Yet even if he loses his children, he is not therefore less blessed, since he is not therefore less perfect; for what is perfect is blessed. Indeed, a man of this kind is usually seen to be more perfect if he endures adversities than if he abounds in prosperity, although neither when outward advantages or the joys of the body are lacking nor when they are present is anything wont to be taken from virtue or added to it; but for most men it has been as much to their praise to have endured adversities bravely as not to have fallen into them. But these things are valued by appearance, not by weight:

35. this is profound: that the just man wills nothing but that sole and glorious good, strives toward it alone, counts this one thing among goods, desires always not something else with it but it alone, and takes delight in it. If something else that delights him is added to him, such as the sweetness of children, the former is not lost, but the latter is added. For accidents do not lessen blessedness, since they cannot increase it, because that remains full and inviolable which the soul has put on, into which it has inserted itself and poured itself. Perfect virtue remains always, among adversities and delights alike, and neither do adversities take anything from its perfection nor do delights add anything to its perfection. For what reason is there that he who has ascended to the summit should feel either loss or gain from perishable things?

36. Nor indeed, though most people hold it to be the most grievous thing, will he think himself wretched if either he himself or his children come into captivity; for he will not bear as intolerable what belongs to nature or what seems good to the Lord. Indeed, the just man said: good is the word which the Lord has spoken, and he said: let there be peace and faith in my days. Certainly this just man Hezekiah was not glad that the hardship of captivity had been turned aside onto his sons, but he could not oppose the will of the Lord, and therefore he accepted his commands with equanimity, like a humble slave. Add to this that he could reckon that the merit of virtue can stand out even in captivity. For Jeremiah was not less blessed in captivity, nor Daniel, nor Ezra, nor were Hananiah, Azariah, Mishael less blessed than if they had not fallen into captivity, since they were led into captivity for this purpose, that they might bring to the people both comforts present in captivity and the hope of escaping captivity. For it belongs to the perfect man to sustain the common lot of nature by the virtue of the soul and to lead it on to better things, and not to succumb to those things that seem terrible and fearful to most people, but like a brave soldier to sustain the onslaughts of the most grievous misfortunes and to undergo conflicts, and like a provident helmsman to steer the ship in a storm and, by meeting the rising waves, to avoid shipwreck by cleaving the waves rather than by turning aside from them. Such a man is not fearful in persecution, not softer under torments for fear of exasperating the torturer, but like a strong athlete who strikes back at the one who beats him, if not with the lash of blows, certainly with the lash of words, who despises the torments that many must fear, saying: the arrows of children have become their wounds, who, though he wrestles with the most grievous pain, does not show himself pitiable, but shows, like a light in a lantern, that even amid rough tempests and the most violent blasts the virtue of his soul shines and cannot be extinguished. Such a man is not soft at the injuries done to his own, nor anxious about the tomb of his body, since he knows that heaven is owed to him; he is not more downcast in the captivity of his fellow citizens, but like a stern judge he condemns the unbelief and the errors of the unbelievers, as Daniel did, who exposed the thefts of the priests and refuted their superstitions, showing that they rested on no truth but were shadowed over by frauds. Such, finally, is the perfect man, who would wish all to do well and no evil to befall anyone, and, if it should befall against his will, he himself nevertheless loses nothing of his own blessedness.

37. But perhaps someone may think that sickness and weakness of the body are an obstacle to fulfilling the task of perfection, because no works or deeds of the hands can go forward. But the just man will not feel these things to be an obstacle to him; rather, he will even rebuke the one who pitiably bewails them and accuse him as if he were slothful, because he sets more store by the use of the body than by the virtue of the soul, because he longs for the things with which he might serve, when he has rather what enables him to give orders to others, because he groans in poverty, he who could be above the riches of the world — for to the faithful man the whole world of riches belongs — because he bewails low birth, he who ought to despise royal powers and to command the rich and the powerful. For this is the life of the just man, who ought to regard even the resources he has as common, nay rather to divide them among the needy, to distribute them to the poor, to cut back his own pleasures, to reduce his expense, to apply the thrift of temperance, to keep sobriety in prosperity, patience in adversity, endurance in pain, greatness of soul in dangers, to know nothing of prayers for perpetual health, not to be shaken by the terror of impending death, nor to think him more excellent on whom, according to nature, children, kinsfolk, health, gladness, abundance have overflowed than him who has lacked them, and to weigh merits not by the outward things of the world but by the inward goods of virtue.

38. Who would deny that this too is the pattern of the just man: that he fears nothing, dreads nothing except the loss of virtue, and checks the vain fears of others, which they have from anxiety over dangers, from fear of death, from weakness of the body; that he teaches that to be released from the body and to be with Christ is far better; that he shows that activities are not hindered by weaknesses of the body but increased? Nor is he commended by the splendour of his family or the support of his kinsfolk or by riches, but by a good disposition. For Elijah was not less blessed than Moses, since the one, in need of food, in a cheap sheepskin, without children, without means, without a companion, and the other, leader of peoples, glad in his offspring, girded with power, laid the foundation of equal merit in different ways, as was declared in the Gospel when they shone with the Lord Jesus in the glory of the resurrection; for he seems to have given an equal reward to them as to equal witnesses of his glory. Nor was Elisha less blessed than David, since the one, subject to kings, and the other, endowed with royal power, obtained a not unequal grace of prophetic sanctification.

39. For what is lacking to him who possesses that good and has virtue always as his companion and partner? In what state is he not most powerful? In what poverty is he not rich? In what lowliness of birth is he not illustrious? In what leisure is he not hard at work? In what weakness is he not vigorous? In what infirmity is he not strong? In what quiet of sleep is he idle, he whom his own virtue does not leave even when he rests? In what solitude is he not thronged, he whom the blessed life surrounds, whom grace clothes, whom the mantle of glory makes illustrious? For he is not less blessed at leisure than when he works, nor less glorious asleep than awake, because he is not less safe and sound asleep than when he is awake. But when can he seem to be idle, whose mind is always at work? And when can he be alone, who is always with that good of which the prophet says: we shall be filled with the good things of your house? When cast down, whose citizenship is in heaven? When not comely, who conforms himself to the likeness of that comeliness and of the sole good, who, even if he is loosened in his limbs, yet raises himself up in mind? And just as one who was accustomed to sing to the lyre, if he sees it broken apart, its strings loosened, shattered and its use cut off, casts it aside and does not seek its measures but soothes himself with his own voice, so also this man will allow the lyre of his body to lie idle, will delight himself in his heart, will soothe himself with the remembrance of a good conscience, will lift himself up with divine oracles and prophetic writings, holding in his soul and embracing in his mind that sweet and pleasant good, he to whom nothing sad can happen, since the grace of the divine presence always breathes upon him and he is present to himself, steeped in the highest tranquillity of soul.

Book Two

Chapter I.

1. In the preceding book we discussed the precepts of the virtues; in the following let us use the examples of illustrious men who, placed in the greatest dangers, did not lose the blessedness of life but rather acquired it. Was not Jacob blessed even when he was leaving his homeland? Nay, plainly blessed, he who took upon himself the hardships of exile in order to soften his brother's anger. For if he is blessed who turns aside from sin, certainly he cannot be denied to be blessed who lightens another's guilt and averts a crime. And so by a voluntary exile he turned aside the fratricide that was prepared, and by that deed he sought safety for himself and bestowed innocence on his brother. Deservedly, therefore, divine grace accompanied him everywhere, so that even when he slept he acquired the gift of the blessed life. For he saw the mysteries of things to come and heard divine oracles. He was a good labourer in his sleep and rich in poverty, he who by the task of a hired man's life procured both an inheritance and a marriage by one and the same service. The same man, an excellent reconciler of brotherly affection, strove with gifts and attentions to shut out all indignation and to turn away the pain of offence, showing that he had not been made less by exile, since he could bestow what he had not received.

2. But I am hastening too quickly to what comes later and running past useful things, since it must first be defined what "blessed" is. For it is written: blessed is the man who has not gone in the counsel of the ungodly, and has not stood in the way of sinners, and has not sat in the chair of pestilence, Scripture signifying by this that he is blessed who has either kept himself from the fellowship of unbelievers — for this is ungodliness, not to acknowledge the author of life and a kind of parent of salvation — or has not remained in sin, or has not persisted in luxury and wantonness; and that he too who meditates on the law of the Lord day and night will be like a tree that will yield its fruit in its season. The former are the merits of the rewards; this is the reward of the merits.

3. Which of these things that pertain to the merits of blessedness was lacking to holy Jacob, who was so far from the fellowship of the ungodly that from him the faithful people received its name, he being called Israel because he beheld God with the eyes of the inner mind; who abstained from sin, was sober from the drunkenness of all luxury, so that he drank down the hardships of labours and disregarded the ease of leisure? Was it not beautifully and truly said of him that he too will yield his fruit in its season, he of whom it is written: behold, the smell of my son is like the smell of a full field? For he was perfect in every flower of the virtues and was fragrant with the grace of the sacred blessing and of heavenly blessedness. For he himself is the field which the Lord has blessed, not this earthly field, either bristling with woods, or broken by torrents, or marshy with sluggish waters, or barren of grain, or useless for vines, stony with gravel still unfruitful, or gaping and parched with drought, or soaked with blood, or left untilled with briars and thorns, but that field of which the Church says in the Song of Songs: I have adjured you, daughters of Jerusalem, by the powers and the strengths of the field. For this is the field of which the Lord also says: and the beauty of the field is with me. In this field is found that grape which, pressed out, poured forth blood and washed the world; in this field is that fig tree under which the saints will rest, refreshed by the sweetness of spiritual grace; in this field is that fruitful olive tree flowing with the ointment of the Lord's peace; in this field blossom the pomegranates, which cover very many fruits with the one defence of faith and cherish them in a kind of embrace of love.

4. With these fruits, then, Jacob was fragrant, he who followed God through dangers and believed himself safe everywhere under his guidance. For although the smell of a field is sweet and pleasant, because it is the smell of nature, yet in the holy patriarch it was the grace of the virtues that breathed. How continent he was in his thrift, he who did not seek the food prepared for himself but yielded it without delay to his brother when he asked for it. From him he received the primacy of the blessing: how devoted he was toward his parents, so that he deserved to be preferred to his elder brother by his mother's affection and to be consecrated by the gift of his father's blessing; how scrupulous, that he refused to harm his brother; how modest, that he feared to deceive his father; how respectful, that he could not refuse his mother what he was bidden!

Chapter II.

5. But neither must we leave the parents unexcused for having preferred the younger son to the elder. At the same time we must take care that no one, while he looks to their example, makes an unjust judgement among his children, so as to think that one is to be loved and the other set aside. For from this fraternal hatreds are stirred up, and over the increase of paltry money a fratricidal crime is plotted. Let the same measure of love cherish one's offspring. Granted, however, that affection may seize something more for itself toward the more winning or the more like, the pattern of justice ought to be equal toward all. More is bestowed on a beloved child when the love of his brothers is sought for him; but more is taken away from him who is burdened with the envy of an unjust preference. Esau threatened to kill his brother, and neither brotherly kinship nor reverence for his parents called him back from fratricidal fury, and he grieved that the blessing had been snatched from him, though he certainly ought to have proved himself worthy of it by gentleness, not by crime.

6. But Rebekah too was preferring not son to son, but, as it were, the just to the unjust. For with a devoted mother the mystery outweighed her child: she was not so much preferring him to his brother as offering him to the Lord, knowing that he could keep the gift conferred on him; and in this she was also taking thought for the other, whom she was withdrawing from divine offence, so that he would not be entangled in heavier guilt if he lost the grace of a blessing once received.

7. Consider, however, a good contest between parents. Let the mother bestow affection, the father judgement. Let the mother incline with tender love toward the younger, let the father keep toward the elder the honour that nature gives. Let him honour more, let her love more. While each cherishes one, let them not both conspire toward one and defraud the other. Let there be equality amid their differing contests, and by unequal zeal let equal favour be bestowed on each through the equal love of the parents. Let the one make up what the other has lessened. Thus with devoted affection the patriarch Isaac and holy Rebekah contended so as to make neither inferior, but both equal.

8. Yet he won who was preferred by the oracle; promptness won over slowness, gentleness over harshness. While the other seeks wild prey in the rough hunt, this one served his loving father the food of gentle manners, this one a homely grace, this one the sweet dishes of tender gentleness and love. Whatever comes readily to hand pleases the mind more than whatever you might think should be offered with affectation. Jacob went to the sheep and brought the offspring of innocence, or the gifts of sacred prophecy, because he believed that no food was sweeter to the patriarch than Christ, who was led like a sheep to slaughter and like a lamb to the sacrifice. He judged this food useful both for the common father and for the people, whose type he bore, since by it there was to be the remission of sins.

9. Therefore he received the robe of his brother, because he excelled in the wisdom of age; therefore the younger brother stripped the elder brother, because he shone forth in the dignity of faith. This robe Rebekah, as a type of the Church, brought out and gave to her younger son: the robe of the Old Testament, the prophetic and priestly robe, that royal robe of David, the robe of Solomon, of the kings Hezekiah and Josiah; and she gave it to the Christian people, who would know how to use the garment they had received, since the Jewish people had it without using it and did not know their own adornments. This robe lay in the shadow, laid by and neglected; for it was obscured by the dark gloom of ungodliness and could not be unfolded more widely in the narrow heart of the people. The Christian people put it on, and it shone. They illumined it with the brightness of their faith and the light of devout deeds. Isaac knew the familiar smell of his own race, he recognized the robe of the ancient Scripture, but he did not recognize the voice of the ancient people, and therefore he knew that it had been changed. For the same robe remains even today, but the confession of a more devoted people has begun to ring with song, and rightly he said: the voice indeed is the voice of Jacob, but the hands are the hands of Esau. And he smelled the smell of his garments. And perhaps this is because we are justified not by works but by faith, since the weakness of the flesh is an obstacle to works, but the brightness of faith, which merits the pardon of offences, overshadows the error of deeds.

Chapter III.

10. After the blessing had been solemnly given, the elder brother arrived. By this it is declared that the kingdom was conferred in predestination on the Church before the synagogue, but that the synagogue came in, so that sin might superabound and, when sin had superabounded, grace too might superabound; at the same time it becomes clear that the candidate for the heavenly kingdom ought to be prompt, so as to snatch the blessing beforehand and lay claim to the privilege of his own commendation. Hence the younger son was not censured by his father but praised, for Isaac says: your brother came with guile and took your blessing. For guile is good where the seizing is blameless; and blameless is the seizing done by devotion, because from the days of John the kingdom of heaven is taken by force, and those who use force seize it; for the fathers celebrated the Passover in haste, they who ate the lamb in haste, not lingering, and holy Joseph by the device of a devoted deceit summoned his brother Benjamin and kept him.

11. And yet the other too by force drew out and obtained a blessing for himself, but such a blessing as agreed and accorded with the former one, namely that he should serve his brother. For he who could not command and rule another had to serve, so that he might be ruled by one more prudent. For it was not the part of the holy patriarch to bind over his son to the degrading condition of slavery; but as a good father, having two sons, one intemperate, the other prudent and sober, in order to take thought for both, he set the sober one over the intemperate and appointed the foolish one to obey the prudent, because the foolish man cannot be a willing disciple of virtue nor persevere in study, since the fool changes like the moon; and rightly he denied him the freedom of his own choice, lest like a ship in the waves without a helmsman he should drift about, but he subjected him to his brother, according to what is written: the imprudent man indeed serves the prudent. Rightly, then, he subjected him, so that under the rule of the one governing him he might improve his own disposition. And therefore he says: you will live by your sword and you will serve your brother; for devotion is the mistress of cruelty, and gentleness surpasses harsh impulses.

12. Everyone serves who does not have the authority of a pure conscience; whoever is either broken by fear, or ensnared by pleasure, or led by desires, or exasperated by indignation, or cast down by grief, serves. For every passion is slavish, since he who commits sin is the slave of sin, and, what is worse, he who is subject to vices is the slave of many. He has bound himself over to many masters, so that he is scarcely allowed to get out of slavery. But he who is the arbiter of his own will, the judge of his counsel, the interpreter of his choice, who restrains the appetite of bodily passion, who does well what he does — and acting well he acts rightly, and he who acts rightly acts without fault and blamelessly, having power over his own acts — he surely is free. For he who does all things prudently and as he wills, he alone is free. It is not a chance condition that makes a slave, but shameful folly. Indeed, a prudent servant rules foolish masters, and their own slaves will lend to masters. What will they lend? Not money, certainly, but wisdom, as the Law also says: you will lend to many nations, but you will not borrow. For the Jew lent to the proselyte the oracles of the divine Law; but because he himself could not see the mysteries of the Law and did not know the oracles he had, he who lent the letter to the Gentiles now borrows from them the grace of spiritual teaching, and rightly he has been subjected to slavery, since he who borrows is a slave, as if bound over to the interest of his creditor, while he who imparts the interest of devout teaching is a prince, as the Law says: you will be prince of many nations, but there will be no princes over you from among them. For he is a prince who rules and who holds the principate of wisdom. The Jewish people held it, but since it could not keep what it taught, it must learn what it did not know how to teach.

13. This, then, is what the patriarch Isaac says: you will serve your brother. But it will be when you have put down and loosed his yoke from your neck, signifying that there would be two peoples, one the son of the bondwoman, the other of the free woman — for the letter serves, grace is free — and that the people which attends to the letter would be a slave so long as it follows the interpreter of spiritual teaching. Then too would come about what the Apostle says: that a remnant may be saved according to the election of grace. You will serve your brother, therefore, but you will feel the benefit of slavery when you begin to obey your brother willingly rather than under compulsion.

Chapter IV.

14. Hence envy arose, and Esau threatened that he would kill his brother after their father's death. But if that should happen, let us learn from Rebekah how we must take precautions, so that envy may not stir up anger, nor anger rush headlong into fratricide. Let Rebekah come, that is, let patience be put on, the good guardian of innocence; let her persuade us to give place to wrath. Let us withdraw somewhat further off, until indignation is softened by time and forgetfulness of the offence steals upon it. And so patience does not dread even exile, but undertakes it readily, not so much that danger to one's life as that an incitement to crime may be avoided. The loving mother too endures that her most beloved son be absent from her, meaning to bestow the more on the one whom she hurt, yet taking thought for both, so that she might keep the one free from fratricide and the other unharmed by the crime.

15. We have heard what intemperance, drunk with bodily desires, has said; let us consider what true virtue does. It seeks nothing but the grace of God; it follows that one and supreme good alone, it is content with that alone, from which we receive all things, while we ourselves confer nothing on it, because it needs nothing, as David says: I said to my Lord: You are my Lord, for you have no need of my goods. For what does he need who abounds in all things and bestows everything on us, and supplies all things without failing?

16. And Jacob set out and slept, which is a sign of a quiet soul, and he saw the angels of God ascending and descending. That is, he foresaw Christ on earth, to whom the throng of angels descends and ascends, to render homage to its own Lord with devout service.

17. And he came to the well, so that he might drink from his own vessels and from the fountains of his own wells, and that waters might overflow for him from his own fountain; for the fountain of life is in the hands of the just man.

18. And he came to Laban and fed his sheep. Wickedness sits idle and unattended; wisdom does not neglect the duty of ruling. It does not know how to be idle even in what belongs to others, it does not know how to be an exile among strangers. For how is it an exile, when everywhere it keeps its own right and has in itself what it possesses?

19. The just man enters as a hireling and is a ruler, he who was gathering for himself, through the ministry of the preaching of the Gospel, a flock shining with the splendour of many and excellent virtues, so that for the sheep that were to drink he set out in the channels a rod of storax and of nut-wood and of plane, in order that, desiring the prefigured mysteries of the most blessed Trinity, they might form offspring by no means discoloured through the conception of a devout mind. Good sheep, which have brought forth offspring of good works not unworthy of the sacred faith! By the storax is signified the incense and the evening sacrifice which is offered to God the Father in the psalm. By the rod of nut-wood the priestly gift is offered to Christ; for this is the rod of Aaron, which, when it was laid up, blossomed, by which the priestly grace of sanctification shone forth. By the plane is signified the richness of spiritual fruit, because the vine is joined to this tree, so that, glad of its fellowship, it may pour itself forth in rich offspring, since the grace of the Spirit, joined to them, has been accustomed to cherish the gifts of the Lord's passion and the remission of all sins.

Chapter V.

20. By these means, then, as Scripture says, he became very rich, rearing a good flock for Christ, which he ennobled with the title of faith and the variety of the virtues, as with the insignia of a glorious name. And so, rich in the wealth of faith, he did not seem to himself straitened, and he was made an object of envy to the sons of Laban as very wealthy, because he had heaped up his own flock by the addition of another's flock. And God said to him: Return to the land of your father and to your people, and I will be with you, showing that nothing was lacking to him to whom the fullness of all things was present; that this alone abounds for the perfect man, that in this all things consist and all things are referred to it, that nothing was new to him, nothing superfluous was necessary to him, who had a faithful peace at hand, through which he might reconcile what had at first been at variance. Nor is it surprising if he had peace who had set up a pillar and anointed it to God, which is the Church. For she is called the pillar and foundation of truth. He anoints it who pours out on Christ the ointment of faith, on the poor the ointment of mercy.

21. Now let us consider what the just man ought to be like if envy has arisen. First, that he avoid it; for it is better to go away without a quarrel than to stay on amid wrangling. Next, that he possess such things as he can carry away with him, so that in nothing can he be held liable by his adversary, but may say: Recognize whether anything of yours is with me. And Laban searched and found nothing of his with Jacob. A great man and truly blessed, who could lose nothing of his own, have nothing of another's, that is, have nothing too little, nothing superfluous. And so he is perfect to whom nothing is lacking, and just, to whom nothing is over; for this is to hold the measure of justice. How great a virtue, whose fellowship brought gain and inflicted no loss! This is to be perfect: to give very much advantage to those who cling to one, and to bring no disadvantage.

22. In short, he who desired to do him harm could not send him away empty. For the wise man is never empty, always having within himself the cloak of prudence, being one who can say: I put on justice and clothed myself with judgment, as Job said. For these are the inner coverings of the mind, which no one else could take away, except when a man's own guilt strips him. In short, Adam, stripped in this way, was found naked; but Joseph, even when his outer garment was thrown off, was not naked, for he kept the garments of virtue safe. The wise man, therefore, is never empty. For how is he empty who has received of the fullness of Christ and keeps what he received? How is he empty whose soul is filled, the soul that guards the garments of the grace it received? This is what must be feared: that someone lose the covering of innocence, that the impious, overstepping the bounds of justice in the onset of a sacrilegious persecution, snatch away the garment of soul and mind. This does not easily happen, unless the voice of his own iniquity has first stripped a man. Hence David too says: If there is iniquity in my hands, may I deservedly fall empty before my enemies: let the enemy pursue my soul and seize it.

23. None of your enemies, then, can seize your soul unless it has first been made empty. Do not fear, therefore, those who can plunder treasures of gold and silver. They take nothing from you. For they take away what you did not have, they take away what you could not possess, they take away what did not adorn your soul but burdened it, they take away what did not enrich your heart but rather weighed it down; for where your treasure is, there will your heart be also, as you heard read today. Many shut up their gold behind the bolts of their doors, but these do not trust even their bars and locks; many employ watchmen, but they too are wont to fear the guards themselves more; many brood over buried gold: their gold is under the earth and their heart is under the earth. Beware, then, lest you too bury your heart in the earth while you are alive. It is not, then, the thieves of that gold who are to be feared; it is that money-lender you must beware of, who searches the substance of your soul to see whether you have contracted any debts of graver sin, who shuts your heart up in the ground, who overwhelms your soul with the same turf with which you covered your gold, who bows your mind down with the usury of the hundredth and buries it in a heavy sepulchre, from which no one rises again. Follow holy Jacob, who neither had anything of others' vices nor was empty and void of his own virtues, who was filled with the fruit of justice.

24. But these things belong to the moral sense; this is the mystical: that Laban, that is, "whitened", came to him, because Satan too transforms himself into an angel of light, and began to seek his own things from him. Jacob answered him: Recognize whether anything is yours with me, that is: I have nothing of yours. Search, if you recognize anything of your vices and crimes. I have taken nothing of your frauds away with me, and I have no partnership at all in deceit; I have fled from everything of yours as from a contagion. And Laban searched and found nothing of his. How blessed the man in whom the enemy found nothing that he could call his own, in whom the devil came upon nothing that he would recognize as his! That seemed impossible in a man, but he bore the type of him who said in the Gospel: The prince of this world will come and will find nothing in me. For whatever is the devil's is nothing, since it can have no permanence and no substance.

25. But the one who was prefigured in Jacob is the Lord Jesus himself, the husband of two marriages, that is, a kind of partner of the Law and of grace, who loved the virgin Rachel first and loved her, predestined to be his in marriage, with a devout affection. But since Leah, like the Law, came in besides, and, weaker in her eyes, crept in like the synagogue, which in its blindness of mind could not see Christ, the grace of holy Rachel superabounded, who was sought beyond that first marriage, who even then by the meaning of her name signified the future primacy of the Church: blessed Rachel, who took away reproach by her childbearing; blessed Rachel, who hid the worship and the errors of the Gentiles, who declared their idols to be full of uncleanness. Let no one think that the reverence of devotion due to a father was violated because she sat while her father stood, since it is written: He who has made more of father or mother than of me is not worthy of me. Where the cause of religion was being pleaded, faith had to hold the seat of judgment, and unbelief to stand as if accused.

Chapter VI.

26. These things were approved to such a degree that the angels of God came to meet holy Jacob as he set out. In short, he saw the camp of God drawing near and said: This is the camp of God; for divine protection is wont to be at hand for the perfect and the faithful. But the perfect man was thinking of reconciliation with his brother, so as to invite him by humility, win him by kind services, and he thought that he should also buy him with gifts. And so he went to meet his brother with his wives and his children, so that, even if Esau was indignant with Jacob himself, he might be swayed by the courtesies of kinship.

27. And he worshipped seven times on the ground. What does this mean? The Law says: You shall worship the Lord your God and serve him alone, and here he worships an intemperate man, an angry man, one threatening the fratricidal outrage. Or did he worship that earth, clotted with human blood, steeped in the poisons of serpents, or wretched with starved gravel, or bristling with hard and rough rocks? What, too, does it mean that he worshipped seven times? The answer would stick fast, were it not that this comes to mind: that when Peter asked in the Gospel: If my brother sins against me, how often shall I forgive him? Up to seven times? the Lord Jesus answered: Not only seven times, but even seventy times seven. This, then, is what the holy patriarch signifies in the prophetic spirit, looking toward him who was to come, who would command that pardon be granted to a brother not only up to seven times but even up to seventy times seven, so that Esau, approached out of regard for him, might remit to his brother the injury which he thought he had received, and, though wronged, might return to friendship, because the Lord Jesus was to take on flesh and come to earth for this very reason, that he might give us a manifold pardon of our offences.

28. In short, when he was about to seek concord with his brother, he slept in the camp. Perfect virtue has the tranquillity and stability of rest; therefore the Lord reserved its gift for the more perfect, saying: My peace I leave you, my peace I give you. For it belongs to the perfect not to be easily moved by worldly things, not to be disturbed by fear, not to be agitated by suspicion, not to be shaken by terror, not to be troubled by pain, but, as if on a most safe shore, to keep the mind unmoved and calm in a faithful anchorage against the rising waves of the storms of the world. This firm support Christ brought into Christian minds, bringing inner peace into the souls of the approved, so that our heart may not be troubled nor our soul agitated. That this peace is above every mind the Apostle, our teacher, asserted, saying: And the peace of God, which surpasses every mind, will guard your hearts and your thoughts in Christ Jesus. And so the fruit of peace is not to be disturbed in one's breast. In short, the life of the just man is quiet, but the unjust man is full of disquiet and disturbance. And so the impious man is afflicted more by his own suspicions than most men are by the blows of others, and the weals of the wounds are greater in his soul than in the bodies of those who are beaten by others.

29. It is a great thing for someone to be tranquil within himself and at one with himself. Outward peace is sought either by the anxious foresight of an emperor or by the hand of soldiers, or it comes from the prosperous outcome of wars or from some slaughter of barbarians, if in a hostile commotion they turn their arms against themselves. In that peace there is no virtue of ours, but only an outcome. Certainly the glory of that peace is referred to the emperor; the fruit of this peace is in us, the peace which is in our minds and is held in the dispositions of each one. Greater is the fruit of this peace, by which the temptations of spiritual wickedness are repelled, than that by which hostile arms are repelled. This peace, which shuts out the allurements of the bodily passions and calms disturbances, is more sublime than that which quells the incursions of barbarians; for it is more to resist an enemy shut up within you than one far away.

Chapter VII.

30. And so Jacob, who had cleansed his heart of all rancour and bore a peaceable disposition, after he had sent away all that was his, remained alone and wrestled with God. For whoever disregards worldly things draws nearer to the image and likeness of God. For what is it to wrestle with God, but to take up the contest of virtue, to engage with one who is stronger, and to become an imitator of God beyond the rest? And because his faith and devotion were invincible, the Lord revealed hidden mysteries to him, touching the breadth of his thigh, because from his line the Lord Jesus was to arise, born of a virgin, who would be neither unequal nor inferior to God. The breadth of the thigh, growing numb, signified his cross, because, spread through the whole world by the remission of sin, it would bring salvation to all, he who by the numbness and sleep of his own body would grant the resurrection of the dead. Hence not undeservedly did the sun rise for holy Jacob, upon whose race the saving cross of the Lord shone, and at the same time because the sun of justice rises for him who sees God, since he himself is the eternal light.

31. But Jacob limped on his thigh. For this reason the sons of Israel do not eat the sinew even to this day. Would that they had eaten and believed! But because they were not going to do the will of God, therefore they did not eat. There are also those who take it in this way: that Jacob limped on one thigh for this reason, because, of the two peoples springing from his line, the numbness of one with regard to the grace of faith was already then declared as soon to come. That, then, is the people which limped through the numbness of unbelief.

32. In short, not long after a revelation of this kind, when Dinah, Jacob's daughter, had been violated by the son of a foreigner, the modesty of her virginity deflowered, her brothers, who did not understand the mystery, out of zeal for vengeance killed the foreigners, who were offering a fellowship of faith through the union of their families; but Jacob, who in moral gentleness loved clemency, or in the mystical spirit foresaw the mystery of the Church to be gathered from the Gentiles, received that scene of exacted revenge unwillingly and with grief. Hence to him, as he prophesied the coming of the Lord Jesus, the divine answer was given: Arise, go up to the place Bethel, that is, into the house of bread, where Christ was born, as Micah the prophet testifies, saying: And you, Bethlehem, house of Ephrathah, you are not the least, that you should be among the princes of Judah. For from you shall come forth a prince in Israel, and his going forth is from the beginning, from the days of the world. Truly the house of bread, which is the house of Christ, who came to us from heaven as the bread of salvation, so that now no one hungers who gains for himself the food of immortality. There the patriarch is commanded to dwell, there to make an altar to God who appeared to him. There he received the foreign gods and hid them under the lentisk; there too Rachel was buried, on the way, it says, to Ephrathah; this is Bethlehem. There also Jacob set up a pillar upon her tomb.

33. How great are the mysteries, since there is the Church of God, in which God appears and speaks with his humble servants! There the idols of the Gentiles are snatched away and hidden; for the faith of the Church has abolished every observance of paganism. But I ask why he hid them under the lentisk. Certainly that kind of tree is unfruitful. There, then, are the gods of the Gentiles, where there is no fruit. There the earrings of the Gentiles are buried, which they gave to Jacob, so that they may now grow accustomed to hear a new language and know nothing of the old sound of unbelief, that their ears may grow deaf to sacrilege and be cleansed for grace. Not undeservedly did holy Daniel there detect the false testimony against Susanna. For there unbelief still wished to set its roots, but it could not lie hidden, because it was detected by the prophetic spirit. And the error of the elder was in keeping with the true confession, in that he said chastity had been defiled there where the idols of the Gentiles were buried; but the truth of the Church did not cover over unbelief: it hid it away, and stopped up the ears of paganism.

34. Fittingly too holy Rachel was buried there, because all who are baptized in Christ are buried together with him. For so we are taught, as the Apostle says: For we have been buried together with him through baptism into death, so that, as he rose from death, raised by his own power, so we too may rise again by his grace. Then, therefore, is every error of the Gentiles truly hidden, when someone has been washed clean of vices, because our old man, nailed to the cross, no longer knows how to serve the old sin. Suitably too a pillar is set up upon Rachel's tomb, because the Church is the pillar and foundation of truth.

Chapter VIII.

35. Now Jacob grew old, and he had already grown old in character long before; but in him the tireless liveliness of youth and the tranquillity of old age contended together. For there is both an old age green with grace and a youth grey with counsels, of which Scripture says: for old age is venerable, and the age of old age is a spotless life. Of this life was Jacob, who by good works anticipated the time of long old age, so that he might both pluck its fruit before its time and not fear its last days when their time came. Blessed indeed is the young man too who lives well, but blessed also is the old man who has lived well. For what the young man hopes for, the older man has attained; what the old man has been, this he who is young wishes to be. Surely the one, with a longer course still remaining before him, is tossed by the waves of a restless sea, but the old man is in the anchorage of old age as in a harbour. Jacob, then, was such a man, who held, already locked and sealed with the safe and secure key of grace, goods that young men can scarcely hope for. But what you possess is better than what you still hope for. Jacob, therefore, was an old man to whom young men, struck by the adverse storm of some season, betook themselves as into a harbour; while he himself, placed as on a watchtower of this life, kept watch before them with anxious affection, and long beforehand foresaw remedies for uncertain affairs.

36. In fact, there was a famine in all the land: the old man, laid low, whether he was the first to hear what the eager young men did not know . . . . I shall see. Meanwhile he was the first to advise his sons that grain abounded in Egypt; they must set out there, to buy what would be necessary for their use. He even willingly acquiesced in the counsels of the young men, so as to send his younger son with them as well. And when they reported back that his son Joseph was alive, he, though broken in body, yet with lively strength of mind, did not wait for his son to come to him, but rather went himself to his son; for with love the measure of rank does not outweigh it. So he felt none of the hindrances of weary old age as he went; for love lightened the toil. But when, having enjoyed the fruit of his recovered son for some time, seventeen years, namely, which in view of the patriarch's great length of life is a time of small portion, he knew that the end of his life was drawing near, he called his son Joseph and bound him by the future heir of his line that he should not be buried in Egypt; and having received his pledge, when a little later he fell sick and his son Joseph had come to him, as if his strength had been restored he sat up on his bed and blessed the twelve patriarchs and prophesied.

Chapter IX.

37. Let some now say that Jacob was not blessed when he was in the very days of his death, he who held almost more converse with God than with men; not blessed when his eyes were heavy with old age and he could not see; for to some blindness seems to be a grievous hardship of misfortune. But even then Jacob was blessed, because he discerned by the spirit those whom he could not discern with his eyes and his sight. He saw things to come, he who was thought not to see things present. Indeed, Joseph himself erred: when he had placed his elder son at his right hand, and at the left hand of the same, that is, of Jacob, had placed his younger son, so that the order of age might keep the order of the blessing, Jacob stretched out his right hand over the younger grandson and his left over the elder grandson, and when his son wished to turn his father's right hand over onto Manasseh the elder, he answered: I know, son, I know: this one too will become a people, and this one will be exalted; but his younger brother will be greater than he. And he added the reason for the preference, saying: his seed will be a multitude of nations. So much better did he see, though the sight of his body was hindered, that he taught the one who could see that he had erred. For who sees better than he who sees Christ? Or who can say that he was hindered in his eyes who saw in Christ the Church shining forth? Is it not clear, then, that weakness cannot hinder blessedness? He, hindered in the service of his eyes, he, with the strength of his body worn out, leaving his body on his bed as in a tomb, rose up within himself, and, gathering himself far from others and within himself, had withdrawn himself from present things and was mingling with the future age of the last days. For so it is written: I will announce he says to you the things that will befall you in the last days.

38. What, then, was lacking to him, to whom God was present, who had said to him as he was about to set out: I will go down with you into Egypt, and I will lead you forever? Nor was God absent when the Holy Spirit spoke in him. Who is so powerful in his own dwelling as this man in another's? Who so abounding in plenty as this man in famine? Who so strong in youth as this man in old age? Who so active in business as this man in leisure? Who so swift on the racecourse as this man on his couch? Who so glad in the flower of youth as this man on the border of death? Who so rich in a kingdom as this man in a foreign place? Indeed, he blessed kings. And not without reason was he not poor, who lacked nothing. He was not poor who did not think himself poor. And who would call him poor of whose way of life the whole world was not worthy, and whose way of life was therefore in heaven? This man was truly most rich in the riches of simplicity and sincerity, comely with a greater beauty of soul than of body, a beauty that knows not how to fade, unwearied in age, one who could, when he wished, go out of this hovel of the body and pierce the heights of paradise by the vigour of his mind, exulting in spirit when he gave his last commands about his tomb. For he did not suppose that he was to be shut up in an earthly grave, but that he was to be received into a heavenly dwelling. And therefore he gave commands about the tomb as if for another, but, secure about himself, he thought that death to be immortality. He seemed entangled in the hindrance of the body, and yet with a watchful disposition he ran ahead into future times, saying of the persecutors of the Lord, who were to come forth from the tribe of Simeon and Levi as authors of wickedness: Let my soul not come into their counsel, and let my inward parts not strive to meet them. But who is so strong in power as this man was brave in weakness, who said: Cursed be their fury, because it is proud and rash, and their anger, because it is hardened. I will divide them in Jacob and scatter them in Israel?

39. But who is so tuneful in songs as this man in his utterances, who was heard by the whole world, who is heard through all peoples, through all ages? Who, sounding the different notes of the seven tones, was so sweet in his measures as this man, who resounded with the sevenfold grace of the Holy Spirit? Though loosened in his limbs, yet raising himself in soul and lifting himself up in spirit, he looked down with lofty mind on the harmony of his body, as of a lyre, destroyed now that the framework of his limbs was dissolved; he did not seek it again, but let it lie idle on the ground, while he himself soothed himself with an inward song and delighted himself with a prophetic melody, saying: Judah, may your brothers praise you. Your hands are upon the back of your enemies; the sons of your father will worship you. A lion's whelp is Judah: from the shoot you have gone up for me; lying down you have slept like a lion and as a lion's whelp. Who will rouse him? And below: He will wash his robe in wine and his mantle in the blood of the grape. His eyes are cheerful from wine, and his teeth whiter than milk. What song is sweeter, what sound more delightful, than the remission of sins and the resurrection of the dead? This song holy David, that instrument of the divine voice and interpreter of the Lord's word, sang on the spiritual lyre. With these measures of grace he soothed that lofty soul and mind. With this song he eased the rough things of this world, with this sound he softened the hard things of the world, with this psaltery he broke the terror of death, with this sweetness of strings he trampled on the underworld.

40. But let us hear also the other things which the holy patriarch Jacob sounded forth on that wonderful instrument of his mind: Naphtali is a vine spread out, putting forth beauty in its shoot. My son Joseph is increased, my son is increased, my son assailed with jealousy, my youngest son, return to me and below: He has prevailed over the blessings of the abiding mountains and the desires of the everlasting hills. What is sweeter than a blessing, what more pleasing than eternity? The words themselves are song, and in the words are the great rewards of prayers, the summits of merits.

41. What is sweeter than holy Joseph, who freed us from reproach by the mystery of the Lord's cross? For just as Christ was made a curse to loose the curse of the Law, and was made sin to take away the sin of the world, so he was made a reproach to take away the reproach of the Gentiles; but that reproach of Christ was esteemed more precious than the treasures of Egypt. And therefore Moses left the court of King Pharaoh and chose the reproach of faith, and for that reproach the seas divided before him. He, then, is Naphtali, a vine spread out through the whole world, to pour into all peoples the abundance of the spiritual cup; he is the one increased, having the name above every name, who, because he offered himself to death for all, therefore hears from the Father: return to me. Jacob spoke, and God was heard. He blessed, and the Lord sounded forth, saying to the Son: return to me, that is, return after the passion. Return to your seat, return with the trophy, return to me, so that the dead may follow you as you rise and may rise by your power and likewise by your example, so that you may become the firstborn from the dead, so that you may sit at the right hand of the Father. Hence the Son also said: From now on you will see the Son of Man sitting at the right hand of power.

42. Would anyone deny that Jacob was blessed at the very last moment of death, when he poured forth divine oracles? Would anyone deny that Joseph was blessed in prison, where by the spirit of wisdom he interpreted the truth of the dreams he had heard and revealed the course of things to come? Would anyone deny that Isaiah was blessed when he was sawn through the middle, Jeremiah when he was sunk down, holy Daniel when he stood unafraid among the lions and a prophet, carried off by an angel, brought him his meal? He was certainly not blessed because he ate another's meal, but because by his merits he shut the unfed mouths of the lions. But who would not call blessed the mother of the Maccabees, who added herself as the eighth to the deaths of her seven sons, for the praise of the heavenly triumph?

Chapter X.

43. Nor shall I pass you over, Eleazar, being a priest as you were a priest; rather I must be helped by your prayers, you who, a priest by descent, skilled in the Law, ripe in years, when you were brought before Antiochus the persecutor, could neither be caught by temptations nor bent by rewards nor broken by the bitterness of tortures. Indeed, he began with blandishments, because he judged that his torments could be overcome. 'I feel shame' he said 'before your grey hair, I revere your old age, I admire your prudence: why do you think you must abstain from good food which nature bestows? Relax this kind of obstinacy, wake up at last, while I still prefer to persuade you rather than to wring it from you, lest, overcome by tortures, you do what you refuse when invited by reason.' To this the old man answered: 'We, Antiochus, are not led by obstinacy, but are held by reverence for the Law, which has commanded that we abstain from swine's flesh. You judge it a most beautiful creature; yet you cannot deny that temperance is better than pleasure, obedience to the Law better than transgression. But if you think it a light thing that we should eat swine's flesh, how will he who has despised the Law in the least things keep it in great ones? Or if a creature of this kind is precious, you are avenged when we are cheated of what is most beautiful. Our abstinence, moreover, is a discipline of chastity. For we learn to cut back luxury, to conquer desires, to shut out concupiscence, to resist the pleasures of the body. It is also an exercise of fortitude not to yield to punishments for the Law's sake, and a mark of justice too, and of prudence, that what we have chosen to follow in the fear of God we should keep even when death is set before us. Who would dictate food to free men? And who would offer himself to be laughed at, so as to be a slave in this? But you will not laugh. I am not so old that the fortitude of my soul does not grow young in me.'

44. So when he was hung up and beaten severely on this side and on that, and his aged limbs could no longer bear the punishment of the blows, though the executioners and the stretchers were wearied, he, bowed down to the earth, bore an unbowed mind. And someone, whether pitying his great age or wishing to get round him with temptations: 'Only answer' he said 'that you will eat: we will make you exempt from that food'. But he cried out: 'Never may it befall me that as an old man I become an incitement to the error of the young, I who until now was a saving pattern of our way of life. Shall I, then, by these mockeries, gain a little more life, and make over the labours of my whole life for the journey-money of a brief old age? Old age ought to be a harbour, not a shipwreck, of the life before it. I will not deny you, law of our fathers; I will not forswear you, holy institutions of our ancestors; I will not discolour you, priestly fillets; I will not openly defile you with the dust of faithlessness, grey hair'. Why say more? By dying in his torments he became a lesson in perseverance to the rest, he who had been chosen as an example of weakness. Blessed, then, is he in whom torments could not conquer reason. Or is he not blessed who could by the virtue of his soul be victor over punishments and, by the oarage of devotion, keep his passion whole amid such great waves?

Chapter XI.

45. After him the seven boys were set before him with their mother. One may jeer at the tyrant, who, while he cunningly thought that he should begin with the old man, chose a teacher who would make his disciples braver; their boyish age, as it were, he tried to provoke to guilt with rewards and pressed to fear with terrors. But they, not unworthy of so great a leader, answer: 'Why do you despise us or try to get round us as if we were boys? Our faith is grey-haired; our discipline is strong. Put it to the test, by all means: subject our boyish flesh to whatever punishments you please; you will not find boyish hearts, nor will the engines of torment be more powerful than the watch kept by our observance of the Law. Him whom old age conquered, boyhood, the rival of old age, will overcome. As sons we follow our father, as disciples our teacher. Gather up the instruments of torture you have set out: while they are seen, they bring a training in patience, not the terror of ignorance'.

46. He ordered the eldest to be chosen. But he, laughing: 'Rightly you keep the order of nature. But why do you think that the law of God must be profaned? For devotion, indeed, we are all the eldest; yet I rejoice that it has begun with me. What do you seek, tyrant? I confess that we serve the most high God. And you teach us what we ought to do. If with such pertinacity you wish to wring the truth from us, why should we not think that we must hold it with all our strength?' Why say more? Kinds of various punishments are applied. But devotion conquered the fury of savagery: the soul was driven out, not religion.

47. The second came forward and, not unworthy of his brother, fulfilled the duties of a devout confession. And when the skin of his head was being torn off, he answered: 'You take away the skin indeed, but I have a spiritual helmet which you cannot take away'. And truly no one can take this helmet away, as the Apostle afterwards taught in the Church of the Lord: for the head of man is Christ, and we are his members. Rightly did the boy foresee by the divine Spirit this apostolic teaching. The savage beasts were stripping the hide from his head and raging with the ferocity of leopards. But he, as his strength failed: 'How sweet it is' he said 'to die for religion, how pleasant all the bitterness of death for devotion, because the reward of these labours remains! Yours, O king, are the heavier torments; you are tortured more violently by silent torments, because you see yourself conquered in your power.'

48. And when he was dead, he ordered the third to be set before him. And when he partly tried him with snares and partly wished to strike him down with terror, he answered him: 'I will not do your will, nor will I yield to your command. By that blessed passion and nobility of my brothers, I will not deny our loving brotherhood. Apply whatever tortures you like: by pressing the harder with them you will gain only this by the harshness of the punishments, that you will receive greater testimonies of our brotherhood'. He therefore ordered his tongue to be cut off. But he, crying out: 'You are conquered' he said 'Antiochus, you who order the instrument of the voice to be cut off. You have confessed that you cannot answer reason, and you prove that the scourges of our tongue are greater than your blows. For we do not fear your blows; you cannot endure the scourges of our voice. But these are the scourges of devotion, yours the scourges of unbelief. Yet even when the tongue is taken away, it will scourge you more heavily with its murmur as it falls. Do you think you escape, Antiochus, if you snatch away the voice? God hears even the silent, and hears them more. See, I have opened my mouth, I have loosed my tongue. Cut off the tongue, but you will not cut off constancy, you will not take away virtue, you will not blot out reason, you will not snatch away the testimony of truth, you will not snatch away the cry of the heart. If the tongue is cut off, the blood will cry out, and it will be said to you: The voice of your brother's blood cries to me. For he hears the voice of blood who hears the inner thoughts — 'though darkness covers me and the enclosures of walls surround me' the ungodly man may say, because no witness stands by him — God searches out all things, God sees all things, nor is there any crime that can be hidden from the judge of all, who knows all things before they happen. Why do we deal in words? Wounds are more eloquent: even if the wounds are covered, even if the scar is hidden, faith is not hidden. Yet do not applaud yourself that by taking away the tongue you snatch away the confession of praise. We have already praised God enough by speech; now let us praise him by suffering'.

49. And when he had been killed, he ordered the fourth to be bound to the wheel, so that by its turning he might be loosened in all his limbs. But he, while he was being savagely tortured: 'You dissolve the limbs of the body' he said 'but you add grace to the passion, and you do not snatch its solace from death.' For there is the voice of thunder in the wheel, because in the good and unstumbling course of this life the heavenly oracle resounds, as it resounded in John and in James, the sons of thunder. And so what I have read I now recognize more clearly, that a wheel runs within a wheel and is not hindered. For a smooth life, without any stumbling, turns in any suffering whatever, and within these things too the wheel runs. The Law runs within grace, and the observance of the Law is within the course of divine mercy; for the more it is turned, the more it is proved. 'It is better here to bear adversities from the ungodly, so that there we may find consolation from the Lord'. And he too, finishing his course, broke off his breath and, a victor, poured out his soul.

50. When the fifth was set before him, he ordered that, after he had first been beaten, fires should be brought to him and flames set beneath him. Gore flowed from his wounds, and the blood pouring from his gashed sores was putting out the very balls of flame; but amid the crackling of the fires he was heard saying: 'Thanks to you, Lord, that you have granted us to say: we have passed through fire, as elsewhere your same prophet says: you have tried us with fire, as silver is tried with fire. I shall stand before you like gold purged by burning, and if there was any guilt, the fire has burned it out'. And so he too, transfigured from corruption to incorruption, breathed out his life.

51. Tortures were applied to the sixth also. But he: 'Do not' he said 'err in vain and ascribe it to your power that you exercise these things against us. This is the price of our offences, that we are punished as sinners; and thanks to the Lord, that here our sins are exacted from us twofold, so that there consolation may be brought to us. Thanks also to you, that you are so hard and pitiless, so that through such tortures of ours the Lord, against whom we have sinned, may become merciful to our nation. We too lighten our hardship, while we delight in the sufferings of faith'. And he too, torn apart by hard and harsh tortures, was laid beside his brothers.

52. The youngest of the brothers remained, and Antiochus was now ashamed that he had been made such a laughing-stock by an unripe age. So, wishing to get round him by tricks, he promised him honours, riches, his own friendship, a share in his secrets. But the devout mother admonished her son, saying to this one as to the rest: 'I do not know how you came into my womb; neither did I give you breath nor form your limbs, but these are the gifts of almighty God'. Antiochus, seeing the mother anxious, thought that she feared for his safety, and he began to urge her too to call her son back from his purpose. But she said to her son in her native tongue: 'You alone, son, survive, the sum of my prayers; you last closed my childbearing, do you, the last, complete my joys. Have pity on me, who carried you in my womb through so many courses of months, lest in one moment you put my old age to shame, lest you discolour the trophies of so many brothers of yours, lest you leave their sacred company, lest you desert their fellowship. These triumphs still await you. Look to heaven, whence you drew your breath, to the Father of all; look to the earth, which before supplied you with nourishment; look to your brothers, who seek their companion; look to your mother, I who gave you milk. Pay back the reward of loving blood: do not be torn from your brothers, do not be torn from your mother. Temporal are the riches that Antiochus promises, temporal the honours: everlasting is the crown which is conferred by almighty God. The Lord gave you to me as the lights of seven days; I have now closed the sixth day, and the works of all are very good. You owe it to me, son, that I, who laboured in those six, may rest in you on the seventh, as one now keeping holiday from the works of the world.' And so the young man, rushing forward, said: What are you waiting for? And crying out many things, that he could by no means be torn from the fellowship of his brothers, whose deaths were far more blessed than the commands of the king, while he pressed the king with reproaches, he too, tortured with bitter kinds of torments, discharged the duty of this life.

53. Last of all the mother was brought to death. Who would deny that she was blessed, who stood among the bodies of her sons as if walled about by seven walls, feeling no onset of death? Who, I say, would doubt her blessedness, who, surrounded by seven towers, raised her head into the seat of paradise, who, girded with seven sons, brought to the heavenly altars a most sacred choir for God, tuneful not only with voices but also with sufferings, to sing together the praises of the Lord? How good a childbearing of faith, how safe this harbour of devotion, how splendid a lamp of the Church, shining with sevenfold light and, with her womb as the eighth, supplying oil to all the lights! Of them it is beautifully said: give a portion to those seven, and indeed to those eight, because in both numbers they attain the fellowship of grace, nourished in the Law, crowned through grace, seven as in the sabbath, eight as in the Gospel, the devout mother having been joined as the completion of the passion, she who in such sons both travailed with and brought forth the perfect form of devotion.

Chapter XII.

54. There come back to memory the words of the holy woman, saying to her sons: 'I bore you, I poured into you the juice of milk: do not lose your nobility'. In this way other mothers are accustomed to call their sons back from martyrdom, not to call them to it. But she placed a mother's affection in this, that she should persuade her sons to a life everlasting rather than temporal. So the devout mother watched the contests of her sons and, though she was shaken by the fellow-suffering of a mother's inward parts, she pressed down her grief by the zeal of devotion; and when Antiochus offered it, though she could have chosen the safety of her sons, she preferred the danger, and, stifling the groans of nature, she wished the punishment of her sons to be increased, so that death might approach the more quickly. We see the mother's prayers turned round upon her sons: that she might leave none of them surviving, but might win them all as co-heirs of a devout death.

55. But neither were the sons less than such a parent; they exhorted one another, they said with one zeal and as it were with one battle-line of souls: 'Let us crush the onset of hostile death. For then we shall live, when we have died. Let no one desert the rank of devotion, let no one withdraw himself from the triumphal battle. Not to a man, but to almighty God have we devoted our souls; not for a man, but for the author of all things do we serve as soldiers. Such is this fight, that he has conquered more gloriously who has been more cruelly slain'. And so no one feared, no one trembled, none of so many brothers was slower to death, but all ran to death through bitter tortures as if on the way to immortality. And the mother, seeing the harmonious ranks of her sons, offered, like a devoted soul, the members of her own body in her sons, and seemed to herself to undergo in her own limbs the torments she had prayed for.

56. Her sons fell, ulcerated by torments; the dead were rolled upon the dead, bodies tumbled upon bodies, heads were cut off upon heads; the place was filled with the corpses of her sons: the mother did not weep, did not lament, did not close the eyes or the mouth of any of the dying, did not wash the wounds, knowing that they were more glorious if they were seen disembowelled and clotted alike with dust and blood, such as victors are wont to return from war, such as they are wont to bring back trophies from their enemies. She judged that no coverings were to be thrown over them, no funeral rites to be performed, except with the company of her own death as well. What lyre could give forth sweeter songs than the dying sons had given forth in such grievous tortures? For the groan of nature broke forth even from the unwilling. You might have seen the corpses of the slain in order like the threads of strings. You might have heard a seven-stringed psaltery resound with triumphal groans. Not so would those enticing songs of the Sirens, as they say, draw the hearer; for they drew him to shipwreck, these to the victory of sacrifice. Nor would the songs of swans so soothe the ears and the soul; for swans die by the lot of nature, these died for love of devotion. Not so do the hoarse murmurs of doves resound in a secret grove as the last words of the dying resounded with the utmost sweetness. Nor does the moon so shine among the stars as the mother shone among her sons, both when she led them down to martyrdom to give them light, and when she lay embracing the victors in the midst of her sons.

57. O mother, truly stronger than adamant, sweeter than honey, more fragrant than a flower! O indissoluble bond of love! O truly love strong as death, jealousy hard as hell of devotion and faith! No floods of such great sufferings could shut out your love, no rivers of such great bitternesses could overflow it. As the ark in that flood of the world was borne unharmed over the expanses of the whole earth, so you too stood immovable in devotion against the tide of such grievous sufferings, and, though you could have chosen the safety of your sons, you would not.

58. With what affection shall I honour you, loving pledges of a holy mother? With what brush of my speech shall I portray the likeness of your form and of your souls? You stood amid the royal armies by which the whole world had been subdued, which India too shunned, fleeing into the secret places of the farthest sea, and you alone brought back victory over a proud king without warlike conflict. In you the arms of devotion alone conquered; the tyrant paid the penalty, both because he could not conquer you and because he was destroyed by a dreadful death.
