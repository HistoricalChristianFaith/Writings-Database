Second Apology for the Prophet David

Latin source: the second apology for the prophet David, a sermon on David's sin with Bathsheba and the beginning of Psalm 50 (51), addressed in turn to the gentiles, the Jews and the Church, in twelve chapters. It is transmitted among Ambrose's works, but his authorship is disputed: Schenkl judged it spurious and printed the author's name in brackets; Connolly, Visonà and Lucidi have defended it, and Savon has argued against it again. Translated from the critical text of C. Schenkl, Corpus Scriptorum Ecclesiasticorum Latinorum 32/2 (Vienna, 1897), pp. 359–408, verified against the page images and collated with the Maurist text in Migne, Patrologia Latina 14, cols. 887–916. Four readings depart from Schenkl's printed text: Chapter V §31 satis ("a sufficient answer"), with the manuscripts and Migne, for his tantis; Chapter VII §35 meis for the misprint mei; Chapter XII §64 a single negation for his doubled non; and Chapter XII §68 read as a statement rather than a question. Scripture is translated as the author quotes it, from his own Old Latin text; italics mark the Scripture he quotes. The chapters and the section numbers are Schenkl's (the Maurists' and Migne's), with the sections running through the whole work; the Maurist chapter summaries are not translated. Psalms keep the Latin (LXX) numbering, with the Hebrew number in parentheses. Schenkl's two supplements are read into the text and the word he secludes in Chapter IX §49 is left out; the gap he prints in Chapter XII §68 is marked [lacuna], and his critical apparatus is not translated. This English translation is released into the public domain.

Chapter I

1. Perhaps the title of the psalm, which you heard read, may have offended many, in that Nathan the prophet came to him, when he had gone in to Bathsheba. At the same time the reading of the Gospel that has been gone through could also have stirred no small scruple in the unskilled, the reading in which you noticed that an adulteress was brought to Christ and that the same woman was sent away without condemnation. For surely, if anyone receives these things with idle ears, he runs into an incitement to error when he reads of the adultery of a holy man and the acquittal of the adulteress, falling by a human and almost divine example: as though a man had thought that adultery was to be committed and God had judged that adultery was not to be condemned. Slippery, then, is the way to a fall, whether of pardon or of desire.

2. There is this besides: he seems to kindle the very torches of youthful wantonness, because he did not blush at his adultery, did not hide it, but made it public by a kind of proclamation in a divine song. What then? Was holy David so shameless and so improvident that he himself should sing his own disgrace, especially since he himself said in another psalm too, which has been gone through today: How long shall sinners, O Lord, how long shall sinners glory? Does he forbid others to glory in sin, and does he himself glory, even in a sacred song? How then are we to distinguish these things?

3. Grant, however, that David erred wantonly: did Christ also err, so that we should think he did not have right judgment? Aptly, then, did David prophesy today, as if to them, saying: Understand, you senseless ones, and you fools, be wise at last. For how could Christ err? It is not right that this should even come into our thoughts. He who planted the ear, does he not hear? Or he who formed the eye, does he not consider? He who chastises the nations, shall he not reprove, he who teaches man knowledge? Is Christ, then, ignorant of how to examine guilt and to hear a just accusation? Could Christ approve wantonness? Did Christ, who chastises the nations, not think that the adulteress should be reproved? Christ, who knows the inner things of each one's heart, teaches the knowledge of the Law: could he either be deceived by an error or judge against the tenor of the Law? And how did he himself say: I came not to destroy the Law but to fulfil it? Could Christ, who condemns hidden things, overlook things made public? Can Christ, who made the times, not know the times? He who knows the vain thoughts of men, does he not know the criminal ones also?

4. And so let this be answer enough to those who, though they cannot deny the Creator, yet deny the might of the Creator; who, though they confess his wisdom, assert his folly, charging him with ignorance of things to come. On this I could discourse more widely, but another treatise seems to have been set before us. And although a sequence of different readings has been gone through, yet they serve the same assertion, above all the title of the psalm and the Gospel reading. But although one assertion suits them, still let the order of the treatise follow the order of the readings, and so it seems that we must treat first of the title of the psalm.

Chapter II

5. The history that has been set before us contains adultery and murder. For thus we find it written in the book of Kingdoms: that King David, while he was walking about in his house, saw the wife of Uriah washing herself, loved her at once and ordered her to be summoned. Then he ordered her husband, an innocent man, as Scripture presents him, to be thrown against the fiercest warriors, so that he might be overwhelmed by the force of the enemy. These things were done, and they are not denied. How, then, will they be defended? The Gospel reading well admonishes us that, even when the sin is evident, the judgment of the judge ought nevertheless to be sober, and that each one ought to be mindful of his own condition and desert. For often, even in judging, the sin of the judgment is greater than the sin of the very one on whom judgment has been passed. For if it has been prescribed by certain wise men of the world that in judging one should take care that the penalty be not greater than the guilt, and this is observed, how much more does it seem that this ought to be observed: that each one who is about to judge another should first judge himself, and should not condemn lesser errors in another when he himself has committed graver ones. Who, then, are you who judge David, a holy man? I say it to the Gentile, I say it to the Jew, I say it to the Christian. And therefore it seems to me that the treatise should be divided into three parts: one against the Gentiles, another against the Jews, the third among the Christians.

Chapter III

6. First, then, my discourse is against the Gentiles, who are mostly accustomed to object: "Behold how the Christians follow innocence, display faith, venerate religion, teach chastity, whose chief men are reported to have committed both murders and adulteries! David himself, of whose race, as you say, Christ chose to be born, he himself has sung of both his murders and his adulteries. What sort can the disciples be, whose masters are such?"

7. What then? Do we deny the deed, or do we repudiate the master? Neither of these; but let him who casts up the particular consider what is common. The particular of the deed is what is common to nature. It is no wonder, then, if the particular lies within the general. For I acknowledge that David was a man, and it is no wonder: I acknowledge that it is common for a man to sin. For the weakness of the human condition is not new, and it seems more wonderful if a man is free of sin than if sin befalls him. Holy David, then, committed sin — I shall hold you in no ambiguity — he committed adultery, he devised murder, and he devised it and carried it out. He sinned, as kings are wont to do, but he did penance and wept, which kings are not wont to do. He asked for pardon, not arrogant of his power but conscious of his weakness; prostrate on the ground, he covered himself with sackcloth, forgetful of his rule and mindful of his guilt.

8. What man of this kind can you find me who, set in power, does not rather love his crimes, proclaim his guilt, defend his sins; who thinks that what is not fitting is not permitted to him; who binds himself by his own laws and recognizes that what is not permitted by justice is not permitted by power either? For power does not loosen justice, but justice loosens power; the king is not loosed from the laws, but by his own example he loosens the laws. Or can it be that he who judges others should himself be free of his own judgment, and admit in himself that for which he binds others?

9. Good, then, is David, and he who conquered power is much more admirable than he who conquered love. For chastity is sometimes granted to the body, often by error; power is subject to God, and a man more easily restrains himself in love than moderates himself in power. Will you not, then, pardon what is lesser in one in whom you admire greater things? Slippery in all men and prone to sinning is nature; slippery also, even amid good morals, is the licence of power and the offer of opportunity. For the anger of a king does not differ from the anger of a lion; but he who provokes him and meddles with him sins against his own soul. Let one beware, then, that no one provoke power, lest the soul, entangled in bodily snares, be unable to strip itself of vice. It is no wonder, then, if David too fell through the show of power, but it is much more to be admired that he was called back by the contemplation of faith.

10. You see, then, that this worldly power frequently profits nothing and mostly does harm; for often the issue of guilt lies in the authority of power. Do not, then, you, whoever you are, O king, presume upon might and opportunity; for the heart of the king is in the hand of the Lord. Do not flatter yourself on the subjection of peoples, because the king is not saved by the multitude of his power, nor shall the giant be saved in the multitude of his power. For not in the lofty pinnacles of dwellings and the costly summit of roofs, but in those who fear him is the Lord well pleased, and in those who hope upon his mercy.

11. We see, then, that power is slippery toward vices; yet I would not say that it is always bent toward them, since we read both that David, exalted by royal power, increased the divine worship, and that Solomon consecrated a temple to the Lord.

12. Now let us establish what we touched on briefly, that the faculty of commanding is an incentive to sinning, and let us follow the sequence of the history. David would not have adulterated another's right of the marriage-bed and the compact of a covenanted marriage, had he not seen from within his house a woman naked and washing. And therefore it is well written: Do not fall into the beauty of a woman and do not covet a woman, and elsewhere: Beware of the eyes of every shameless woman. Presume nothing of your continence and virtue. For indeed the fornication of a woman shall be known in the lifting up of her eyes and in her eyelids, and therefore flee first the cause of sin; for no one is strong for long, and it is said not to the frail only, but to every man: Let not the desire of beauty conquer you — if you do not wish to be conquered, do not join battle with sins, lest vices be crowned over you — nor be captured by eyes nor carried off by eyelids. A woman seems to you cheap in price, but she is strong for vice, because a woman captures the precious souls of men.

13. It is difficult, therefore, for anyone ensnared in the allurements of lust to escape unharmed. Nor is this judged difficult by my opinion only, but it has been judged impossible even in the Proverbs of holy Solomon, who, led by his own example, says: Shall anyone bind fire in his bosom, and not burn his garments? Or shall anyone walk upon coals of fire, and not burn his feet? Beware, then, you too, lest you kindle within the bosom of your mind the fire of lust and love, lest the bodily garment be consumed by that burning of the mind, and the perpetuity of the resurrection, or at least the footstep of your mind, be burned, if you think that you must walk through the torches of lusts; for he who is burned in heart is burned up in body.

14. In every way, then, the encounter with a woman's wantonness must be avoided. And therefore Acquire prudence as a disciple for yourself he says, that she may guard you from a strange wife and a harlot, lest she hold you bound in the snares of her lips and wrap you in the little curls of error. Therefore the prophet has taught you from where you ought to beware of the prostitute: that you beware of her entrances by the window. For she enters by the window of her house; her window is the eye, and therefore beware of the eyes of every shameless woman, lest love come in through the window and lust penetrate. For indeed the harlot entices the lover by sight, and unless you restrain the wanton gaze of mind and spirit, death enters through the window. Not sluggish is the impudence of the prostitute, not idle her wantonness, which makes the hearts of young men fly, so that they cannot keep the constancy of their own mind and are swept hither and thither by burning love. Beware, then, of a woman of this kind, who does not rest on her feet, wanders out of doors, lies in wait at the corners, binds with her eyes, entices with words, has woven her bed with ribbons and with coverlets spread from Egypt. For she takes her adornment not from divine knowledge but from worldly allurement, she who rightly says that her husband is absent, since no adulteress can have Christ present.

15. You see, then, by how great nets even the hearts of the saints are caught, and therefore do not wonder if holy David too was caught, a man indeed great, and one who by faith conquered the Philistine, monstrous in body and bristling also with weapons of war. But would that he had conquered himself; would that, as he laid low that adversary, so he could have laid low and overcome his inner adversary! Heavier is the battle of him who fights within than of him who fights without.

16. But why do I speak of David alone? Let us summon others too to a treatise of this kind, lest we think that the feebleness was David's, that is, of one man, rather than the weakness of the bodily condition. Samson, mighty and strong, strangled a lion, but he could not strangle his love. He loosed the bonds of his enemies, but he did not loose the knots of his own desires. He burned the harvests of others, and he himself, set alight by the little fire of one woman, lost the harvest of his virtue. Solomon built a temple to God: but would that he had kept the temple of his own body! But let the discourse return to him from whom it departed. David triumphed in ten thousands, but he erred in twenty thousands and more; and, because he erred, he recognized that he was a man, confessed his guilt, and begged for pardon, saying to the Lord: Lord, do not rebuke me in your anger, nor chastise me in your wrath. Have mercy on me, Lord, for I am weak. If David was weak, are you strong? If Solomon fell, are you immovable? If Paul was the first of sinners, can you be the first of saints? So if these erred, they erred as men, but they acknowledged their sin as just men. If the just received the sentence of a harsher punishment, how do you set before yourself a hope of impunity, when Scripture says: If the just man is scarcely saved, where will the sinner and the ungodly appear?

17. You observe, then, children, how often the cause of sinning overturns even a good purpose, and therefore you ought first to flee and avoid the causes themselves. You do not wish to be caught by love: then do not fix your gaze on the beauty of a woman, because when your eyes have seen a strange woman, then your mouth will speak perverse things, and you will lie as in the heart of the sea and like a helmsman in a great whirlwind. For the multitudes of desires make a great storm, which, as in a kind of strait of the body, toss the voyager this way and that, so that the mind cannot be its own helmsman, not knowing day from night in the darkness of love.

18. By the eyes, then, lust is roused, but by drunkenness it is set ablaze; and every drunkard and fornicator shall become poor. Nor should you flatter yourself on your power of drinking. Noah was made drunk, and he who was not made drunk by the flood was made drunk by wine. But he did not know the nature of wine, for he had not drunk before: you have learned in him what you ought to beware of. Lot was deceived through sleep; and you, if you do not wish to be deceived, shake off the sleep of your mind, lest a son mock you when drunk or a daughter when asleep. It is not piety that is laughed at there, but drunkenness; nor is it a father who is cheated there, but a sleeper. Do you wish not to be scorched? Do not go near the fire. Do you wish not to be cast headlong? Flee what totters, beware of steep places, turn aside from what is falling.

19. You see, then, that in those who merited to be just there was not a different nature, but a different discipline. For after the fall of the first man and the just sentence, since in one man the appetite of the flesh was condemned, the condition contracted vice and guilt infected nature; but faith tempered the vice of nature itself, and the intent of devotion softened the offence.

20. Turn, then, to God, O nations, and know that the Lord himself is God. He made us, and not we ourselves. Know that we are flesh and dust. Let us, then, leave what we have done and venerate the very author of our being, who gave life, who forgave sins, who alone can say: I am, I am he who blots out your iniquities, and I will not remember. Great indeed and merciful is God, who confers a benefit and does not reproach the gift of the benefit. Do you wonder that he made David a king and the conqueror of many nations? So he is wont to raise up his little servants. Not greedy for gifts nor sparing of benefit nor narrow in grace nor thrifty, but rich in largesse, he increases in reward those whom he has redeemed from sin. So much against the Gentiles.

Chapter IV

21. But since we promised a threefold division of this treatise, one part against the Gentiles, another against the Jews, the third before the Church, it now seems that we must treat against the Jews. Let us review, then, what has been set before us. The title he says of the psalm of David, of understanding: when Nathan the prophet came to him, when he had gone in to Bathsheba. Surely this history presents David both as an adulterer and as a murderer, and this man you, Jews, say is the Son of God! But God is the God of virtues, not of crimes, and of him God the Father says: His throne is as the days of heaven. He who has practised guile has not conquered sins: and how is it written: No one is without sin but the one God? If, then, he who is the Son of God cannot not be God, surely the Son of God is the arbiter of justice, not liable to guilt.

22. And by what opinion do you judge David to be the Son of God, or think Solomon to be so? Is it because it is written: O God, give your judgment to the king and your justice to the king's son, and because the title of the psalm says that the seventy-first psalm (72) was composed for Solomon? But observe which Solomon it means. For Solomon is "peaceable": this is what the name means. The psalm is spoken of him whom we truly know to be the lover of peace. But how was Solomon peaceable? The blood of Joab does not show it, whom he ordered to be slain among the altars of the temple; nor the punishment of Adonijah, whom he struck down as guilty of aspiring to royal kinship, and was not called back from his indignation even by his mother's entreaty. And how do you reckon that what is written fits Solomon, the son of David, that he shall endure with the sun and before the moon for ages of ages, when he, having obtained the use of a short life, passed his span of living within narrow bounds? And how is it said of him: He shall have dominion from sea to sea, when he, established within Syria, that is, within the province of a single region, held the bounds of his rule circumscribed, while Christ alone has extended his rule to the ends of the whole world? For he alone is the one of whom the sequence of the psalm rightly prophesied that the kings of the Arabs and Sheba shall bring gifts, and all the kings of the earth shall adore him, all nations shall serve him. For we know him as ruling over all peoples and nations with an uncircumscribed rule, with an unbounded power.

23. Solomon, then, is not the Son of God. But on what reasoning did you think him the Son of God? Is it because he was wise? But he asked for wisdom, so as to receive what he did not have: Christ is himself Wisdom, having by nature, universally, what Solomon received by grace in human affairs. In fact, what is received from time is possessed in time. For Solomon neither had wisdom at his beginning nor possessed it at his end. For he did not believe that he had what he asked for; and afterwards, turned away from the worship of God, he fell not as a wise man but as a foolish one, so as to give offence. And he gave offence, so that he lost even what he had earned. Why, then, did you believe him to be the Son of God? Is it because he built a temple to God? But from this very thing you ought to have believed that he was not Lord and God, because it is written: Unless the Lord build the house for himself, they have laboured in vain who build it. He laboured in vain who built that temple which fire set beneath it burned up. Or is it because it was said to David his father: Not you shall build me a house, but when you have fallen asleep, I will raise up your seed after you? But there too a temple is promised that is not bodily, not material; for God does not dwell in things made by hand, but the temple is the holy Church, which surely is founded not by human but by heavenly power.

24. For if we admire the works of a virtue more excellent, granted, but still human, we find many men of this kind whom we can compare with Solomon. And therefore where there is equality of work, there is no pre-eminence of power. Nor can we speak of many sons of God, since we have read of one Son of God; but no injury must be done to the Son of God on the ground of his own benefits, which he conferred on our fathers. For many men were illustrious, but through one, Jesus son of Nave (Joshua), Let the sun stand still he says over against Gibeon. You see that his power went beyond David's; for David commanded earthly things, not the stars. You see also that the sun is a servant, not a lord, since it even obeyed a human voice. Yet it stood still, because in Jesus it recognized both the type and the name of the one to come. For Jesus son of Nave commanded the heavenly lights not in his own power but by the mystery of Christ; for it was being signified that the Son of God would come into this world, who by divine power would delay the setting of the worldly light, which was going down and already declining into darkness, would restore the light, would bring in brightness. Enoch too was caught up to heaven; but he was caught up, this one returned. He was caught up lest wickedness should change his heart; this one abolished the very wickedness of this world. Elijah ascended to heaven by chariot and horses, but Christ came down from heaven with neither chariot nor horses. As he ascended because he could not have done otherwise, this one went back by his own power. Elisha commanded the leper to wash in the Jordan, that he might be cleansed of all contagion: this one in the Jordan washed the whole world clean. Moses himself, to whom the people of the Jews was entrusted that it might pass over, divided the water. And he indeed divided the elements, because he did not divide the might of the Trinity; he separated the masses of the waters, because he did not separate the Father from the Son.

25. You see how great the men were, and of what works they left the proofs. How, then, do you judge unequal in condition those whom you recognize as equal in virtue to King David or to Solomon, so as to think that Solomon, the son of David, sits at the right hand of God, especially when David himself plainly expressed of whom it had been said? For he would not say of his own son: The Lord said to my Lord: Sit at my right hand. For how would he name his son his lord? The Law forbids, religion resists, faith abhors that you should place a mortal man at the right hand of almighty God. Another is he who sits at the right hand: he who took up a body, not he who began from a body; he who was begotten before the daystar, that is, before the brightness of all lights, since he himself created the brightnesses of the various lights; for Solomon surely is after the daystar. Solomon was not a priest; and therefore it could not be said to him by almighty God: From the womb before the daystar I begot you; it could not be said to him: You are a priest for ever according to the order of Melchizedek, especially since at that time when Solomon lived the priests were according to the order of Aaron, not according to Melchizedek. For the priests were still offering the blood of goats and bulls for the sins and errors of the people; but after he came who offered himself for the salvation of the world, so that the blood of Christ might wash clean those whom the blood of bulls could not wash, the priests began to offer themselves in place of victims.

26. And so by one question our Lord Jesus Christ stopped the mouths of all heretics and shut in their sacrileges. For by this question he refuted not only the Jews, but also the Photinians and the Arians and the Sabellians. And therefore, since we now see that the Jews have failed and that execrable faithlessness has been diminished, we must now touch briefly, in the treatise on this question, on some things concerning the Arians, who do not differ much from the Jews, and the Photinians and the Sabellians. Let Photinus, then, be silent, who says that Christ is the son of David, not of God; and let him be silent, condemned by the heavenly voice. For how does he say that Christ is the son of David, when David himself said in the book of Psalms: The Lord said to my Lord: Sit at my right hand, until I put your enemies under your feet? If David he says calls him lord, how is he his son? Not, then, the son of David, but the Son of God is Christ. Let the Sabellian also be silent: when he reads that the Lord said to the Lord, let him understand that he who speaks and he to whom it is spoken are not one and the same — for the Father does not say to himself: Sit at my right hand, nor did he say to himself: I will put your enemies — and let all at length understand that it is the Son who sits at the right hand, and let them keep this distinction between the Father and the Son, so that there is a distinction of Father and Son, in that the Father is one and the Son another; but, because they sit on one throne, enjoy one majesty, and sit not as a mark of distinction in dignity nor by an order of power, but by the union of love, let them understand one power of the Father and of the Son. In this let Arius too fall silent, who presses on in the tracks of an error contrary to Sabellius. Let him hear the Lord saying to the Lord, let him hear that the Son sits at the right hand of the Father, and let him cease to raise a question against the divinity from the speech and order of men. Nor let him argue here that the Father says sit; for we read elsewhere too that the Son sits even without the Father's word, as it is written: and he sits at the right hand of God, and elsewhere: Henceforth you will see the Son of Man sitting at the right hand of God. For he to whom sit is said is the one who is named the Son of Man. He hears as man, he sits as the Son of God. What can be called more pre-eminent than this power, which set even the flesh of man at the right hand of God, and joined that weak thing of the human condition, once the Word had indeed been made flesh, to the eternal divinity?

27. But what is this? After Photinus has been struck dumb, Arius has fallen silent, Sabellius has lost his voice, I still see heresy rousing diverse mouths against the Church. For behold, the Manichaean and the Valentinian and every offshoot of the Manichaeans relies on that weapon by which the others were slain: it contrives a prejudgment against the faith by means of the testimony of the faith. For the detestable heresy says: "Behold, Christ denied that he was the son of David, and therefore" it says "it must be believed that he did not take up flesh". To them Scripture answers most plainly at the very beginning of the Gospel and says: The book of the generation of Jesus Christ, the son of David. Why, then, is he called the son of David in one place, and in another denied to be the son of David, unless that you may understand that it is one thing to be a son according to the divine substance, another according to the taking up of flesh? For according to the divine substance he is the Son of God, according to the flesh the son of David. And so where the generation of the Saviour according to the flesh is described, he is named the son of David; but where the fullness of confession is required, when the generation of the Saviour is designated, the Son of God does not wish to be called the son of David; for the designation of his majesty interprets his nature. But now that the interpretations of the perverse have been refuted, the discourse must turn to the Church.

Chapter V

28. But that we often repeat the title of Psalm 50 (51) is an example not of presumption, but of weakness. For in one day, whether through the narrowness of my talent or through the frailty of my voice, I cannot complete the whole sequence of the treatise. And therefore, since on the day before our discourse was directed against the Jews, whom the proofs of the faith indeed subdued so far that Christ was placed on the heavenly throne, which surely cannot fit a man — for it is not credible that any other than the Son of God sits at the right hand of God — surely no other must be reckoned by us, all ambiguities removed, to sit at the right hand of the Father but the Son of God. Again, since Scripture has: Of the fruit of your womb I will set upon your throne, the same Son of God surely must be believed to have taken up flesh from Mary, in whom is the fruit of the prophetic womb, so that you may truly recognize the succession of origin and not doubt that he, the Son of God, made afterwards the son of man, namely through the taking up of flesh, sits on the heavenly throne. For Christ did not have any royal throne in this world, so that you should think that another throne was promised to the seed of David, especially since the Lord himself said: My kingdom is not of this world.

29. Shut in, therefore, on every side, they turn the attempts of their faithlessness to this, that they assert it to be incredible that God took up flesh; but on what reasoning he could not take it up they cannot explain. For I ask whether they reckon that flesh could not be taken up by God by reason of impossibility or of injury? If of impossibility, is anything, then, impossible to God? For there is nothing that he has willed to do and could not do. It remains, then, that it be shown whether he willed or did not will. But what greater sign of his will is there than what he himself said: I appeared openly to those who did not seek me. I spread out my hands to a people not believing and contradicting? If, then, he willed and was able, do we think that he did not will by reason of injury? And what injury to itself does the divinity feel? For it is free of insult and is not subject to our passions; for flesh could bring the divinity neither loss nor increase.

30. Yet those who object that the Son of God could not take up flesh: if they are Gentiles, how can that be, when they testify that their gods, since they cannot deny that they were men, were seen in human form? Or could their king, whom they set before all their gods, take up portentous shapes for love, and could Christ not take up, for the salvation of the world, the flesh of man, whom he made in his own image, that most excellent work of his? And does it seem more beautiful to them that the form of flesh was assumed for the sake of adultery with another's marriage-bed than for the redemption of all? But if the Jew objects this, how does he read: Behold, a virgin shall conceive in the womb and bear a son, and his name shall be called Emmanuel, which is interpreted "God with us?" How does he read: Behold, I who spoke am here? And how does he assert that it is read: Behold, like a panther and like a bear in distress I will meet them? Could the Son of God, then, not take up flesh for the redemption of all, when they themselves cannot deny that for the punishment of our sins God put on a certain disposition of savage cruelty, so that he who by nature is kind and merciful, moved nevertheless by the atrocity of our crimes, assumes against us a certain fierceness of beasts?

31. But now, since I judge that both on the day before and today a sufficient answer has been given to the Jews, before you, dearly beloved brethren, I must render the account of the prophetic deed. For indeed when this title of the psalm was first read, that Nathan came to David, when he had gone in to Bathsheba, we said that very many are offended by the sequence of this history, and therefore we took up a threefold treatise of it; and I thought that the division of the treatise should be made thus: that before the Gentiles the fall of the condition should not be denied and the correction of the error should be established; but before the Jews we should teach that holy David fell for this reason, that the faithlessness of the Jews should limp no longer and that they should cease to believe him to be the Son of God, whom they saw to have been liable to sin by the common condition; while Christians may perceive the mysteries of the rising Church. The threefold division, then, has been made: one according to nature, another according to faith, the third according to grace. Neither does weakness shut out from pardon, and faith excuses from guilt; the sacraments also secure the grace long since promised.

Chapter VI

32. Let David have sinned, so that the whole world might not err; let him have sinned for himself, so that he might amend us all; finally, let him have sinned against his own body — for he who indeed commits fornication sins against his own body —, let him have sinned against his own body, so that he might be redeemed in the body of Christ: behold, the one whom we thought scarcely to be defended we now see to be proclaimed. For who is there who would not rather have divine gifts proclaimed in himself than human works? For we reckon, according to the Apostle, that a man is justified by faith without the works of the Law. Let David, then, be justified by faith, who through the Law came to know sin, but by faith believed in the pardon of sin; let David be justified, because in his sin the mysteries of the Church that was to arise shone forth.

33. Someone will say: for what reason did the one chosen as ancestor of the Lord's lineage commit both adultery and murder? But I say something else: such was the ancestor who had to be chosen for the Lord's body. For what is the incorporation but the remission of sins? And therefore he could not be without a share in sin, so that he might set forth divine grace both by example and by message. Accordingly, it is by that dispensation that Bathsheba and Tamar are reckoned among the ancestors of the Lord's lineage, of whom the one committed adultery, the other committed incest. By that dispensation both Ahab and Jeconiah, as Matthew the evangelist has written, are numbered among the forebears of Christ, so that he who was to redeem all men might begin the benefit from his own forebears; and at the same time, lest he who subjected himself even to the suffering of the body should seem to have sought out the nobility of a spotless origin; for this is the boasting of men, to seek another's glory, not their own; and yet among men themselves the grace of virtue is greater than that of nobility. At the same time an example had to be given, so that all might understand that the disgraces of forebears cannot be a dishonour to their descendants, since each one could abolish the stain of his succession by the merit of his own virtue. Do you see how many, and how grievously criminal, are those whom the sequence of the Lord's generation comprises, from whose origin Christ was not ashamed to be born for your sake? And if you believe these things, they are gifts of the divine mercy in you, and this is the mark of the heavenly power; for sin superabounded, that grace might superabound. Let David, then, not have been exempted from guilt, so that he might be chosen for grace.

34. But now let us review the mysteries of the history itself and draw from the very fountains of the Scriptures, and, so that we may be able to look into the whole sequence of the mysteries, let us go back over the text of the history itself. For thus, we remember, it is set down in the book of Kingdoms: that David, when he had seen Bathsheba, the wife of Uriah, washing, fell in love with her, and then ordered her to come to his house while the woman's husband was away. Not long after, her husband returned as a messenger of the war, a man truly religious and devout, who did not think that he ought to enter his house in time of war, when he saw his comrades intent on the war and keeping watch not in the tents of home but in the tents of battle. Afterwards, by David's order, he was sent back to the war under the instruction that he be thrown against the enemy fighters, so that by the husband's death the king might have free opportunity of possessing the woman. And here there is some shamefacedness about the sin and some shame for the guilt, in that he sought a hiding-place for his error and did not assume to himself authority for an unjust killing by right of royal power, but avoided odium. I confess the guilt; but the guilt that is held down by shamefacedness is more tolerable than that which is proclaimed with insolence. So Uriah died, thrown against the fighters; but afterwards those same men who had slain him were put to death by David's order when the city was stormed. This is the sequence of the history, in which one may behold profound mysteries.

Chapter VII

35. And since we have undertaken not to defend David — for he does not need my help —, but to excuse him, or rather to proclaim him, then, lest I waver in so great a depth of mysteries, it is right that I should use the mouth of him whose history I am using. I will use, then, the prophetic responsory, saying: Create a clean heart in me, O God, and renew a right spirit in my inmost parts. For no one without the infusion of the Holy Spirit can behold such great recesses of the divine mystery. For if so great a prophet asks that the gift of the Holy Spirit be poured into him, what ought I, who am weak, to do, especially when even the holy Apostle asks to be helped also by the prayers of the people, so that a door may be opened to him to speak the mystery of the word? O, if Christ would deign to open that door to me! Let us knock, nevertheless; for he is wont to hear those who knock, since he himself said: Knock, and it shall be opened to you. O, if he would unlock himself to me, for Christ is the door. He himself is within, he himself without, he himself the way that leads, he himself the life to which we strive to come.

36. Come, then, Lord Jesus, and open your fountains to us, that we may drink of the water which whoever drinks shall not thirst for ever; or, if we cannot yet draw from your fountain, deign to grant that we may at least draw from the well, from which you promise a drink to that Samaritan woman who was still doubting. And you indeed promise drink from the fountain to all; but to those who doubt, like that Samaritan woman, your fountain is still a deep well. Let us too drink the water of the heavenly secrets, and, since we have been found worthy to come to your fountain, may it be granted to us at least to see an image of the heavenly mysteries.

37. And so, unless I am mistaken, by the prophet we shall understand the Holy Spirit, while by the adulteress we may reckon the various fornications of the synagogue. And therefore we see it revealed long before that from the Holy Spirit and from the family of the Jews, whose are the fathers, of whom is Christ according to the flesh, the Lord Jesus was to be begotten, who indeed, as a Jew, was born of an adulterous family, but, as one spotless, of a virgin. The family of the Jews was under the Law, was as it were under a husband. The observance of the Law had to be abolished, so that truth and grace might be put in its place.

38. We have one mystery. Receive another as well, yet in such a way that you remember that from Bathsheba's first conception a little child was born, who died, and that afterwards Solomon was born: that little child from a stolen conception, but this Solomon from an avowed marriage. It is clear, then, that by David the prophet is understood, and by the prophet the prophetic people; and he who was born first from that people and from the synagogue, the people of the Jews, since he degenerated from his forebears, formed through shameful deeds and conceived through vices, could not attain to the age of resurrection nor grow up into a perfect man, but failed in a meagre smallness of understanding and in a kind of infancy of virtue. But he who was conceived afterwards from a lawful union, the Christian people, he, wise and peaceable — for these things are disclosed by the interpretation of the name Solomon —, attained to the hoary age of a long-lived resurrection and to that heavenly kingdom. Through that people the Law was dissolved; through this one grace was restored.

39. Receive also a third mystery. What the Hebrews call David the Latins interpret as "humbled". But who is truly humbled except him who did not think it robbery to be equal to God, but, taking the form of a servant, humbled himself, being made obedient even unto death? This, then, is the one who is signified by David: exalted by nature, but humbled by mercy; lofty in divinity, but meek in the body. And so where he is humbled, there he is obedient. For obedience is born from the same source as humility, and ends in it. For when he is said to have been made obedient even unto death, and death not of the divinity but of the body, that obedience was surely not of the divinity but of the body, nor was that humility of the majesty but of the flesh. And so, as regards the taking up of the body, the reading from the Apostle has declared in what the humility of Christ consisted; but what pertains to the nature of the divinity was disclosed by the Gospel reading, which you followed with devout assent when you heard it read, the Son of God saying: I and the Father are one; for the Son and the Father are one in divinity, but the mystery of the body and the eternity of the divinity are not one. Yet not only did the Apostle say that the Lord was humbled, but he himself also declared that he was humble, saying: Take up my yoke, for it is light, because I am meek and humble of heart. And yet he is signified not only by interpretation but also by an open appellation, for it is written: I have found David my servant, with my holy oil I have anointed him, and below: He shall say to me: You are my father, and: I will say to him: You are my son. So we have found the David whom we were seeking: a servant indeed in appearance, but Lord in truth.

Chapter VIII

40. Here, then, David was walking about within his own house. And what house of Christ is it, surely, but that of which he says that with my Father there are many mansions? Set, then, in that palace, he saw the human condition naked and, taking pity, loved it. For it was still naked of virtues, because through the snares of the serpent it had been deprived of the native garments of its nature. For it does not seem likely that a woman would strip in front of the king's house, that a woman would wash herself in front of the king's house, as if there could be no other place more fitting for washing. It does not fit, it does not agree, it does not accord with belief: it is far from the truth, it is repugnant to sense. Such a woman, so wanton, so forward, the king could have shuddered at, not loved. Or could she, if she could not feel shame before the gaze of men, not at least have stood in awe of the king's? Or could not even his servants have kept her away before the king saw her?

41. If, then, this cannot accord with belief, let us seek who this naked woman is: namely, the human condition, stripped of all the garments of nature, lacking the mantle of immortality and despoiled of the veil of innocence; for he is naked who is stripped by sin and guilt. Accordingly, that first sinner of our race — and would that he had been the only one —, before he sinned, did not perceive that he was naked; after he sinned, he saw that he was naked, and therefore thought that he must be covered with leaves, because he knew that he was naked. So he became naked to himself after he became answerable for guilt. In him the whole human condition was stripped, and through the succession of nature became liable not only to guilt but also to hardship. So he perceived and saw that he was naked. Hence our whole condition is such that he who thinks himself naked both sees and perceives himself naked. Accordingly, he who covets riches is naked; he who despises them is wealthy. So true is it that each one's own feeling is his hardship, and each one's own virtue is free from injury. So Christ first claimed for himself in marriage, through the Law, the human condition, which he later repudiated. Hence he also says: What is the bill of your mother's divorce, by which I put her away? In this way, then, Christ saw his household naked and loved it; for Christ loves a holy soul. Accordingly, Jesus loved Lazarus and Mary; Christ loved his Church, although naked, although still clothed with no adornment of virtues.

42. Accordingly, so that we may know that the sequence of the Scriptures agrees with the dispensation set before us, let us learn that the Church was naked, let us learn that she was seeking, hastening and washing herself before the house of Christ, when John was baptizing in the Jordan, saying: I indeed baptize you in water unto repentance, but he who comes after me is mightier than I, whose shoes I am not worthy to carry. He will baptize you in the Holy Spirit. So when the people were being baptized unto repentance, the Church, already near to his house, was surely seeking Christ, that she might attain to grace. So the Church was seeking Christ in John, saying: Announce to me him whom my soul has loved, recalling that he himself was the cause of her desire, he himself the cause of her washing, as you have it: I am dark and comely, daughters of Jerusalem, like the tent of Kedar, like the skin of Solomon. You have the reason why she longs to wash herself: because she recalls that she is dark. For, as though to John asking why the great multitude, flocking together, longed to be washed, she answered, saying: I am dark and comely, daughters of Jerusalem. Because she is dark, she desires to be washed; because she is comely, she does not fear to be seen naked. Do not look, she says, at me, for I have been darkened, because the sun has not looked upon me. So we are darkened when we are not seen by Christ, but when we are seen, we grow white. So he saw her, he to whom all things are naked and from whom the inner things of the breast cannot be hidden, because he is the searcher of heart and reins; nothing is covered to him, nothing wrapped up. He saw his Church naked and, as he saw, he loved her. He saw his beloved naked and, as a son of love, he loved her.

43. See how he invites her, see how he calls her. Here are not the reproaches of adultery, but the mysteries of chastity. You are all, he says, beautiful, my near one, and there is no reproach in you. Come hither from Lebanon, bride, come hither from Lebanon; you will pass over and pass through from the beginning of faith. And well does he say "from the beginning of faith". So where you have faith, you ought not to fear adultery; for faith belongs to marriage, fraud to adultery. But so that she might be able to come from Lebanon, he said beforehand: Arise, come, my near one, my beautiful one, my dove, my perfect one. Near, surely, by the desire of faith; beautiful by the comeliness of virtue; a dove by spiritual grace. For the wings indeed of a dove covered with silver signify that eternal power, and the flight of the dove declared the presence of the Holy Spirit. So Christ calls her to himself, that she may come, since she was already coming endowed with spiritual riches, because behold, he says, the winter has passed, the rain has gone, it has gone its way, flowers have been seen on the earth. See how the holy Church is invited. The winter, he says, has gone its way, so that, naked, she may not dread the winter: a winter not of season but of weakness, which despoils the fruitful field of the soul of every flower. For there is a winter not of the earthly sun; there is a winter of the mind, when cold steals into the mind, when the warmth of the mind vanishes, when the vigour of understanding is loosened, when excessive moisture overflows and weighs down the mind, when the inner sight grows dark. And therefore the Lord says: See that your flight be not in winter or on the sabbath. For it is good that the day of judgment or of death should come at the time when the gentle temperateness of the mind is in its vigour, when the heavenly mystery shines with serene light, when our heart burns within us. For then Christ is present, as Ammaon and Cleopas testify in the Gospel, saying: Was not our heart burning within us on the way, when he had opened the Scriptures to us? And the mind is in its vigour when a flower, too, is seen on the earth. Who is this flower of good fragrance but he who said: I am the flower of the field and the lily of the valleys? Of whom it is also written in Isaiah: A rod shall come forth from the root of Jesse, and a rod shall rise up from the root, and a flower shall come forth from the rod. The root, surely, is the family of the Jews, the rod Mary, the flower of Mary Christ; and when he shines forth in our earth and is fragrant in the field of the soul or blooms in his Church, we can neither fear the cold nor dread the rain, but can await the day of judgment.

44. And therefore the Church, in order to see this flower, hastened with all eagerness, as she herself testifies, saying: In my bed by night I sought him whom my soul has loved. I sought him and did not find him; I called him, and he did not hear me. I will rise, then, and go into the house, into the market-place and into the streets, and I will seek him whom my soul has loved. I sought him and did not find him. The watchmen who go about the city found me; they struck me and took my cloak from me. You see how she seeks him whom she desires to find, so that she does not fear to be wounded. But these wounds are not to be feared but to be desired, because they are wounds of love, as she herself said: I am wounded with love. Good are the wounds of love. Accordingly, more useful are the wounds of a friend than the voluntary kisses of an enemy.

Chapter IX

45. Rightly naked, then, because she lost her cloak; or perhaps naked because it sometimes belongs to virtue not to have a covered breast, not to have a veiled heart. Accordingly, that veiled one, that is, the synagogue, which had a veil in the reading of the Old Testament, a veil which is not lifted, since it is done away in Christ — that veiled synagogue, I say, Christ despised and spurned; whence even today a veil is laid upon the heart of the Jews. But she who is turned to the Lord with her whole mind is naked and open to view. For when anyone has turned to the Lord, the veil will be taken away, so that, beholding the glory of God, we may be able to see. Accordingly, elsewhere too to be naked is a sign of virtue, for it is written: I have put off my tunic, how shall I put it on? I have washed my feet, how shall I defile them?

46. What tunic, then, she put off and did not find how she might put it on, let us, if we can, reveal from the Scriptures. For there is a bodily tunic; there are coverings woven of desires; and therefore it is sometimes better to be naked in body than veiled in heart. Hence Paul too admonishes us that it is much better to be stripped than to be clothed over, saying also elsewhere: Stripping off the old man with his deeds, put on the new, who is renewed unto knowledge according to the image of him who created him. So she who has been stripped also has her feet washed, and therefore she who has washed does not know how she could be defiled; for through grace she forgets what she had drawn in through nature. Great, then, and just is this love of the woman washing herself before David's palace.

47. But she not only washed, but also called, as we receive it in the same book, where the holy Church says: Come, my brother, let us go out into the field, let us rest in the villages, let us rise early to the vineyards, let us see whether the vines have flowered. So she not only washes, but also summons and invites Christ to come to her, saying: To you I will give my breasts. And not only her own, but she also promises things new and old, as you have it: New and old, my brother, I have kept for you. And as though impatient of love she seeks the support of someone by whom Christ may be asked to come. See her burning, see her longing. Who will give, she says, you, my brother, sucking the breasts of my mother? And she shows for what reason she seeks and with what grace she invites and how she can hold him, and she shows how she awaits him while he lingers outside, and she implores him to enter her house, saying: Finding you outside I will kiss you, I will take you and bring you into the house of my mother and into the secret chamber of her who conceived me. You see how she is naked, she who cannot keep covered the secret of a nature surely undefiled, the secret of an inmost conscience not stamped by any recklessness of vices. For the holy Church and spotless virginity is a garden enclosed, which therefore deserves this grace from Christ, because it sought, desired and found the Word of God, keeping watch before the doors of wisdom, as she herself says: Blessed is the man who hears me, and the man who will keep my ways, keeping watch at my doors daily, guarding the posts of my entrance; my goings out indeed are the goings out of life.

48. Thus Christ desired the beauty of his Church and prepared to take her to himself as wife. But because she was under the Law — for Peter, John and the other apostolic men were under the Law —, he thought that we must first be released from the bonds of bodily observance. For although the Law is religious, the Law is just, as the person of Uriah is presented, because he was so religious and chaste that on returning from the war he did not know his wife again nor go in to her, yet when Uriah came to know of the union of David, that is, of the humble man, the Law was departing from its conjunction with the synagogue and preparing a place for the future wedding of the Church. Hence John too received the type of the Law, who, although he was of the fathers, announced that the ways of the Lord were being prepared and prophesied the union of the Church, and therefore he is presented as slain, to show the failing of the lawful observance; for the Law and the prophets were until John. So Uriah has now been slain in type of the Law, so that the synagogue might be loosed from the snares of the Law, since the woman who is under a husband is bound to the Law while the husband lives, but if the husband has died, she is loosed from the law of the husband. Therefore, while the husband lives, she will be called an adulteress if she has been joined to another man; but if her husband has died, she is freed from the law of the husband, so that she is not an adulteress if she has been with another man. So the human condition is not an adulteress: though it was under the Law, yet, the observance of the Law having been in a certain way abolished, it has been claimed for grace.

49. Yet it was not Christ who abolished the observance of the Law. Hence too it was not David who killed Uriah, but he allowed him to be killed by the fighters; that is, Christ allowed the rite of the observance that came from the Law to be profaned through the incursion of the barbarians and the captivity of the Jews. Accordingly, Uriah means "my light". And what is the light of Christ but the Law and the Gospel? — For if you believed Moses, you would believe me also; for he wrote of me — because the light of Christ went before in the Law, and afterwards his grace in the Gospel, as in a seven-lamped Spirit, filled all things of this world. From this Law, then, he took away the flesh and joined the Church to himself. The light of this Law the hostile people diminished, when they violated the holy things of the Law. So the light of God was diminished in the people of the Jews, because blindness has befallen Israel in part, until the fullness of the Gentiles should come in, and so all Israel should be saved.

Chapter X

50. There still remains a fourth mystery, which I beg you to receive with calm ears, weighing our mind, not our words; for the kingdom of God is not in word but in power. Adultery was committed, murder was committed. This is what was said to the prophet: Take for yourself a staff of nut-wood. And elsewhere we read of a rod of nut-wood, by which we understand a certain law and the sum of prophecy, which, after the manner of a nut, is bitter outside in the rind, hard in the middle in the shell, and tender and fruitful within. Even if, then, in the history you hear things rather bitter, and in the type you recognize things hard, yet hope for things fruitful in the mystery. Adultery, I say, was committed in a type of salvation; for not every adultery is to be condemned. Accordingly, it was said to the prophet: Go, take for yourself a wife of fornication. The Lord commands this, that there be marriage with her who had committed fornication, a marriage whose offspring, as we said above, is Christ. For to the son who was born of fornication the name Jezreel was given by the Lord, which means "divine offspring". If, then, that union with a harlot was godly, this partnership too is surely a godly union with an adulteress. But that concerns the Jews. Of divine things, however, I do not dare to speak of an adultery, though a godly one, lest even the sound of the word offend anyone, though a more reverent sense is ready to hand. Yet, as may be said more cautiously, if not more expressly: from unequal unions a godly cohabitation was made when the Word was made flesh. For the partnerships of divinity and flesh are not lawful, nor do the divinity and the flesh keep, as it were, the law of a lawful marriage-bed, as by a certain covenant of nature flesh is joined to soul and soul to flesh. God took up flesh, assumed a soul; through an unwonted and not lawful incarnation he made the partnership to be lawful, so that God may be all and in all.

51. Accordingly, so that you may know that it is a mystery, interpret the names. For you take David in type of Christ; Bathsheba is called "daughter of the sabbath" and "full daughter" and "well of the oath". What, then, is more express than that the daughter of the sabbath is the flesh of Christ, because God sent his Son, made of a woman, made under the Law? The same is full, because in his passion is the fullness of the Law, or because she is full of the Holy Spirit; for Jesus, full of the Holy Spirit, returned from the Jordan. The same is also the well of the oath, that is, of religion and faith. And rightly a well, because rivers of living water shall flow from her belly. Assuming her, then, to himself, God the Word made the union lawful, a mystery which that wedding also declared which is in the Song of Songs, in which the Church is wedded to Christ and the flesh to the spirit. And therefore she ran about and sought the Word of God everywhere, because, wounded, naked, adulterous in all, though spotless in Christ, the wretched flesh was seeking a redeemer.

52. Christ joined her to himself, to render her spotless; he made her his partner, to take away the adultery. And because she was under the Law, she had to die, so that she might be freed from the Law, so that through that death a kind of marriage, as it were, of the Law and the flesh might be dissolved. So the flesh died in Christ, so that we, made dead to the Law through the body of Christ, as the Apostle said, might belong to another, who rose from the dead, and that the passions and desires of the flesh and the thoughts of sins, which through the Law were in our members, might die in that death, and that we, loosed from the law of death, might rise again in newness of spirit as by a new union with Christ.

53. And yet those who broke the Law in the flesh of Christ and thought that it was to be violated were all slain by the fighters at David's order. By this sign is shown what they have taught to be marked out for later times: that of those who killed Christ none escaped; and this is most evidently revealed, that those whom Christ had assumed before his death, as being Jews, perished as little ones and unformed, but those whom he enrolled after his death into the number of sons were preserved for the kingdom. Hence, in keeping with this mystery, it is fitting that the first offspring among the Jews was weak, so that afterwards among the Christians there might come an eternal fruit.

Chapter XI

54. But now let us pass on to the messenger. For in what went before our treatise was about that odious adultery and murder of David the prophet, and, having gone through the mysteries, we found not only nothing that should be condemned but even something that should be praised. Now the treatise begins to be about the messenger, whom the title of the psalm that has been gone through, already often repeated, shows. For it runs thus: A psalm of David, of understanding, when Nathan the prophet had come to him, when he had gone in to Bathsheba.

55. And so this part of the history too must be unfolded again; it is of this kind. "A rich man," he says, "and a poor man were within one city. The rich man had many herds, many sheep; the poor man had one sheep, which was fed on the poor man's bread and wine and rested in his bosom. A guest came to the rich man; the rich man took nothing from his herd or from his flocks, but killed the poor man's one sheep. When David learned this, 'That man shall die,' he said, 'and shall restore fourfold.' Nathan answered him: 'You are he.' Then he disclosed to him the heavenly command, saying that the Lord had sent word that he had conferred many good things upon him, that is, royal wealth, when he had been taken from the number of the shepherds, and that he had poured out his mercy upon him, and that therefore, since he had provoked the Lord by sin, all his possessions were to be carried off, to be seized by the enemy, and his house laid waste. Then he said: 'I have sinned'; and Nathan answered: 'Since you confess that you have sinned, the Lord will remit your sin, but your son who was born of Bathsheba shall die.' While this son was sick, David wept, lay on the ground, covered himself with sackcloth, fasted. When he learned that he was dead, he rose, washed, dined, comforted others."

56. See how many things there are here. First of all, that God is loving and merciful, and that the adversities which befall us we receive as the price of our error. We have learned, then, that captivity is the price of sin; for this penalty is decreed for crime. Next we notice this, that the fall of kings is the penalty of peoples. For as we are preserved by their virtue, so too we are endangered by their error. Hence we must pray that we may be able to have a glorious and perfect king. Do we, then, who wish to have a perfect king, not wish to have a perfect Lord? Or can those really wish a man to be perfect who do not wish God to be perfect? The text of the history also briefly brings about this, that we notice how quickly the hope of pardon followed. And it is no small thing that it was Nathan who denounced it to him, that is, a lesser prophet; for it is a grievous shame and disgrace to have one's offence reproved by an inferior. You see that guilt diminishes grace. Nathan knew what David did not know: so much does the mind grow dark when it is overshadowed by a certain cloud of vices. Then, this is the Nathan who in what went before had prophesied that David was acceptable to the Lord, so that he might grieve that his sin was convicted by the same witness by whose prophecy his merit had been proclaimed.

57. But now let us seek the mystery. There were two men, he says, in one city, one rich and the other poor. Who is this rich man or poor man, unless perhaps the one is the people of the Jews, the other the Christian people? That one is rich in the Law, rich in the words of God entrusted to him, rich in prophecies, rich in oracles; but this one is poor. But do not flee from this poverty, which the kingdom of heaven follows; for it is written: Blessed are the poor in spirit, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven. A good poverty, which does not lose what it has had! A good poverty, which, if it has no treasures of money, yet has treasures of knowledge and wisdom! Do not, children, despise poverty as something cheap; this poor man cried and the Lord hears him. Do not envy riches as though they were great; the rich have been in want and have hungered. But if you wish to have good things, seek the Lord; those who seek indeed the Lord shall not be lacking in any good. Therefore do not reject any poor man. Peter was poor and had no small coin to give, but he gave salvation, more precious than all money. Poor was that Lazarus in the Gospel; but he who before reclined rich in crimson and purple wished, after the end of his life, to be refreshed by the dripping finger of the poor man. Although that rich man, proud in royal crimson and purpled garments, fastidious, shuddered at the poor man's wounds, yet, placed in the underworld, he wished he had been in the place where Lazarus was. In this place the reading does not seem to describe a rich man and a poor man in terms of money, but that poor man who for faith and devotion did not shudder at the wounds of the body, and endured hunger and fasting.

58. Though, then, the people of the Jews had in its flocks very great stores of victims, yet it snatched away the one little sheep which that poor people had, subjecting it to that glorious passion. Do you wish to know what sheep? He was led like a sheep to the sacrifice, he who was fed on the bread of the poor man, and used the drink of the poor man, and slept in the bosom of the poor man. For he used our food, he rested in the lap of our breast. For he made mention that he could not rest in the bosom of the Jews, saying: The Son of Man has not where to lay his head.

59. And there came a guest, he says, to that rich man. Who is this guest if not wretched sin? For guilt entered into this world like a guest, foreign and alien to our nature. And so he offered no victim from his own flocks, but took the poor man's little sheep and killed it. And although its passion availed for the sanctuary of salvation, yet to him it seems to have been an increase of his own fury, and food for guilt, and an incitement to sin. In fact, Judas received bread from Christ and then was the more filled with the devil, because he did not receive it in faith, he who was preparing betrayal for so hospitable a Lord.

60. And David was angry with that man. We have said that by the prophet the people of the Jews is signified, and so under this figure it condemns itself. That man who has done these things is worthy of death, and he shall restore the little sheep fourfold: that is, the people of the Jews shall perish, but the Christian people shall enjoy more abundant good things, because the state that is owed from the resurrection is more excellent than present things.

61. It was my purpose, as you will deign to remember, to answer with my discourse those who condemn the murder and adultery of holy David; and by the favour of your unanimity the course of our treatise has so turned out that he whom we thought could not be defended seemed to all rightly worthy to be proclaimed. But since the holy man does not need our help, let him now speak to you for himself, and let his own words defend his deed.

Chapter XII

62. Have mercy on me, he says, O Lord, according to your great mercy. This joining of words is new, if I am not mistaken, dearest brethren, that mercy should be called great, and I do not remember having readily read it elsewhere. And therefore we ought to consider what great mercy is. For even if we do not readily read of great mercy, yet we read of the great power of God. And therefore, by considering what great power is, we shall be able to conjecture what he wished to designate as great mercy. For we read in holy Jeremiah: Who are you, O Lord? You made heaven and earth in your great power and with your lofty arm. Great power, then, made heaven. What is this great power, the Father's or the Son's? Certainly the Father's is great power — for he himself made heaven and earth through the Son, as you have it: In the beginning God made heaven and earth — but yet the Son too is great power. For when you read that the Son is the power of the Father, as you have Christ the wisdom and power of God, surely, since the Son is the power of the Father, the Son is great power. How, then, is he denied to be almighty whom we confess to be the great power of God? This great power, then, made heaven, because the Son too made heaven, as you have it: All things were made through him. Therefore if both the Father made it and the Son, surely the unity of the deed indicates the unity of operation. The operation of the Father and of the Son, then, does not differ. But if the operation is one, surely the power of the operation is one, and the majesty of the things made is one. So much concerning the Father and the Son.

63. Why, then, are we silent about the Spirit, especially since divine Scripture does not suffer us to be silent? For the passage gives a most fitting testimony for proving the unity of the Holy Spirit with the Father and the Son, so that we cannot separate his operation either from the work of the Father and the Son. For we read that the power of the Father is great, in that he made heaven, and that the Son too is great power, since he made heaven, and not only made it but also made it firm; by the word indeed of the Lord the heavens were made firm. Therefore, if both to have made and to have made firm the heaven belongs to great power, neither can we separate the Holy Spirit from the majesty of great power, because it is written: and by the spirit of his mouth all their power. For when heaven and earth were being made, the Spirit was borne above, of whom he says elsewhere through the prophet David: Send forth your spirit, and they shall be created. Of whom he also said elsewhere: For I shall see the heavens, the works of your fingers. For God did not make heaven and earth with bodily fingers, but by the sevenfold grace of the Spirit, namely by that finger of which you have it in the Gospel: But if I cast out demons by the finger of God. For this finger he elsewhere called the Spirit, as you have it: But if I cast out demons by the Spirit of God. If, then, the Spirit is the finger of God, since the Son is the arm of God, surely the Spirit, working together with the Father and the Son through the unity of operation, made heaven and earth. For the Son called him the finger for this reason, that he might express the unity of the divinity as of one body. And so, if great power founded heaven, surely great wisdom founded heaven, because it is written: In wisdom you have made all things.

64. We have learned, then, what great power is: let us now gather what great mercy is. If great power made the heavens, great mercy too must be from heaven, and great justice must be from heaven; justice indeed has looked forth from heaven, and mercy came from heaven. Great, then, was the mercy that was done, because the Word was made flesh and dwelt in us. Therefore great power made the heavens, great power bowed the heavens, as we read: And he bowed the heavens and came down. By this it is shown that the Son of God was never without a share in his divinity, not even when he dwelt among men, since, even if he took up what he was not, yet he did not cease to be what he was. And so, when it is said: And he bowed the heavens and came down, he seems not so much to have come down from heaven as to have come down with heaven itself. For when the Father addressed the Son set on earth, and angels ministered to him, the Son of God seems not so much to have changed his seat as to have moved it. Therefore we have learned that the mercy is great.

65. And so, if great mercy is to have come down from heaven, we must distinguish and understand what the multitude of compassions is. For thus it follows: And according to the multitude of your compassions blot out my iniquity. For the two verses do not signify the same thing: the former is referred to the incarnation, while this, which follows, seems to be referred to those injuries which the Son of God took up in the flesh, because he fasted, because he hungered, because he wept, because he was scourged, because he was crucified, because he died, because he was buried; for these are the marks of the flesh, not of the divinity. And it was necessary that the multitude of compassions should drive away the multitude of hardships, the contest of the Lord's passion being destined to profit us.

66. For he was not blotting out his own sins, because he had committed no sin; but because he was made sin, our sins had to be blotted out, as David said: Blot out my iniquity. What is blot out? Let us look at this word; it is not idle. In fact, elsewhere too he says: It is I, it is I who blot out your iniquities, and I will not be mindful. There are surely certain sores of offences deeply impressed on our conscience, and certain weals on our minds and souls, which are overlaid with the sores of our errors. Guilt, then, has its own characters and letters, by which it is betrayed. This we do not invent by our own ingenuity, but we seal it with prophetic authority. It is written, then; and see where it is written. On the breast he says of your heart, that is: the record of guilt is written there, where the form of virtue is also, not on tablets of stone, but on fleshly tablets of the heart. But the things that are good are not written in with ink, but with the Spirit of the living God, while the things that are vicious with an iron stylus and an adamantine nail. The writing is almost such as the penalty of sin is. The one is written with an iron stylus, the other is restrained with an iron rod, as it is written: You shall rule them with a rod of iron. Different is that other one, the straight rod, the rod of your kingdom. The one restrains the guilty with punishment, the other governs the innocent with command. Guilt, then, is written; let us see where. Not outside, but within, in your heart and in your breast. For as the word is near, in your mouth and in your heart, so in unbelievers the figure of sin and the image of error are impressed in the heart itself. Within, then, is guilt; within is virtue. And therefore the Lord, I perceive, he says, that virtue has gone out from me, so that he might show that the things which are good proceed from within, and, on the other hand, that the things which are evil come forth from within. For not what enters, he says, into the mouth defiles a man, but what goes out of the mouth. For from your heart thoughts proceed, and therefore beware lest evil thoughts come forth from your heart which may convict and accuse you at a later time. For God does not need testimonies and proofs to refute you; your own guilt itself accuses and betrays you.

67. Hence David rightly says in this psalm: My offence is always over against me. For woe to me, that I desire to lie hidden and cannot lie hidden! For how shall I lie hidden, who bear inscribed on my breast the marks of my offences? On that day of judgment the breast of each one will be stripped bare, his own conscience rendering testimony before all, and his thoughts accusing one another or even defending, on the day on which God will judge the hidden things of men. What shame will that be, when what we thought hidden begins to be betrayed to all, when the image of each one's offence begins to lay itself open, so that each one is convicted by the record of his own crime? What shame, when you see preferred that man whom you judged despicable in this world, that servant of yours, when you learn that he whom, by the cunning of this world, you considered foolish and unfit, is honoured for the riches of his simplicity? Therefore, while we live, let us take refuge with him who can blot out sin. And do not fear that it is written with an iron stylus and an adamantine nail — for he has broken the gates of iron and of adamant — and do not be afraid to confess your guilt.

68. David confessed, he acknowledged his iniquity, he acknowledged his error, and therefore he says: For I acknowledge my iniquity. David acknowledges, and do you not acknowledge? David confesses, and do you deny? Paul cries out that he is guilty, and do you assert that you are innocent? There is, then, no very great cause for blushing, since our partnerships are mixed with very many sins, provided, however, that we follow the zeal for correcting [lacuna] nor again should you be afraid that the confession of guilt may be held liable to punishment. For the good and merciful God is wont not only to pardon guilt to those who confess, but also to bestow rewards on those who correct themselves, provided that each one asks that what he understands is not hidden be granted to him. Tell, then, your iniquities, that you may be justified. This is the voice of God, who wishes to pardon; this is the voice of God, who promises that he wishes to blot out your sin.

69. Against you he says I have sinned and done evil before you, that is: even if men do not see, yet you see; even if men cannot judge me, since they are partners in offences, yet you are the judge of each one, you who are free of offences. However much harmful thoughts may lie hidden in the inmost heart, yet they are before you, who can say: Why do you think evil things in your hearts? Surely it is Christ who says this. He, then, who knows what is written in the heart can blot out whatever is inscribed in the heart. To him holy David confesses that he has sinned, not only in himself but also in the first man, when the divine precepts are violated.

70. Here one may notice, dearly beloved brethren, how all heresies, while they attack one another, run back into one another. And so the Arians, while they separate the power of the Father and of the Son, rush madly into such an outcome of their disputation that their assertion falls in with the Manichaeans; for these say that there is one God of the Old Testament, another of the New. This profane assertion the holy Church condemns, which reads of one God, because one God the Father, from whom are all things, and we unto him, and one Lord Jesus, through whom are all things, and we through him. Surely the power of the Father and of the Son is declared almighty and one, so that we neither set the Father apart from the grace of the New Testament nor, again, separate the Son from the establishing of the world.

71. In fact, let us consider whether the same God is the founder of both Testaments. Surely we have all sinned in the first man, and through the succession of nature the succession of guilt too has been poured from one into all. Against whom, then, did I sin, against the Father or against the Son? Surely against him who entrusted to me that which I sinned by not keeping. It was commanded to man that he should taste of all the things that were in paradise, but not touch the tree of the knowledge of good and evil. Adam, then, is in each one of us. For in him the human condition offended, because through one man sin passed over into all. I see the sum entrusted to me, I see what debts of transgression I have contracted while I taste things forbidden and prohibited. Hence I owe the interest on the principal committed to me, because I could not keep with inviolate faith the loan at interest of the heavenly commandments that was entrusted to me.

72. We have acknowledged what was entrusted; let us acknowledge the creditor too. Surely it is the creditor's right both to exact and to remit. And therefore the same one remits who has the right of exacting. When, therefore, Christ has said to me in the Gospel: Your sins are remitted to you, do I not understand that the same one is lord of the principal whom I recognize as the arbiter of pardon? Or if the Father lent and the Son remitted, not through unity, as we say, but, as the Arians assert, through a difference of power, did then one God lend and another remit? Whence they run into this, that they do not escape the faithlessness of the Manichaeans. Next, in saying that one God is good, let them notice into what snares of their own madness they fall. If he who lent is good, how is he not good who remitted? Therefore let the Arians have their portion with those with whose assertion they agree. Nor is it surprising if he who has once begun to stray from the truth entangles himself in the knots of another's error.

73. And therefore there is one judgment which destroys the engines of all the heretics: that we believe the Trinity to be of one power, majesty and virtue, and therefore neither separate the Father from what the Son did nor set the Son apart from what the Father commanded. For so it will come about that we do not bring in one God of the Old Testament and another of the New, but that through the unity of power both the Son is understood in the Father and the Father in the Son, and the Son lends at interest in the Father and the Father remits in the Son. For the Father too forgives sin: that the Son has forgiven we have already said.

74. Learn that the Father too forgives. Forgive he says us our debts, as we also forgive our debtors. Surely the Son says this to the Father in our person. Nor does he say it as though he himself could not forgive, but he says it so that you may understand the unity of power.

75. In fact, if you seek authority in the Son, Now you he says are clean because of the word which I have spoken to you. He forgives by a word, he forgives by command, but he forgives with reason, saying: Go, see that after this you do not sin. You have authority, because he pardoned the things of the past; you have judgment, because he gave a rule for the things to come.
