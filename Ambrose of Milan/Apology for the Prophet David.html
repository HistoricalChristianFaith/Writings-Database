Chapter I

APOLOGY FOR THE PROPHET DAVID, TO THEODOSIUS AUGUSTUS

I, 1. We have taken up our present pen to write an apology for the prophet David, not because he stands in need of this service, he who shone with such great merits and flowered with virtues, but because many, having read the record of his deeds without looking into the force of the Scriptures or the hidden things of the mysteries, wonder how so great a prophet did not avoid the contagion first of adultery and then of homicide.

2. Therefore it has been our concern to review the story itself, which seems to have lain open to sin. For in the second book of Kingdoms we read that David, walking about in his royal house, looked out and saw a woman bathing — her name was Bathsheba — of outstanding beauty, with comeliness of countenance and a face exceedingly fair; and, softened by these allurements, he had conceived the desire of possessing her. Now the woman was married to a husband; Uriah was the husband's name, and for him, by royal commands, the scene of his death was contrived. For although his life was reckoned no hindrance at all to desire, it was yet reckoned a very great obstacle where the adultery's shame was concerned.

3. And so, to begin from the plainer things: him whom God has justified, you judge. It is a very small thing to me, says Paul, that I should be judged by you or by a human day; but neither do I judge myself . And he was still set in the body, still exposed to temptation, yet he did not judge himself for this reason, that the spiritual man is judged by no one except by God alone. Then he added: He who judges me is the Lord. Therefore do not judge anything before the time . But David has already fulfilled his time and has merited grace and has been justified by Christ, since the Lord himself rejoiced to be called the son of David, and those who confessed him so were enlightened. Why do you summon the man of God from reward to judgment? The Lord has already judged concerning him, of whom he said to Solomon: If you walk in my sight, as David your father walked in holiness of heart and justice, to this end, that he might do according to all that I commanded him . Is he then worthy of judgment or of reward, who did according to all the heavenly commands, walking in holiness and justice of heart? Where the sins and vices of others lie hidden, there David received the divine testimony of his virtue and of his glory.

And we idly dispute about his sin, for whose merit and grace the sins of others have been relieved. For when Solomon had given offense because he had not kept the commands of the Lord, and God had determined to tear his kingdom into many parts, he said to him: Yet in your days I will not do these things, for the sake of David your father. From the hand of your son I will take it. Yet I will not take the whole kingdom; one sceptre I will give for the sake of David my servant . Since, then, the Lord justifies him, who is it that judges so great a man? What God has cleansed, he says, do not call common .

4. Saving, however, the heavenly judgment, by which you yourself also may honour so great a prophet, enter into his acts and his character. Do you not marvel at him, and judge a man worthy to be made equal even to the angels, who, spending the greater part of his life, indeed from boyhood, amid riches, honours and powers, and placed in many temptations, gave place to error only once, and to that error by which even the angels of heaven, as Scripture records, were cast down from their grace and power? Certainly another error of his is also read of, that he had the people numbered.

Chapter II

II, 5. How many transgressions each one of us commits hour by hour, and yet each one of the common people does not think that his sin should be confessed! That king, so great and so powerful, did not allow the consciousness of his transgression to remain with him even for a brief moment, but by an early confession and immense grief he rendered his sin to the Lord. Whom could you easily find for me now, a man of rank and wealth, who would not take it ill if he were accused as guilty of some fault? But he, illustrious with royal power, approved by so many divine oracles, when he was rebuked by a private man for having grievously transgressed, did not roar with indignation, but confessed and groaned out the grief of his fault. And so the grief of his inmost feeling moved the Lord, so that Nathan said: Because you have repented, the Lord also has taken away your sin . The swiftness of the pardon, then, made plain that the king's repentance had been profound, since it carried away the offense of so great an error.

6. Other men, when they are rebuked by the priests, make their sin heavier while they desire to deny or defend it, and their fall is greater there where correction is hoped for. But the saints of the Lord, who are eager to complete the holy contest and to run the course of salvation, if ever perchance they have fallen, as men, through the frailty of nature rather than through the lust of sinning, rise again keener for the race under the goad of shame and renew greater contests, so that not only are they reckoned to have suffered no hindrance from their fall, but they even seem to have heaped up incentives to speed. If, then, the course of the runners is not broken off when some perhaps have fallen, nor the struggle of the wrestlers, but the contests remain unimpaired, and indeed many after one fall or another have conquered with greater grace, how much more ought those who have entered the contest of devotion not to be judged by the offense of a single slip, since he is blessed who has been able to restore himself after a fall, for to rise again even after death is the gift of the blessed.

7. In other respects too we can understand that sin is profitable, and that it was by the providence of the Lord that transgressions crept upon the saints. For they have been set before us for imitation, and therefore care was taken that they too should sometimes fall. For if they had finished their course unharmed by vices amid so many slippery places of this world, they would have given us, who are weaker, occasion to reckon that they were of some higher and divine nature, so that they could not admit transgression or have any share in fault; and this opinion would surely have called us back, as having no part in that substance, from an impossible imitation. The grace of God therefore passed them by for a little while, so that their life might become for us a discipline for imitation, and that from their acts we might take instruction in repentance as well as in innocence. So while I read of their falls, I recognize them as sharers in our weakness too; while I believe them sharers, I take it for granted that they are to be imitated.

8. The apostle Paul also tells us that the Lord our God took forethought, lest, either through the sublimity of revelations or through the prosperous and unbroken progress of their works, the human disposition should be lifted up even in the saints, and they should credit to themselves and attribute to their own virtue what had been conferred on them by divine working. Therefore, lest they should rush into so great a judgment and fall into the pit of faithlessness, the Lord allowed fault to steal in upon them, so that they too might perceive that they needed divine help and might recognize that the guide of their salvation must be sought. And so Paul testifies that weakness profited him, when the Lord said to him, as he asked that the goad of his flesh might depart from him: My grace is sufficient for you; for strength is perfected in weakness . And rightly he glories in weaknesses; for he knew that through abundance of strength very many, even of the saints, had fallen beyond remedy. How much more profitable, then, to have given place to reproof once or twice than to have drawn upon oneself the perpetual offense of the Godhead.

Chapter III

III, 9. Why should I add also what we can infer from the very practice of the world, that when we have proved many men in some office, we wish to try the same men, as diligent and energetic, in another function? How many athletes, when they have prevailed in one kind of combat, are called to another kind of contest! What if the Lord your God too, when you have given some proof of your virtue, wishes to prove you in another kind of virtue? Holy Job, running his course without offense, he nevertheless allowed to be tempted by the death of his children and by sores over his whole body, so that in this too he might prove his virtue, whether even under the compulsion of injuries and bitter pains he would lessen the devotion of his affection. Is it not plain that he wished to prove holy David also, noble in faith, most excellent in gentleness, strong of hand, to see how he would cover his vice and amend his fall, so that he might teach us how we can conceal a sin we have committed?

10. Unless perhaps it should seem to someone a slight cause that so great a prophet should err for the sake of our correction, when for the redemption of all Christ took upon himself our infirmities, he who was made sin for us, though he knew not sin. And is it thought unworthy and not believed likely that David should have fallen into the reproach of one fall for the profit of posterity, when the Lord himself was made a reproach for us, as he himself says: But I am a worm and no man, a reproach of men and the outcast of the people , and elsewhere: And the reproaches of those who reproached fell upon me ? Therefore he sent on before, in his own, the mystery of the dispensation to come; and the little servants indeed bore the sins of their own condition, and therefore they could not themselves be free of sin, but the Lord took up a burden not his own, and therefore he alone was without any share in transgressions.

11. In sum, by the teaching of the apostle too we have learned that very many of the things that were done in earlier times were done in figure. For when he had said that the fathers in the desert, wounded by serpents, could not be healed otherwise than by Moses' hanging up a brazen serpent, at the sight of which those deadly bites and the harmful infusions of venom were cured, he added: Now these things were done in figure for them, for our correction . In figure the brazen serpent was as it were fastened to a cross, because the true one who was to be crucified was being announced to the human race, he who would make void the venoms of the serpent, the devil; accursed in figure, but in truth he who would blot out the curses of the whole world.

Elsewhere too, that is, to the Galatians, he says that Abraham had two sons, one by the slave woman and one by the free , and he added: But he who was of the slave woman was born according to the flesh, and he who was of the free woman through the promise. Which things are said by way of allegory . What 'by way of allegory' means he set out plainly in what follows, saying that those two generations, the one from the slave woman, the other from the free, are two testaments, the one from Mount Sinai, bearing children into slavery , the mountain on which Moses received the law from the Lord, and the other from Jerusalem, which is free, which has borne sons in Isaac, that is, in the freedom of grace, not in the slavery of the letter; for punishment is decreed for slaves, grace is conferred on the free. Was it not in type of a twofold people that Jacob took two wives, from whom he begot different offspring? Why is the patriarch Judah read to have sought to lie with his own daughter-in-law after the death of his son, from which union a birth of twins came forth, unless that a figure might go before of the two testaments of the Lord Jesus, one of which was established in the type of his coming death, the other in the truth of the gospel, namely that two peoples were to be begotten, of which the later would cut through, in the sign of the cross, every hedge and fortification of the earlier people — it is this later people that was first by the hand, later by birth — or else because the Lord Jesus himself, born of the tribe of Judah, sent his works on before him before he was born to us of the virgin?

12. What shall I say of Joseph, who, set upon by his brothers, stripped of his father's garment, cast into a pit and sold into slavery, expressed a clear sign of the Lord's incarnation, in that he, the beloved of the Father, being in the form of God, did not think it robbery to be equal with God, but emptied himself, so that, taking the form of a servant, he came and humbled himself even to the death of the cross, he who, bought and sold by his own brothers, redeemed the human race by his price?

In type of him David, the younger, chosen from among his brothers and anointed to the kingdom, alone, in the peril of a grievous war, set the whole people free by single combat and triumphed in ten thousands, so that the girls sang with timbrels: Saul triumphed in thousands, David in ten thousands . What figure is there in those young girls, if not of souls, which sing together a triumphal psalm to Christ? He begot sons of himself, one incestuous and another a parricide, because an incestuous and parricidal people was to violate the flesh of its own Author fastened to the gibbet of the cross. And so in the third psalm the title of Absalom is set at the head, and the passion of the Lord is prophesied.

13. What shall I say of holy Solomon, whose later deeds are not free of grievous error, and yet the Jewish common folk reckon that he himself came as the Christ? And how many has the offense of his grievous error called back! The greater fault therefore profited the more, lest he should be believed to be above man who had not been free of human vice. There was, then, in him a wisdom that invited envy and a fault that persuaded, which proved him a man.

14. What then hinders us from believing that Bathsheba too was joined to holy David in figure, so that there might be signified the congregation of the nations, which was not united to Christ by any lawful marriage of faith, since it was to enter into his grace through certain side entrances, outside the prescription of the law; in which congregation the bare sincerity of mind and open simplicity, through the justifying mystery of the washing, might win over the mind of the true David and eternal king and provoke his charity? Rightly he came hidden, and so as to deceive the prince of the world, who is like that Uriah, whose name is interpreted "my light", transforming himself into an angel of light. He came, I say, into this world, and he came hidden; he entered as an adulterer, so as to claim his lawful right.

Chapter IV

IV, 15. We have set out strong arguments, as we think, and have proved that the fabric of this story was in figure; now let us go back to what came before and look into the error as though stripped of its spiritual garments. David sinned, as kings are wont to do, but he did penance, he wept, he groaned, as kings are not wont to do; he confessed his fault, he besought forgiveness, lying on the ground he lamented his misery, he fasted, he prayed, and by making his grief public he handed down the testimony of his confession to everlasting ages. What private persons blush to do, the king did not blush to confess. Those who are bound by the laws dare to deny their sin and disdain to ask for forgiveness, which he sought who was bound by no human laws. That he sinned belongs to our condition; that he made supplication belongs to correction. The fall is common, but the confession is singular. To have fallen into fault, then, is of nature; to have washed it away is of virtue. Who glories, he says, that he has a chaste heart ? Not even an infant of one day is declared clean by the testimony of Scripture.

16. Give me anyone without the slip of a transgression. Samson is read of as the strongest of all, who even strangled a lion with his own hands; but would that he had been able to stifle his love! He set fire to the Philistines' harvests, and he himself burned with the little spark of one woman.

Jephthah returned victorious from the enemy, but, bringing back the triumphal standards, he was conquered by his own oath, so that he thought the loving devotion of his daughter, who ran to meet him, must be repaid with parricide. First of all, what need was there to swear so readily and to vow uncertain things as though they were certain, when he did not know their outcome? And then someone renders sorrowful oaths to the Lord God, so as to discharge his vows with bloody funerals!

17. Nor do I think that we must be silent about the priests, lest I seem to cloak our own transgressions. Aaron himself, the high priest, under whose leadership, together with that of Moses, the people of the Hebrews crossed the Red Sea on foot, when he was asked by the people to make them gods whom they might worship, demanded gold and cast it into the fire, and the head of a calf was formed, to which sacrifices were offered. By this sign it became clear that the desire for gold is the material of faithlessness, and that sacrileges are wont to be begotten by the pursuit of avarice. Again, so great a priest fell into an occasion of offense with his sister Miriam. For while both of them disparaged their brother because he had taken a foreign wife, Miriam at once broke out in the contagion of blotched flesh.

18. In this place there was the figure of a manifest mystery: that that priestly people of the fathers would in later times disparage the brotherly people, not knowing the sacrament of that Ethiopian woman. For if it had known it, it would not have found fault with what agreed with the ancient mystery. And so when the Jew says that he who has believed from among the Gentiles is common, and wishes to separate him from the law, he has a leprosy which he will not be able to escape, unless the spiritual knowledge of the law has helped him to pardon.

19. So David also, who knew that he had been born a man liable to fall, asked for pardon, but did not despair of the mercy of the Lord.

Chapter V

V, 20. Nor does the parable seem to differ from the mystery. For who is the rich man but our Lord Jesus, who says of himself, as was read today, that a certain man, being rich, went into a far country to receive a kingdom and to return ? And truly he was rich in the wealth of his majesty and in the fullness of his own divinity, he whom angels and archangels, virtues, powers, principalities, thrones and dominions, Cherubim and Seraphim served with unwearied obedience.

But yet, though he was rich, he left the ninety-nine sheep on the mountains and sought out the one sheep which had remained behind, weary. This sheep the prince of this world, needy and poor in comparison with that rich man, was feeding like a daughter on his own food. Rightly, then, she had grown faint whose sustenance was the food of the world. This sheep had strayed in Adam, enticed by the snares of the serpent.

21. And it was not a bad sheep, for it was full of the word, being the daughter of the rational hebdomad and the gift of a holy Author; yet it was nourished for a long time not on any precious things but on the cheap resources of a poor man. And so it ate, he says, of his bread and drank of his cup and slept in his bosom . Not good is the food of the Ethiopians; harmful is the golden cup, Babylon, which inebriates the nations; sleep is not useful to those who sleep: I would rather keep watch. And so all the foolish of heart were troubled; they slept their sleep and found nothing .

For hospitality's sake, then, because he had received a guest, in order to set a feast before him, he took away that poor man's sheep; for if he had sacrificed some animal from his own flocks or herds, it could not have profited us, whom he would not have redeemed unless he had sacrificed.

22. He therefore took up in his own flesh, with a kind of hospitable affection, the infirmities of our frailty, and to relieve it, or rather to restore it, he offered his flesh to that saving passion, so that he might provide us with the food of eternal life.

And well did Scripture say ewe lamb , because it was the offspring of a virgin. Well is that rich man pronounced worthy of death by the prophetic judgment, for Caiaphas too prophesied, saying: it is expedient that one man should die for the people . But the Lord Jesus alone was chosen as worthy of such a death, by which he would take away the sin of the world. Beautifully too he added: He shall restore the ewe lamb , because he raised up his own flesh, he restored that flesh of virginal integrity. Nor is it idle that he says: He shall restore fourfold , for the resurrection of the dead is fourfold, as the apostle teaches when he says: It is sown in corruption, it rises in incorruption; it is sown in dishonour, it rises in glory; it is sown in weakness, it rises in power; it is sown a natural body, it rises a spiritual body . He will plainly restore the ewe lamb fourfold also in that way in which a man can now say: If I have taken anything from anyone, I restore fourfold . Fittingly too he added: because he did not spare ; for Christ did not spare himself, so that he might come to the help of all.

23. And therefore it was said to him by the Lord Jesus Christ, to his servant David, so as to declare the mystery: Because you did this in secret, I too will do this thing before all Israel in the sight of this sun . And at first indeed, not knowing the sacrament, David was moved with indignation, but he did not err in his feeling. But afterwards, when he recognized the great mystery — for it is the great sacrament of Christ and the Church — seeing the remission of all sins that was to come, and foreseeing the splendour of grace through the washing of regeneration and the infusion of the Holy Spirit, he said, secure of pardon: I have sinned against the Lord , so that he too might come into the fellowship of those to whom the remission of fault would come. Do you notice how he lamented his own sin? Who, then, would wonder that it was forgiven him?

Chapter VI

VI, 24. Now let us consider his works, by which he was able to cover his sin. For since human frailty cannot be without sin, we must take care that our sins be not more than our works of virtue. Holy Paul expressed this with the great force of his wisdom, saying: The sins of some men are manifest, going before to judgment, and some men they also follow after , that is: no one is found untouched by fault; a man has good merits, he has also vices and sins. And so all that is ours is weighed as in a balance. If, then, the sins outweigh the good deeds, they go before to judgment; for sins sink down as into the depths, and those sink down which are manifest either by their weight and bitterness or by their multitude. But some, he says, they also follow after , that is, those who have conducted themselves soberly but through the frailty of their condition have sometimes given place even to error: good deeds go before them, evil deeds follow. These are more honourable, but nevertheless men who have fallen through lighter vices and errors. So sins follow the just, they do not go before them; they go before the unjust. The sins that sink down outweigh, and whatever has been rightly done follows, weighed down as it were by a kind of prejudgment of the sins going before. Likewise also good deeds are manifest . For the works of virtue shine, and the splendour of merits. And those that are otherwise cannot be hidden . So such things are not covered; the charity which conceals a multitude of sins does not overshadow them, the grace of good works does not conceal them, a multitude of virtues does not hide them: they are exposed as though naked and uncovered. For there is none among them who says: Under the shadow of your wings protect me ; for the cross of the Lord abolishes and hides all errors.

25. Who, then, has concealed and covered more than holy David, who also says elsewhere: And in the shadow of your wings I have hoped, until iniquity pass by , and who so loved the Lord that by his exceeding charity he covered and hid every sin? For if the holy apostle Peter abolished his fall by the confession of charity, and he, questioned by the Lord, who said to him a third time: Simon, son of John, do you love me? , so that him whom he had denied three times he might confess three times, and thus by a kind of threefold veil of love hide the fall of his threefold denial: if Peter, because he wept once, obtained pardon, how much more David, who washed his bed every night and watered his couch with tears, whose tears were his bread day and night, who ate ashes like bread and mingled his drink with weeping. For if Jesus has mercy on him who has turned and groaned, if he looked upon Peter and Peter wept, how much less did he who wept long withdraw from the sight of the Lord? Peter denied and did not weep, because Jesus had not looked back at him; he denied a second time and did not weep, because the Lord had not looked back at him; he denied a third time, Jesus looked back, and at once he wept, and wept most bitterly. And therefore David, who was always weeping, said: My eyes are ever toward the Lord ; he who was always seen by Christ said: Through the outlets of waters my eyes have gone down .

26. But now let us also consider his deeds. Who would not let the praise of such great virtues overshadow the odium of one crime? Chosen by divine examination, he at once proved that he was not unworthy of so great a judgment. He went forth into battle and, while the others trembled, he alone, with faith and valour joined together, laid low the Philistine Goliath, terrible for the boasting of his words and the bulk of his monstrous body. The fortitude of one became the victory of all. Let there be compared, if you please, a private crime and the triumph of all, the death of one man and the life of the peoples, of so many whom he freed from death.

27. Let us come to other things. He suffered plots from the king; the king sought to extinguish his life, but when by divine disposition the king was delivered into his power, and lay wholly open to the wound as his companions were about to strike, holy David ran to meet them and turned the deadly wound away from the body of the man about to perish, saying: Do not touch the Lord's anointed. Indeed he even avenged the death of his enemy, lamented his end most tearfully, and long put off the kingdom owed to him, which he knew was owed to him on God's authority. By this alone he taught all men that a kingdom must not be seized beforehand, even if it is owed, but must be waited for, that it may be conferred in its own time.

Would that posterity had imitated this man! We would not have endured such bitterness of wars. You charge that he killed one man; you do not consider that he taught how perpetual peace might be preserved for the Roman world. With what heavy devastation have we paid until now, with what public funeral, as it were, of the whole world have we atoned for the murder of a king who was attacked? Alas, harsh punishments! From this the barbarian enemy still insults us, while arms prepared against him are turned against us. So the public strength has fallen, so Roman valour, broken by its own upheavals, has grown old, while what is received in reverent regard for a father's care is seized by public parricide. And David guarded against this so far that, when he learned that his son Adonijah was usurping the kingdom for himself and forming gatherings, he chose not him who was eager to seize it beforehand, but him who would wait.

28. The mightiest of kings danced before the ark of the Lord, and when he was rebuked by his own wife because he had been stripped before the face of the maidens, he answered: Before the Lord I will be stripped yet more, and I will be a trifler before your eyes, that the Lord, he says, may be honoured, who took me into the kingdom in place of your father , teaching that no regard is to be had for royal power where homage is paid to religion. For it is honourable to act for religion's sake, even if that is unbecoming to power.

29. Look at another memorable thing. A parricidal son had violently invaded his father's kingdom. At first his father gave way to his fury and avoided the place of battle, so that even thus the impious one might come to his senses from his fury. He was unwilling also to take part in the war; he asked those setting out for the battle to spare his son. He who asked them to spare him was both secure of victory and no stranger to devotion, since he did not think that even an impious son ought to perish. He wept and lamented with great mourning the death of the parricide, saying: My son Absalom, who will give me death for you, my son Absalom? He thought that the man who had slain him in vindication of a father's devotion deserved to be punished.

30. And how patient he was of injury and pain! He was giving way, as I have said, to the fury of his son Absalom, flanked on right and left by strong warriors, when a man named Shimei cursed him, calling him bloody and a man of blood, cast down from the kingdom by the just judgment of the Lord; but he was not moved even by such reproaches, though his companions were moved. Finally one of his comrades — Abishai was the man's name — threatened to take off his head as the price of the injury. But the king, turning to Abishai: What is there, he says, between me and you, son of Zeruiah? He curses me for this reason, because the Lord has told him to curse . With what moral wisdom he taught that the times of our injuries or dangers are contests of temptations and trials of probation, and that therefore they are not usually inflicted without divine judgment. The good athlete is exercised by reproaches, exercised by labours and dangers, that he may be worthy to have the crown of justice conferred on him. And therefore the things that are thought adverse are to be borne patiently. Finally, the divine Scripture teaches you this elsewhere too, when the just man says: If we have received good things from the hand of the Lord, why do we not endure the things that are evil?

31. And the holy prophet added, saying: Behold, my son, who came forth from my body, seeks my life. And if the Benjaminite curses me, let him alone, that he may curse, because the Lord has told him, that he may see my humility, and the Lord will repay me for this curse . O depth of prudence, O signal mark of patience, O great discovery for swallowing an insult! Are you moved, he says, Abishai, that a stranger curses me, when my own son seeks me with parricide? The Lord has told him to curse me. But the Lord is not a curser, nor does he delight in insults.

32. See how carefully he observes every point. He does not accuse the Lord as the author of the injury, but rather praises him because he allows us to suffer lesser things, that we may obtain pardon for greater sins. Behold, the insult of words lightened the affliction of the parricidal crime; he acquitted the insolent man, whose curses profit the more, since they are rewarded with a divine recompense. Who would not count such an injury a fair exchange for himself, so that God consoles with the recompense of better things the one whom man has wounded?

Chapter VII

VII, 33. Let us consider his other exploits too. He fought against the race of the Giants, when one of them had almost struck the king as he was engaged in battle; yet the adversary paid for that daring at the price of the death he met.

34. Having won this triumph too over a fierce nation subdued, he again undertook a mighty war in the valley of the Titans, no less against nature than against the enemy. For while he was engaged in the war he was thirsty, and he had nothing to drink. Who, he said, will give me drink from the cistern which is in Bethlehem at the gates? Now between the cistern and holy David lay the enemy, and in between the palisades of the hostile camp. Three men cut through the multitude of the adversaries and drew water from the cistern which was in Bethlehem, and brought it to the king to drink. But the king would not drink, and poured it out to the Lord — for it was worthy of so great a gift that what was the mark of lively valour should become a sacrifice of devotion — and he spoke a sentence worthy of the prophetic spirit: Far be it from me to do this, lest I drink the blood of the men who went at the risk of their lives . So he conquered nature, so that though thirsty he did not drink, and he offered an example in himself by which the whole army might learn to endure thirst. He also trained his subjects in the duty of valour, so that even through dangers soldiers would willingly obey the royal command. And by refusing to drink he declared that he had given the command for the sake of testing his soldiers, not as one conquered by the necessity of thirst; that he had also taken care that no king should seek the enjoyment of drinking at the peril of others; and finally that he had averted a wound to his devout conscience, since water sought with the blood of so many men could not have any sweetness in the drinking, because it had been bought at the cost of the dread of death set before them.

35. But if you wish to look more deeply and see into the mystery, David was thirsting not for water from the cistern which is in Bethlehem, but he foresaw in the spirit Christ, who was to be born of a virgin. He wished therefore to drink not the water of a river but the drink of spiritual grace, that is: he thirsted not for the element of water but for the blood of Christ. And so he did not drink the water offered him, but poured it out to the Lord, signifying that he thirsted for the sacrifice of Christ, not for the stream of nature, for that sacrifice in which there would be remission of sins; that he thirsted for that eternal fountain which would not be sought at the peril of others but would drive away the perils of others.

36. By so many works, then, and so wonderful, do we not believe that the blood of one man was covered? Rightly does the voice of the blood of just Abel cry out to God, because impious Cain was covered by no good works, because he was a fratricide, because he did not confess his shameful deed but denied it. David, it is true, had killed a man who was by no means guilty, but he had killed him not driven by any zeal for cruelty, but to overshadow his shame, to cover the disgrace of his desire. I do not dare to say that he was overwhelmed by the force of crime — for he was not overwhelmed who knew how to raise himself from that ruin of sin — but I do say that he was bent by the force of temptation. For he had said before: Prove me, O Lord, and try me; burn my reins and my heart , and elsewhere: But I said in my abundance: I shall not be moved for ever , and: You have examined me by fire, and iniquity has not been found in me . The Lord willed that he should be subject to temptation, lest he arrogate to himself anything beyond what is man's; for power is made perfect in weakness . Nor did he do it out of a bloodthirsty disposition: nothing can less be ascribed to the holy prophet, who even when departing this life charged his son Solomon with his last words to take away from him the innocent blood which Joab, the commander of his army, had shed, when Abner, though commander of the enemy's force, was attacked by treachery and fell while he was negotiating to enter into alliance. David wept for him and, walking behind his bier with the insignia of his power laid aside, saw to the due rites of his funeral. By this deed he taught that faith promised must be kept even with adversaries, and that valour must be honoured even in an enemy. Did he not, by the legacy of so gentle a devotion of his, wipe away the blemish of this error too?

37. And how splendid it was that, when three conditions were offered him, to choose whichever he wished, after he had incurred offense by numbering the people: when it was put to him whether he wished a famine to come upon the land for three years, or to flee for three months from the face of his enemies pursuing him, or death to come upon the land for three days, he chose the third, because he preferred to commit himself to the power of the Lord rather than to that of men; for the Lord, quickly moved to compassion, would forgive. And so he speaks thus: I am in very great straits in these three, but I will rather fall into the hand of the Lord, for his mercy is very great, than fall into the hands of men . By this humility, prudence and gentleness he brought it about, to use the words of Scripture, that the Lord repented of his own wrath. And so it is written that the Lord repented of the evil .

38. And how admirable is this too, that he offered himself to the angel who was striking the people, saying: This flock, what has it done? Let your hand be upon me and upon the house of my father . By this deed he who was reckoned unworthy of absolution was at once judged worthy of sacrifice. Nor is it any wonder if by such an offering of himself for the people he obtained pardon for his sin, since Moses, by offering himself to the Lord for the error of the people, blotted out even the sins of the people.

39. Did he, then, cover his sins or not? But who would deny it, when this very prophet has taught that iniquities are remitted, that sins are covered and are not imputed by the Lord? He himself taught that his sin had been remitted to him, as it is written: I acknowledge my transgression, and my injustice I have not concealed. I said: I will declare my injustice against myself to the Lord, and you forgave the ungodliness of my heart . If he said: I will declare , and merited pardon before he declared it, how much more, when he made his declaration about himself, saying: I acknowledge my iniquity , was every sin remitted to him. And besides, the prophet Nathan too answered specifically about this: And the Lord has taken away your sin .

40. So he both merited the remission of his iniquity and covered and concealed his sins with charity, and covered them with good works. Nor was the sin imputed to him, because there was in him not the guile of malice but the fall of error; and then, because it was not a surge of wickedness but the shadow of a mystery. And yet he confessed his transgression, acknowledged his iniquity, saw the washing, and saw and believed. He loved much, so that by his exceeding charity he could cover any error whatever.

Chapter VIII

VIII, 41. But now let him defend himself; for he wrote Psalm 50 (51) with reference to that story. And although he set after it the story of his earlier deeds, such as that of the betrayal by Doeg the Syrian, whose title is in Psalm 51 (52), and that of the Ziphites, which seems to be comprised in the title of Psalm 53 (54), he put first this story, which is the later one, since Doeg betrayed him, and the Ziphites too, before the prophet David took up the kingdom, inasmuch as he was still fleeing King Saul and wandering as an exile through various hiding places, whereas he took Bathsheba when he was already reigning.

42. Why, then, does the order of the psalms not also square with the order of the deeds? Because he wished not so much order to agree with order as the mystery with the deeds, and therefore he wished to fit the number of remission to this story. For the number fifty is the number of remission, as the Lord himself taught us in the Gospel, saying: There were two debtors to a certain moneylender. One owed five hundred denarii, the other fifty. When they had nothing with which to pay, he forgave them both. Which of them, then, loves him more?

And in the law you have it that the jubilee is the name given to the number of fifty years, a recurrence very greatly to be celebrated, in which debts are cancelled, the liberties of the Hebrews are confirmed, and possessions are restored.

This number we celebrate with joy after the Lord's passion, when the debt of all our guilt has been remitted and the bond cancelled, and we are free from every tie, and we receive the grace of the Holy Spirit coming upon us: on the day of Pentecost fasts cease, praise is spoken to God, alleluia is sung.

Finally, the father too of a girl betrothed to no one who has suffered intercourse by force receives fifty silver didrachmas, and she herself shall remain in the marriage. By this number, then, even vices are turned into grace. Great, therefore, is the psalm by which we are taught how penance is to be done.

43. Have mercy, he says, on me, O Lord, according to your great mercy; and according to the multitude of your compassions blot out my iniquity. Wash me thoroughly from my injustice and cleanse me from my transgression. For I acknowledge my iniquity, and my transgression is ever against me. Against you alone have I sinned and done evil before you, that you may be justified in your words and may prevail when you are judged. For behold, I was conceived in iniquities, and in transgressions my mother bore me .

Which of us, even if he confesses his transgression, would not think that it should be touched on lightly rather than repeated? Who would repeat it a second time, or a third? See in how many verses so great a prophet makes his sin resound, how no verse is without confession of transgression. He has heaped everything together at once, sounding out his iniquities and his injustice, joining transgressions to sins, and by repeating them often he rightly asks for great mercy, and not only great mercy but also a multitude of compassions. What sin, then, would not such lamentation wash away, what fault would not a prayer of this kind cleanse? He, for one sin, implores a multitude of compassions; we, for many sins, scarcely believe that his mercy should be besought even once.

Then we read that with great power and with his lofty arm he delivered his people from the land of Egypt, when he led them across through the Red Sea, in which there was a figure of baptism. If, then, there was great power in the figure of the sacraments, how much more is there great mercy in their truth. Rightly too is a multitude of compassions asked for there, where there is a multitude of sinners.

44. Wash me thoroughly from my injustice and cleanse me from my transgression . He asks to be washed not so much more often as more fully, that he may wash away the caked filth. He knew that according to the law there were indeed some aids to cleansing, but none full and perfect. To that perfect one, then, he hastens with all his effort, by which all justice is fulfilled, which is the sacrament of baptism, as the Lord Jesus himself teaches. For when he had come to John, the Baptist said: I ought to be baptized by you, and do you come to me? The Lord answered: Permit it now; for thus it becomes us to fulfil all justice . But after Christ was baptized and the Holy Spirit descended upon him as a dove and the Father sealed the Son from heaven, all justice was fulfilled. Therefore the prophet says: Wash me thoroughly from my injustice ; for great squalor and stain is removed not by a scanty washing but by an abundant one.

45. But if anyone wishes to take it otherwise, he can shape his understanding thus. The divine word cleanses, our confession cleanses, the one while it is heard, the other while it is uttered; good thought cleanses, honourable action cleanses, and so does the practice of good conduct. Cleansed by these, each one more easily draws in and, as it were, seizes into himself the splendour of spiritual grace. So it is that the precious dye does not shine out at once from a single dipping of the fleece, but first the fleece is stained with a common juice, then its natural appearance is repeatedly washed out with one juice after another and changed again and again by different colours, and so afterwards the dyeing of a fuller washing, as it were, is applied, that a truer and more perfect brilliance of purple may glow red. As, then, many murex shells are needed in the dyeing of purple, so in the washing of regeneration a multitude of heavenly compassions is needed, that iniquity may be blotted out. And so he who is thoroughly washed from injustice is cleansed from transgression, and puts off a certain habit of sinning that has grown ingrained in his pursuits and conduct, and forgets its quality. And rightly he is washed from injustice or iniquity, which is greater, and cleansed from transgression, which is lesser.

Chapter IX

IX, 46. And therefore he added: For I acknowledge my iniquity, and my transgression is ever against me . It is no small thing for each one to acknowledge his sin. And therefore he also said above: Who will understand falls? That is, who is so great as to understand them? As in that saying: Who will dwell in your tabernacle? , or: Who will ascend into the mountain of the Lord? Not, surely, no one, but few; for he who can acknowledge can turn aside, can choose what he should follow. Many glory in their falls and think those things matters for praise which are matters for accusation: if a man has defiled another's bed and overcome the affection of a chaste woman, if he has by some fraud changed a widow's resolve; another thinks that murder, the ambushes of brigandage and living by plunder count as virtue; some judge that to circumvent and deceive belongs to wisdom. None of these can say: For I acknowledge my iniquity , but only he who can grieve for what he has done and condemn the wrong he has done, he whom his vices prick. Hence the prophet also says: The things you say in your hearts, be pricked for them on your beds as well . Many horses, when they have fallen, are wont to throw themselves about, and though the fall has not harmed them, they weaken and break themselves by their thrashing. Others, such as the nature of Greek horses is said to be, when they have been thrown in a chariot race or have fallen by chance, are wont not to move at all, and keep a certain discipline of quiet and patience. For these, if the fall has done no harm, the quiet does good; at least the hurt is not made worse. Are not those to be reckoned worse than dumb animals who boast in their shameful deeds and think that to be a mark of virtue where there is a fall into crime? Therefore it is said, as to a dumb beast of burden: You have sinned; be still .

47. Hence he finely added: And my transgression is ever against me . For the foolish man delights in his errors and, by overshadowing old sins with new ones, thinks himself helped, and therefore he exults in crime. But the wise man judges that his transgression is against him, and he thinks that the falls of his faults stand against him like hostile battle lines, in the manner of adversaries. Whatever has sounded, whatever has made a noise, his own fault always meets him; whatever has been said or read, he thinks it said against himself; whatever he turns his mind to, he thinks it is he who is marked out by signs, he by eyes. If he feasts, if he thinks, if he prays and entreats, his own error is always before his eyes, and at every moment his fault knocks at his conscience and lets him neither rest nor forget. Like a stern censor, he harasses himself with perpetual terror. So he who is displeasing to himself finds everything adverse; he himself is his own accuser , he himself his own witness, and he who presses and goads himself finds nowhere to flee. But it belongs to a good mind to feel the wound of sin. For those who are without pain do not feel the bitterness of the wound, which belongs to an incurable sickness; but those who are pricked by some pain, just as they do not lack the sense of pain, so they do not lack progress toward health either. For where there is the sense of pain, there is also the sense of life; for to feel belongs to vital vigour and function. Hence he who does not acknowledge his error is mad, raves, is senseless; but he who acknowledges it surely comes to his senses, does not spurn the remedies of health, restrains himself, repents of his fault, always thinks about it and by thinking condemns himself; and the just man moreover is his own accuser at the beginning of his speech . He who accuses himself is just; he who is just is sober, is sound. The just man does not know how to flatter himself; he does not know how to bend the rigour of judgment even in his own case; he shudders at the remembrance of his own fall and blushes at the error he has committed; he dreads, fears, shrinks from every memory of it, judges it grievous to himself, shuns himself as arbiter and does not dare to entrust himself to himself, because he thinks no one more severe toward him than the one from whom he cannot hide, whom he cannot deceive, whom he can find no way to flee and avoid, unless he deny himself and take up the Lord's cross .

48. Great is the force of a guilty conscience, great its torments. Eve and Adam were afraid and, when they heard the voice of the Lord walking in paradise, they wished to hide themselves, though no one was seeking them. Cain too was afraid that everyone who found him would kill him. Thus he bore within himself the sentence that he deserved that no one should pardon him. Hence he says well: And my transgression is ever against me , that is: without any interval the remembrance and the very image of my error assails me. Consider how it confounds us when we commit some transgression, how it rushes upon our eyes, how it keeps coming back into our memory. He who is ashamed of what he has committed does not know how to commit anything like it afterwards, for which he would blush in the same way.

49. Now iniquity goes before, sin follows. Iniquity is the root, and the fruit of the root is fault. Hence iniquity seems to refer to the wickedness of the mind, sin to the slip of the body. Iniquity is graver, as the material of sins; sin is lighter. And so iniquity is remitted through the washing, while sin is covered by good deeds and overshadowed by works as by wings. Hence this very man well said above: Blessed are they whose iniquities are remitted and whose sins are covered . For charity hides error and conceals a multitude of sins . Much charity also of itself remits sin, as it is written of the woman who poured ointment over the Lord: Her many sins are remitted, because she loved much .

50. There are also those who take the first verse to be about the washing, and the second about repentance. For this reason too Peter, who had been baptized before, is asked, after he appeared to have denied the Lord: Simon, son of John, do you love me? He says to him: Surely, Lord, you know that I love you. And again he is asked: Simon, son of John, do you love me? And again he answered: You know, Lord, that I love you. And a third time he is asked: Simon, son of John, do you love me? And he was saddened, because he said to him a third time: Do you love me? and he says to him: Lord, you know all things; you know that I love you . And it was said to him three times: Feed my sheep ; follow me , as to one who had covered his sin by his exceeding charity. Nor is it idly that after the confession of his exceeding charity he is commanded to rule the people, he who even when troubled had not lost the way to rule himself. We have said this for the sake of the grace of charity, because it covers sin. And so some have said that the threefold question about love was put because the denial had been threefold, so that the profession of charity, repeated as many times, might blot out the fall of the threefold denial.

Chapter X

X, 51. There follows: Against you alone have I sinned . He was certainly a king; he himself was bound by no laws, because kings are free from the bonds of transgressions; for they are not summoned to punishment by any laws, being safe in the power of their rule. So he did not sin against man, to whom he was not held liable. But although he was safe in his rule, he was nevertheless subject to God in devotion and faith, and, knowing himself to be subject to his law, he could not deny his sin, but confessed it with bitterness like one accused, since he knew that he was held by greater bonds because he owed greater things, for more is required of him to whom more has been entrusted.

We can also take it this way: Who judges me, when all are under sin? And so the Lord said of that adulteress: Let him who is without sin, he said, be the first to stone her . And no one stoned her. This, then, is what the prophet says: Against you have I sinned , you who alone are without sin. But he who is liable to sin cannot judge another as a sinner. For every man is inexcusable who condemns in another the things that he himself does; for in the very thing in which he judges another he passes sentence on himself.

Against you, he says, have I sinned and done evil before you, that you may be justified in your words and may prevail when you are judged . Against you have I sinned, who called me forth to the pursuits of virtue, who instructed me in your law. Against you alone have I sinned, whom alone the hidden things of the thoughts and the secrets of the mind do not deceive.

52. And I have done evil before you , to whom sanctification alone is fitting. We shun the witness of man, and in your sight we commit things that are unworthy. It is an affront to a man to watch shameful deeds; we know that God is the arbiter of all things, and with him himself as witness we sin.

And yet in these things the Lord our God is all the more justified, because our injustice commends the justice of God and our lie celebrates the truth of God; but let God be true, and every man a liar .

53. That you may be justified in your words . The words of God are full of truth and justice, and therefore whatever the Lord has spoken about human frailty is true: that their heart is inclined to wickedness and leans towards deceit; and that he has laid it down that there is no man who does not sin: All indeed have turned aside and become useless . Therefore he prevails while he is judged, since by the slip of all men he has proved whatever he judged about our deceitfulness. So if we say that we have done iniquity, we justify the Lord in his words; if however we say that we have not sinned, we make God a liar; but for him it is impossible to lie . It is clear, then, that we are all under sin.

54. How great a crime it is, then, for a man to deny that he is a sinner, since, as far as lies in him, he seems to charge the supreme God with a lie and to refute him, who is so moderate and patient that he prevails when he is judged.

For the case comes to judgment, and God says: My people, what have I done to you, or how have I grieved you, or how have I been troublesome to you? For I brought you out of the land of Egypt, and from the house of bondage I delivered you, and I sent before your face Moses and Aaron and Miriam. My people, keep in mind what Balak devised concerning you . One by one he sets his benefits in your sight, so that you may judge, as it were, of the things you ought to have kept, and so be the more guilty, you who could not abide by the divine benefits. What have I done, he says, to you ? He makes himself the defendant, as it were, and you the judge. Or how have I grieved you ? He does not refuse the charge of an offended countenance, if you have been grieved, with God as the cause. Or how have I been troublesome to you ? He confesses the wrong of importunity, if it has been thought too troublesome. He adds his benefits, whose favour he who proved ungrateful did not blush at.

In this case too, consider how the Lord offered himself to be judged by David himself, that he might prevail; for Nathan says: Thus says the Lord God of Israel: I anointed you king over Israel, and I delivered you from the hand of Saul, and I gave you all that was your lord's, and his wives I gave into your bosom, and I gave you the very house of Israel; and if these things are few, I will add to you. And why have you held the Lord as nothing, so as to do wickedly in his sight ? Confronted by the recounting of these things, when he saw that he, the judge, was the loser, he says: I have sinned against the Lord . Thus he justified the Lord, he who did not dare to deny his sin.

55. We can also take it this way: he who confesses his sin justifies the Lord. And so in the Gospel you have it that the publicans justified God, being baptized with the baptism of John . Now John the Baptist made a baptism that was of repentance . And he who does penance does not deny his transgressions. Therefore, since David had his transgression ever against him, he certainly did not deny what he also blushed at, he did not deny what he also acknowledged. And by not denying it he was certainly doing penance for the error he had committed, and so, by confessing his transgression, he justified the Lord and was himself justified by the Lord. For the Lord is justified when his justice is proclaimed and pardon is asked of him. At the same time he both justifies the one who confesses and is justified in his words, as it is written: Tell your iniquities, that you may be justified .

Chapter XI

XI, 56. There follows: Behold, I was conceived in iniquities, and in transgressions my mother bore me . Who does penance with such deep feeling? He lay stretched on the ground, dissolved in tears; he did not taste food; he refused himself the bath. Why should I now speak of the rest, that he abstained from adornment and from the royal retinue? He added the confession of his iniquity and handed it down to be sung through the whole world for everlasting ages.

Behold, he says, I was conceived in iniquities, and in transgressions my mother bore me . Turn away your face from my sins and blot out all my iniquities . Do not cast me away from your face, and do not take your Holy Spirit from me . Deliver me from bloodshed, O God, God of my salvation . Before we are born we are stained with contagion, and before we enjoy the light we receive the injury of our very origin. We are conceived in iniquity (he has not stated whether our parents' or our own), and in transgressions each one's mother brings him forth. Nor has he declared here whether the mother gives birth in her own transgressions or whether there are already some transgressions of the one being born. But see whether both are not to be understood. Conception is not free of iniquity, since the parents too are not without a fall. And if not even an infant of one day is without sin, much more are those days of the mother's conception not without sin. We are conceived, then, in the sin of our parents, and in their transgressions we are born. But birth itself too has its own contagions, and nature itself has not only one contagion. Marriage is indeed good, the union holy; but nevertheless let those who have wives be as though they had none . The marriage bed itself is undefiled, and no one ought to defraud the other of it, unless perhaps for a time, that they may be free for prayer: now, according to the apostle, no one is free for prayer at the time when he engages in the use of that bodily union. And the cloth of a menstruous woman is defiled, nor can she offer sacrifice in the days of her purification; and the days of childbirth of a woman who has given birth, and a good many other days besides, are kept free of sacrifice, until the mother is cleansed by the lawful rite.

57. Therefore to him in whom the Lord willed that there should be no contagions of an origin of this kind, the Lord says: Before I formed you in your mother's womb, I knew you, and before you came forth from the womb, I sanctified you, and I set you as a prophet among the nations . Who is so great that such great things were conferred on him? Is it Jeremiah? But he certainly was not set as a prophet among the nations, but in Judaea at that time; now, however, he is a prophet among the peoples too, who have believed in the Lord Jesus. See, however, whether it is not said to him who, before he was born of the Virgin, already was, and was always, and was at work even when placed in the womb of Mary, and was so holy that he sanctified his prophets. In him alone both the virginal conception and the birth were without any defilement of mortal origin. For it was fitting that he who was not to have the sin of a bodily fall should feel no natural contagion of generation. Rightly, then, did David tearfully lament in himself the very defilements of nature, that in man the stain should begin before life.

Chapter XII

XII, 58. While he says these things and confesses the filth of sins both particular and common, suddenly the splendour of truth and the brightness of spiritual grace shone upon him. For, passing beyond the shadow in the prophetic spirit, he saw the very sacraments of the heavenly mysteries, whose type Moses prefigured in the law. And so, wounded with the wound of charity and seized by the desire to search out the truth, he stretched the gaze of his mind towards higher things, and, looking forward into the future, he saw the treasures of wisdom and knowledge in Christ; he foresaw the sacrament of baptism, and, marvelling at the grace, he suddenly cried out, saying: For behold, you have loved truth; the uncertain and hidden things of your wisdom you have made manifest to me . Not that the mysteries are uncertain, for they are certain; nor are the secrets and hidden things of wisdom uncertain, but they are not manifest. For this is what it means: things that had as yet been made manifest to no one; for what eye has not seen nor ear heard, nor has it entered into the heart of man, this God has prepared for those who love him . Seeing these things, then, he says: Behold, now no longer in shadow, not in figure, not in type, but in truth the open light shines forth. Behold, now I look upon the truth, I recognize the splendour of the truth; now I venerate you, O Lord our God, with greater love. For behold, you have loved truth . Not through a mirror, not in an enigma, but face to face you show yourself to me, O Christ; I hold you in your sacraments. These are the true sacraments of your wisdom, by which the hidden things of the mind are cleansed.

59. And so, now glad and secure because the fullness of wisdom had flashed upon him, he says to the Lord: You will sprinkle me with hyssop, and I shall be cleansed; you will wash me, and I shall be whitened above snow . Well does he both not make void the sacraments of the Old Testament and teach that the mysteries of the Gospel are to be preferred. He asks to be cleansed with hyssop according to the law; he longs to be washed according to the Gospel; and he reckons that he will be whitened above snow if he has been washed. With a bunch of hyssop he who was accustomed to be cleansed by a typical baptism was sprinkled with the blood of a lamb. But he is washed who is rinsed in the flowing stream of the eternal fountain, and he is whitened above snow whose fault is forgiven. And so it is said of the soul herself: Who is this who comes up whitened ? Before she was baptized, she is the one who said: I am black and beautiful, O daughter of Jerusalem . For she was black, disfigured by the dark horror of sins; but after she had been washed through baptism and had obtained the remission of transgressions, she comes up whitened to Christ. Hence too the Lord spoke through Isaiah, saying: If your sins are like scarlet, I will whiten them like snow , that is: if they are bloody, if they are foul, I will cleanse them. This is the intelligible snow, of which it says that in the Gospel the garments of the Lord Jesus shone like snow, because he knew no sin, and his flesh, which he put on when he came into this world, was clean of every transgression. Why do you wonder that he saw the sacraments of baptism, when he said above, where he described the Lord's passion: The Lord feeds me, and I shall lack nothing; in a green place, there he has set me; he has brought me up beside the water of refreshment , and elsewhere: The voice of the Lord is upon the waters; the God of majesty has thundered ? And of that very sacrament he spoke more fully: You have prepared a table in my sight; you have made my head rich with oil, and your inebriating cup, how excellent it is .

Chapter XIII

XIII, 60. Rightly, then, here too he says, exulting: To my hearing you will give joy and gladness, and the humbled bones will exult . You have proved, O Lord Jesus, that your words never pass away; you have proved that saying of the Gospel which you spoke: Many prophets and just men have desired to see what you see and to hear what you hear . Behold, David rejoices by hearing alone that there would be remission of sins, and he prophesies that the humbled bones will exult . Just as all the bones of the just man will say: Lord, who is like you ? so the humbled bones will exult, that is, the bones of the just man who humbles his soul. Virtues, then, are called bones; certain movements of the mind or of the soul are called bones, as it were, and these movements are humbled by sins and exult by the gift of heavenly grace.

The peoples of the churches are also called bones, whence you have it said in the psalm: The things that you made in secret are not hidden from my bone . He called the Church and the sacred assemblies of the devout people his bone, for we are members of the body of Christ, of his flesh and of his bones . This, then, is what he says: that the Church of the Lord will know all the divine works and will receive the faith of the resurrection.

61. There follows: Turn away your face from my sins and blot out all my iniquities . It is a customary entreaty that we ask those whom we have injured to forget the offense done to them. So the prophet, after the manner of men, asks the Lord to turn away his face from his sins and, as it were, to take on forgetfulness of his sins. But since he beholds all things and nothing escapes him, he cannot forget as we do, from whom the memory of what we have known slips away after a short space of time. Therefore he rightly says that he should turn away his face, not from himself, lest, forsaken, he should fail, but from his sins, so that his sins may have no strength. For those on whom the Lord looks he enlightens, and in the countenance of the Lord there is loving-kindness and forgiveness. And therefore this same man says: Let my judgment come forth from your countenance ; for from the countenance of the Lord pardon, not punishment, proceeds. He is to be asked, then, to look upon us but to turn away his face from our sins, so that he may blot them out. For what he does not look upon he blots out, and the memory of what he has blotted out is buried, as the Lord himself says: I am, I am he who blots out your iniquities, and I will not remember; but do you remember, and let us be judged .

62. Now sin is either freely forgiven, or blotted out, or covered. It is freely forgiven through grace, blotted out through the blood of the cross, covered through charity. So too with iniquity, which is reckoned the habit of an unjust mind, although John in his epistle said that he who has committed sin has also committed iniquity, as we have it written: Everyone who commits sin commits iniquity also . Sin is iniquity, because in sin itself there is iniquity; yet, as it seems to us, sin is the work of iniquity, while iniquity is the worker of fault and transgression. It is a greater thing, then, that iniquity itself should be blotted out, that the root and seedbed of sins should be cut away. Let the evil root be removed, lest it bear evil fruits; let every inclination to error be abolished; let all the kinds of iniquities be taken away.

63. And so, just as the discipline of wisdom, entering into the soul, takes away imprudence, and knowledge takes away ignorance, so perfect virtue blots out iniquity, and the remission of sins blots out every sin. Hence the apostle says splendidly that he has freely forgiven us our sins, the Lord Jesus, blotting out the bond of the decree that was against us, and he took it, he says, out of the way, fastening it to the cross . With his blood he blotted out the ink of Eve; he blotted out the obligation of a liable inheritance. Faith, then, lessens sin. Therefore the Lord, when forgiving sins, used to say: Be it done to you according to your faith .

Chapter XIV

XIV, 64. There follows: Create a clean heart in me, O God, and renew a right spirit in my inward parts . Above he asks to be cleansed from hidden things; here he asks that a clean heart be created for him, which benefits him who is renewed in spirit; for in the new man the heart is clean in whom the filth of old transgressions has been blotted out and no image of iniquity has remained inscribed. And the cleansing of the heart is a great gift. Hence Solomon finely says: Who will boast that he has a chaste heart ? and the Lord in the Gospel: Blessed are the clean of heart, for they shall see God . For this reason David too desired to have a clean heart, lest he should be cast away from the face of the Lord.

65. And in him in whom the heart is clean, the spirit is renewed in his inner parts. For the inward parts are, as it were, the inner parts of the soul; for just as the inward parts are the inner parts of the body, so too there are inner, intelligible inward parts of the soul, as are the inward parts of mercy , as are the inner things that are in him, of which he says: Bless the Lord, O my soul, and all that is within me, his holy name . And the inward parts of the soul are the inventions of the senses, good thoughts, the pursuits of the virtues, perseverance, and finally those which in Greek are called ἔννοιαι (notions).

66. And the right spirit, which directs well, which leads into the right way, is the Spirit of truth , or at any rate the upright conscience of a man, bent by no sins, or the spirit which is in man. We have not passed over what others think; yet it seems to us that, since the reading speaks of the mysteries and the grace of the renewal to come is expressed, it is the infusion of the Holy Spirit that is asked for.

67. And so there follows: Do not cast me away from your face, and do not take your Holy Spirit from me . If one of our slaves has offended us, we are accustomed to turn our countenance away from him. And many of the rich are wont to banish their slaves and relegate them to little farms, and this punishment is reckoned the heavier. Indeed, the slaves are wont rather to offer themselves to the lash. If this is held grievous among men, how much more with the Lord our God! Was it not from this that the fratricidal resentment broke out (which ought indeed to have been checked, if any devotion could have tempered it), that God turned his face away from the gifts of Cain but looked upon the gifts of Abel? And so with a silent look he pronounced the one innocent, the other a sinner. Therefore, like the lowest slave, he humbles himself, and, as one caught in sin and guilty of offense, he entreats that he may be scourged rather than cast away from the face of the Lord.

68. Hear how God casts away from his face, as he says: Take him into the outer darkness; there will be weeping and gnashing of teeth . He who is banished from his face is set in darkness. Therefore the just man, lest he suffer darkness, says: Your countenance, O Lord, will I seek . For where the countenance of the Lord is, there is light, as it is written: Make your face shine upon your servant . And so, as soon as he turned his gaze and saw Peter, he also enlightened him.

69. It is a great punishment, then, to be cast away from the face of the Lord. Adam was cast away when he went out of paradise, and not undeservedly; for he had first hidden himself from the face of God. Cain too went out from the face of God, not only after the fratricide he had committed, but also after he thought that God was to be deceived, so that he denied his crime. The sinner, then, is shut out from the face of God, but the just man says: Behold, here I am . And so David himself, when he saw the people perishing, offered himself, saying: Behold, here I am; I have sinned, and I the shepherd have done wickedly , and so the wrath of the Lord was appeased and pardon was granted.

70. At the same time he shows that the saints remain, while the criminals are cast away. And therefore we must keep spiritual grace, lest on account of our sins it be taken from us. For he in whom the Holy Spirit is is not cast away, but, the fruit of the Spirit's gift remaining unimpaired, he strives always to offer himself to the Lord, like him who, when the Lord said: Do you also wish to go away ? answered: Lord, to whom shall we go? You have the words of eternal life, and we believe .

71. At the same time we must consider this, that the Spirit is not taken away except by the will of the Lord, just as he is not given except by the will of the Lord. And when he is given, he certainly does not work as one compelled, but is distributed according to his own will, as it is written, where the apostle says: But all these things one and the same Spirit works, distributing to each one as he wills . Since, then, he is not taken away except by the will of the Lord, it is clear that the will of the Trinity is one.

Chapter XV

XV, 72. Restore to me the joy of your salvation and strengthen me with a governing spirit. It is restored to the one to whom it is owed: the joy of salvation is restored to a rational nature. Now joy and gladness are the fruit of the Spirit; and our firm foundation, too, is the governing spirit. And rightly, he who is strengthened by the governing spirit is not subject to slavery; he knows not how to serve sin, he knows not how to waver, he knows not how to stray, nor does he totter with uncertain zeal, but, made firm upon the rock, he is established with a solid footing.

73. What do we think is meant by the governing spirit? Many refer the right spirit to the Lord Jesus, who took away the sin of the world and renewed the whole race of men by the shedding of his blood. And so it was said: And renew a right spirit in my inward parts. But the Holy Spirit, of whom he says: And do not take your Holy Spirit from me, they understand as the Spirit of truth, while the governing spirit they consider to be God the Father. And how true to his character it is that he says: Do not cast me away from your face. Faithfully he fears that the grace he has received may be taken from him. And therefore he says elsewhere: My eyes are ever toward the Lord, and in later psalms: Behold, as the eyes of servants are on the hands of their masters, and as the eyes of a handmaid are on the hands of her mistress, so are our eyes toward the Lord our God, until he have mercy on us. Surely this is the spirit that is guide and prince, which rules the mind, strengthens the affection, draws it where it wills, and directs it into the higher way.

74. There are also those who take it as the spirit of man, which is in him. Of this the apostle says: For who among men knows the things of a man except the spirit, which is in him? He who can know all things, he whom the hidden things of man do not deceive, can hold governance in man.

Chapter XVI

XVI, 75. There follows: I will teach the unjust your ways, and the ungodly will be converted to you. That helmsman is outstanding who steers a ship along a rocky shore; that teacher is good who sharpens the duller wits for progress in learning; that warrior is excellent, a wonderful leader, who fires the more timid for battle and supports them with the natural features of the ground he has reconnoitred, so as to make up for weakness of strength by the advantage of a favourable position: so likewise that preacher of the faith too is great who teaches the unjust. Hence he says finely: I will teach the unjust. He did not say: I will teach the just — for the just know the ways of the Lord —, but the unjust, he says, I will teach. And so the author of prudence and master of all says: I came not to call the just, but sinners. And that heavenly physician: The healthy have no need, he says, of a physician, but the sick.

76. Whether, then, he set it down well in the person of him who called the Gentiles or in his own: I will teach the unjust, it is because heavenly teaching can change the innermost affections and convert the purpose, and the divine working can pour the pursuits of devotion into sacrilegious breasts, so that those who lived without the law, who before were turned away, may be converted to the true God; so that also, by the example of a king's repentance, those who practise iniquities and bitter shameful deeds may be corrected and, converted in faith and in deed, may receive the remedy of saving teaching and enter the ways of the Lord, in which no winding of error, no byways of a headlong slip may make them stumble. For just as the pattern of a good life and the model of virtue is found in those men who have run through the seasons of their life without offense, so those who, renouncing the shameful deeds they did before or the errors of unbelief, have amended the course of their later years are set forth for imitation to those who fall either in deed or in knowledge.

77. There follows: Deliver me from bloodshed, O God, God of my salvation. This can also be referred to the death of Uriah, in that, conscious of having ordered his killing, he begs pardon for so great a misdeed, and although as king he is free of the laws, he is nevertheless guilty before his own conscience. Desiring to untie himself from these bonds, he prays for divine help, that he may be cleansed from every stain of the crime he committed. And indeed, since the holy prophet, mild and gentle of heart, always gave outstanding marks of his gentleness and devotion, so that he often pardoned his adversaries and thought he must refrain from killing them, it is no wonder that he grieves so deeply that the sin of shedding innocent blood had crept upon him. Therefore he asked to be delivered from bloodshed, that is, from mortal sins.

He praised the Lord his God, he proclaimed the justice of the Lord, and therefore he added: My tongue will exult in your justice.

Chapter XVII

XVII, 78. O Lord, you will open my lips, and my mouth will proclaim your praise. For he who praises the Lord will be saved from his enemies, as it is written. And certainly he had said earlier, in Psalm 49 (50): But to the sinner God said: Why do you declare my justices? Since, then, he had said with his own mouth that God forbade the sinner to declare his justices, surely by himself declaring the justice of God he made it plain that this offense had in no way been imputed to him as sin.

79. And he added: You will open my lips, and my mouth will proclaim your praise. God closes the mouth of the sinner, so that he may not speak the justices of God; he opens the mouth of the just, so that he may speak. Whose lips, then, he opens, him he absolves from the guilt of sin. And the Lord opens the lips of him who receives the word in the opening of his mouth. Hence the apostle too asks to be helped by the prayers of the people, that a door of the word may be opened to him for speaking the mystery of Christ.

By the tongue, then, we understand the speech that exults in the praises of God. Hence that verse too is understood in this way: My tongue is the pen of a scribe that writes swiftly: the speech poured into the prophet.

80. But if we take it as said in the person of Christ, see whether the scribe that writes swiftly may not be the Word of God, which runs through and penetrates the inward parts of the soul and inscribes in them the gifts either of nature or of grace, while the tongue is that holy body born of the virgin, by which the serpent's poisons were made void and the works of the gospel, to be celebrated through the whole world, were carried through.

There is added, for making sin void, that he took on humility and made his heart contrite, which the Lord chose as a sacrifice rather than the holocausts for sin which used to be offered according to the law. And so he says earlier: Holocausts also for sin you did not require; then I said: Behold, I come, that is: not thinking it robbery to be equal to God, I come taking the form of a servant, I come in the appearance of the human nature assumed, in the truth of the cross, to prove obedience by the humility of death, that disobedience may be blotted out.

81. Rightly, then, he says here too: For if you had wished, I would surely have given sacrifice; in holocausts you will not delight. A sacrifice to God is an afflicted spirit; a contrite and humbled heart God does not despise. And as I said above, it is certain that it accords with the mystery that the Lord Jesus himself seems here too to speak in his own person, he who earlier brought out that saying with evident testimony to his own person. For he is the true David, strong of hand, truly humble and gentle, the first and the last, first in eternity, last in humility, through whose obedience the guilt of the human race was blotted out and justice poured back. He, I say, Jesus, the end of the shadow and of the law, came as the master of humility to teach the proud, puffed up in mind and by the swelling of the heart, that they must pass over to gentleness and simplicity.

How, then, can sin be imputed in the type of that mystery, when in the mystery itself there is the remission of sins? Unless perhaps David confessed his iniquity and the sin of that misdeed precisely so that he too might have a share in the remission of sin and the grace of the mystery.

82. For what does it mean that a man confessing his sin sings of Zion and Jerusalem, saying: Deal kindly, O Lord, in your good will with Zion, and let the walls of Jerusalem be built, unless that he is pleased to have the gathering of the Church hastened through the calling of the Gentiles, he who would spread through the whole world the lineage of his faith, not for the sons of the slave woman but for those of the free Jerusalem, the one which is in heaven, and would found the enclosures of spiritual walls on the affirmation of apostolic teaching?

83. And so the walls of Jerusalem are the bulwarks of faith, the defences of disputation, the summits of the virtues; the walls of Jerusalem are the assemblies of the churches founded through the whole world; for the Church says: I am a wall, and my breasts are a tower. And rightly are the gatherings of the churches the walls of Jerusalem, since whoever enters the Church in good faith and deed becomes a citizen and inhabitant of that city on high which came down from heaven. These walls are built by a structure of living stones.

84. Seeing, then, the true Jerusalem and Zion, he said: When you have blessed Jerusalem and Zion in your will, then you will accept the sacrifice of justice, that is, the sacrifice of the body of Christ, who says, when he was speaking of his own passion: Open to me the gates of justice, and having entered them I will confess to the Lord. And in the gospel he says to John: Let it be so now; for thus it is fitting for us to fulfil all justice, and further on: Blessed are they who suffer persecution for justice's sake. Christ is justice; so he declares that the sacrifice of Christ will be acceptable to the Father. This, then, is what he also speaks of earlier: Sacrifice the sacrifice of justice, and hope in the Lord.

This justice is the spiritual offering and the holocaust of fervent devotion and of the infusion of the Holy Spirit, which he says is to come when the souls of believers begin to be brought to that spiritual altar of the Lord, souls which, renouncing pleasures and delights, draw as it were a plough through their inward parts, so that they may bear the fruits of a devout cultivation.

85. Or certainly thus: when you have blessed the Church acquired from the Gentiles, and the spiritual sacrifice of justice has begun to be offered often, then the holy martyrs too, who offered their body to be ploughed up for Christ, will be offered like calves upon the sacred altars, as we find written in the Apocalypse of John that under the altar were the souls of those who offered their bodies to martyrdom for the name of the Lord Jesus, that they might purchase for themselves the grace of Christ. To him be honour, glory, praise, perpetuity, with God the Father and the Holy Spirit, from the ages, and now, and always, and unto all the ages of ages. Amen.
