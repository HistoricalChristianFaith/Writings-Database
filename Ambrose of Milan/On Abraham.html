On Abraham

Latin source: Ambrose's two books on Abraham, written at Milan in the 380s, the first a moral instruction on the patriarch's life addressed to those preparing for baptism, the second an allegory of the mind and the virtues drawn largely from Philo's Questions on Genesis, from the critical text of C. Schenkl, Corpus Scriptorum Ecclesiasticorum Latinorum 32/1 (Vienna, 1897), pp. 501–638, verified against the page images and collated in sample with the Benedictine edition as reprinted in Migne, PL 14, with the edition's misprints corrected; in one place the text departs from Schenkl's conjecture with the manuscripts and the Benedictine edition (Book Two, Chapter II §6, sensus is sit). Scripture is translated as Ambrose quotes it, from his own Old Latin text; italics mark the Scripture he quotes. The section numbers are the Maurist ones shared by all editions and restart in Book Two; the Maurist chapter summaries are not translated. Greek words and the two Greek verse quotations are kept in Greek letters, with an English rendering in parentheses where Ambrose gives none; Psalms keep the Latin (LXX) numbering, with the Hebrew number in parentheses. Schenkl's supplements are read into the text, the words he brackets as spurious are omitted, the word he marks as corrupt (Book Two, Chapter III §11, † aggeę) is kept as printed, with the probable name (Aggai, the Septuagint's Ai of Genesis 12:8) in parentheses, his seven lacunae are marked with dots as printed, and his critical apparatus is not translated. Book One

Chapter I.

1. Abraham is the title of this book, since it has come into my mind to consider in order the deeds of this patriarch also. Our treatment of him will first be moral and simple. For if by a loftier discussion a certain progress and form of virtue and a certain likeness of it may be expressed, yet to look also at the outward traces of his actions is an advance in virtue. For if the things which nature has produced for the sustenance of men are of not one but a twofold or even a richer grace, how much more fitting is it that the things on which minds feast should be reckoned to be of no narrow but of more abundant use, and of manifold food.

2. Nor is it a slight or idle task. For since the Lord our God endowed him with the rich dowry of his blessing, so that his grace might call forth others and his instruction correct them, and Moses too described him for us to imitate, so that by the sight of this man he might raise up the hearts of men slipping into vice as from a kind of earthly tomb, it ought not to seem perfunctory if we too turn over the footsteps of this man with some care. For if the wise men of this world, that is, Plato himself also, the prince of philosophers, set before himself to be pursued not some real but a feigned and shadowy republic, that πολιτείαν (Republic) which we read, in order to teach what kind of commonwealth there ought to be, and so thought that he should describe one which he had neither heard of nor seen in any city, so that those whose office this is might be taught how to govern the commonwealth; and if Plato's fellow-disciple, Xenophon the Socratic, likewise wished with feigned matters to shape the person of the wise man in the book which he entitles Κύρου παιδείαν (The Education of Cyrus), so that the discipline of a just and wise king might proceed from the inmost bosom of philosophy: how much more ought we to review more intently, not a composed figure of a wise man, but virtue expressed and established by divine teaching, and to follow the ways of him whom Moses so described that in a certain manner he looked back upon himself.

Chapter II.

3. Truly a great man, and renowned for the distinctions of many virtues, a man whom philosophy could not equal even with its wishes. In short, what that man invented is less than what this man did, and the simple faith of truth is greater than the ambitious falsehood of eloquence. Let us therefore consider what kind of devotion there was in this man. For that virtue is first in order which is the foundation of the others, and rightly God required this one of him first, saying: Go out from your land and from your kindred and from your father's house. It would have been enough to have said from your land. In that lay going out from his kindred, going out from his father's house; but he added each one for this reason, to test his affection, lest perhaps he should seem to have caught him unawares, or some deceit be prepared by the heavenly commands. But just as the precepts had to be heaped up, so that nothing should be hidden, so also rewards had to be set before him, lest perhaps he should despair. He is tested as a brave man, urged on as a faithful man, challenged as a just man; and rightly he went out as the Lord had spoken to him.

4. And Lot went out with him. This is what is celebrated among the sayings of the seven wise men, ἕπου θεῷ, that is, 'follow God'. Abraham anticipated the sayings of the wise men by his deed, and, following the Lord, he went out from his land. But since earlier his land had been another, that is, the region of the Chaldaeans, from which Terah the father of Abraham went out and moved to Haran, and since he to whom it had been said: go out from your kindred brought out with him his nephew, let us consider whether perhaps this is going out from one's land: to go forth from this earth, that is, from a certain sojourn of our body, from which Paul went out, who said: but our way of life is in heaven; and from the allurements and bodily delights, which he called as it were the kindred of our soul, since the soul must suffer with the body as long as it clings to it, bound by its bond. Therefore we ought to go out from earthly conduct and worldly enjoyments and from the habits and acts of our former life, so that we change not only places but also ourselves. If we desire to cling to Christ, let us forsake corruptible things. Now the corruptible things in us are the flesh, delight, the voice, subject to bodily passions; and by the voice we understand the passions. Hence, since our soul is διμερής, that is, in two parts, having both the rational and the irrational, which is divided through the flesh and the allurements of bodily delight and the other passions of the body, the man who is just ought to separate and set apart the rational part of his soul from the irrational. For this is to go out from Haran, as it were to go forth from certain caverns and burrows and hiding places; for to hide belongs to a guilty conscience. And so let us also, following Abraham, go out from our hiding places. For if we are sons of Abraham, let us do the works of Abraham, so that our works may shine before God and before men. The just man tells his works to the King; the sinner hides himself, as Adam desired to hide himself. But he could not hide. So Abraham obeyed the command, and no delay is read to have come between.

5. Having gone forth, he travelled through as far as Shechem, which in Latin interpretation is called shoulder or neck, by which we understand the carrying out of the prescribed work, since below too we have it written: he put his shoulder under to labour. Hence we observe that by the figure of the places this is expressed: that holy Abraham proved his devotion not only by zeal but also by fruitful effectiveness, by which he came as far as the oak. In that place the Lord appeared to him and said: To your seed I will give this land. See how by a promise, as though by one often repeated, he shapes and trains one who is still weak, and how Abraham, mindful of himself, ascribes everything to God and claims nothing for himself. Therefore also he built an altar to God, who appeared to him, and withdrew from there to the mountain east of Bethel, desiring to see the Sun of justice still rising for him. And therefore he pitched his tent not in the valleys but on a mountain, because God is God of the mountains and not of the valleys.

6. And he called upon the name of the Lord. Where Bethel is, that is, the house of God, there also is the altar; where the altar is, there is the calling upon God. And not undeservedly did he make such great progress, because he hoped that God would be a help to him. The athlete of the Lord is exercised and hardened by adversities. He went off into the desert: famine befell; he went down into Egypt. He had learned that in Egypt there was wantonness among the young men, luxury, insolent desire, intemperance in pleasures. He perceived that among men of this kind his wife's chastity would be unsafe, and that his wife's beauty would be a danger to himself: he advised his wife to say that she was his sister. By this we are taught that beauty in a wife is not greatly to be sought, since it often brings death to the husband. For it is not so much a woman's beauty as her virtue and dignity that delights a husband. Let him who seeks the sweetness of marriage not court a woman superior to him in wealth, whom the necessities of marriage would not hold, nor one adorned with necklaces, but one adorned with good character. It often offends a husband if his wife knows herself to be nobler. These things are next door to pride. Sarai was not richer in means, not more illustrious in birth. Therefore she did not think her husband unequal to her, therefore she loved him in favour as her equal, therefore she was not held back by wealth, not by parents, not by kinsfolk, but followed her own husband wherever he went. She went to foreign lands, she declared herself his sister, content, if it had to be so, to be endangered in her modesty rather than her husband in his safety; and to protect her husband she feigned sisterhood, lest those who lay in wait for her modesty should kill him as a rival and the avenger of his wife. In short, as soon as the Egyptians saw her, marvelling that she was very beautiful, they brought her in to their king, and treated Abraham well, honouring him as the brother of the woman who had pleased the king.

7. But the Lord afflicted Pharaoh with great and severe afflictions, and his house, because of Sarai the wife of Abraham. A great testimony and lesson for guarding chastity is this passage, so full of exhortation that each person should show himself chaste, should not covet another's bed, nor, in the hope of concealment or of acting with impunity, assail another man's wife, nor be provoked by a husband's carelessness or foolishness or by his longer absence. God, the guardian of marriage, is present, from whom nothing is hidden, whom no one escapes, whom no one mocks. He takes the place of the absent husband, he keeps watch; nay rather, without a watch he catches the guilty one before he does what he has prepared: in the spirits of individuals, in the minds of all, he recognizes the crime. Though you, adulterer, have deceived the husband, you do not deceive God; though you have escaped the husband, though you have tricked the judge of the forum, you do not escape the Judge of the whole world. He avenges more heavily the wrong done to the helpless, the insults done to an unwary husband; for the wrong is greater when the author of the marriage chamber is spurned and disregarded than when its guardian is.

8. Pharaoh himself also, though king of the Egyptians, whom both the insolence of royal power might have made careless and the wantonness and luxury of Egypt might have drawn away from zeal for chastity, called Abraham and rebuked him, saying: What is this you have done to me? Why did you not say that she is your wife, but said to me that she is my sister? And I had taken her to myself as a wife. And now behold, your wife is before you. Though he was by nature wild and barbarous, yet he shows that even amid foreign and barbarous errors there is a care for modesty, and that even by them the crime of adultery must be shunned. He who pleads ignorance condemns intemperance. Nor is it a wonder if a barbarian knows the law of nature: among the dumb animals, which are bound by no laws, there are yet some which not only keep faith in union with their mates, but also guard the chastity of a single coupling. Thus the law of nature is greater than the prescription of laws. It is no wonder, then, if this Egyptian king too feared God, though he did not fear man, and paid the penalty of adultery, though he was held guilty by no laws; and immediately, when he recognized that she was another man's wife, he not only gave her back to her husband but also gave him escorts to conduct him, lest anyone of the barbarous people should do violence either to the man's property or to the wife's modesty.

9. Most beautiful too is this passage for arousing zeal for devotion, in that he who follows God is always safe. And therefore we ought to prefer God to all things, and neither regard for our country nor love of parents and children nor consideration for a wife ought to call us back from carrying out the heavenly precepts, because God bestows all those things on us and is able to preserve what he gives. Thus it is a great example of Abraham's devotion that he went down into Egypt with a beautiful wife. The just man did indeed have a care for conjugal chastity, but greater was his zeal to bring his devotion to fulfilment without delay, lest he should seem to have preferred the guarding of his marriage bed to the heavenly commands. And so, since for God's sake he despised all things, he received all things multiplied from God. But first God gave a reward to chastity, which he knew to be pleasing to the wife; for since in his zeal to fulfil the heavenly oracle he had led his wife too into danger to her modesty, God also defended the purity of the marriage.

Chapter III.

10. Devotion, then, has by just order claimed the first place for itself. Let us see also the grace of the other virtues. Holy Abraham was comforted by the presence of his nephew, to whom he showed a father's affection. A quarrel broke out between the servants of the nephew and of the uncle. Being the more prudent, he perceived that the harmony of masters is usually dissolved by the dissensions of servants; he cut off the fringe of discord, lest the contagion should creep further. For he thought it more tolerable that their companionship be separated than that their goodwill be broken. This you too ought to do, if perhaps you meet with something of this kind, so as to remove the seedbed of dissension. For you are not stronger than Abraham. He judged that the quarrels of servants should be avoided, not despised. And if you are stronger, beware lest the other be weaker, and lend his ear to the whisperings of servants. Undivided households of slaves often sow discord among kinsmen. Divide rather, so that friendship may remain; an undivided house does not sustain two. Is it not better to move away in good grace than to live together in discord?

11. The patriarch also teaches what kind of division it ought to be. Let the stronger divide and the weaker choose, so that he may have nothing to complain of. He will not be able to find fault with his own choice. No occasion for going back on it will remain to the one who is given the option of choosing, nor is the divider burdened; for the more prudent he is, the more cautious, so that he is neither cheated in the division nor defrauded in the choice.

12. Abraham divided, because the land could not hold them he says together, since they were exceedingly rich. It is a vice of the world that the earth cannot hold the rich; for nothing is enough for the greed of the rich. The richer anyone has become, the greedier he is to possess. He desires to extend the boundaries of his field, to shut out his neighbour. Was Abraham anything of this kind? Not at all, although in the beginning he too was less perfect. For whence came perfection before the coming of Christ? He had not yet come who would say: If you wish to be perfect, go, sell all that you have and give to the poor, and come, follow me. Yet, as one not at all avaricious, he offers the choice; as a just man, he cuts off the dissension. Let there not be he says a quarrel between me and you and between my shepherds and your shepherds, because we are men who are brothers. Behold, is not the whole land before you? Depart from me: if you go to the left, I will go to the right, or if you go to the right, I will go to the left.

13. And Lot lifted up his eyes and chose the well-watered region he says of the Jordan, because it was all watered and was like the paradise of God. Often inherited possessions fall to people, some more profitable, others more pleasant. They should certainly not be cut up into portions; for the worth of each begins to be diminished. But if the parties cannot agree about the more profitable ones, let the pleasant ones be set against the more profitable. The dispositions of men are different; some are delighted by the profitable, others by the pleasant. The weaker man chooses the more pleasant things and disdains the more profitable. Sometimes there is a useful steward or manager of the farm: he is set against a town slave. If the one choosing is foolish, he chooses either a cook or a singer, whom he thinks of more charming grace, and rejects the more useful one. Often too, even where the returns are not unequal, the more prudent man avoids the more pleasant things. They quickly stir up envy, they quickly rouse the mind of the greedy man against themselves. Here, however, Scripture said nothing about one part having been more profitable and the other more pleasant, lest Abraham should seem to have captured the young man's eyes by design. It described the pleasant part; it did not add the more profitable. It was necessary that he make two parts of the whole region. Then, he was dividing things present, not things absent. One region could not hold both. He offered the choice, which could be a mark of the highest justice.

14. Lot chose the pleasant part, which quickly caught the eyes of plunderers. Hence war among kings, the victory of the adversaries, the captivity of the inhabitants. And so Lot too paid the price of his weaker counsel, deceived not by the barrenness of the lands but by the envy their pleasantness aroused, so that he himself too was led away captive, since through the vice of servile wickedness he had turned away from the better and had chosen the portion of the most shameful men; for Sodom is luxury and wantonness. And therefore Lot is called in Latin interpretation turning aside, because he chooses vices who turns aside from virtue and deviates from equity.

15. When Abraham learned this, he numbered his home-born servants, and with three hundred and eighteen men he gained the victory and freed his nephew. The affection behind the division is proved, since he so loved his nephew that he did not even shrink from the danger of war for his sake. What is he numbered? That is, he chose. Hence that saying too refers not only to God's knowledge but also to the grace of the just, which the Lord Jesus spoke in the Gospel: and all your hairs are numbered. For the Lord knew those who are his, but those who are not his he does not deign to know. And he numbered three hundred and eighteen, so that you may know that not the quantity of the number but the merit of election is expressed. For he took to himself those whom he judged worthy to be in the number of the faithful, who would believe in the passion of our Lord Jesus Christ. For the Greek letter T signifies three hundred, and the sum IH, eighteen, expresses the name of Jesus. It was therefore by the merit of faith that Abraham conquered, not by a populous army. In short, going out with a few home-born servants, he triumphed over those to whom the arms of five kings had yielded.

16. But he who conquers ought not to claim the victory for himself, but to ascribe it to God. This Abraham teaches, who was made humbler by his triumph, not prouder. In short, he offered sacrifice, he gave tithes; and therefore Melchizedek too, who in Latin interpretation is called king of justice, king of peace, blessed him; for he was a priest of the most high God. Who is the king of justice, the priest of God, but he to whom it is said: You are a priest for ever according to the order of Melchizedek, that is, the Son of God, the priest of the Father, who by the sacrifice of his own body made the Father propitious again toward our sins?

17. And how great is this, that he wished nothing of the spoils of victory to touch him, nor to take what was offered! For the acceptance of a reward diminishes the fruit of a triumph and takes away the grace of a kindness. For it makes the greatest difference whether you have fought for money or for glory: the one is reckoned in the place of a hireling, the other is held worthy of the glory of a preserver. Rightly the holy man refuses to appropriate anything from the spoils, or to accept what is offered, lest he who gave should say, 'I made him rich'; he testifies that this alone is enough for him, what had gone to feed the young men who fought. Someone will say: 'Since he himself conquered, how does he say to the king of Sodom, 'nothing shall I take from you', and this when the spoils were surely in the power of the victor?' He teaches military discipline, that all things be kept for the king. Yet he declares that to those who had been with him, associated perhaps as auxiliaries, a share of the profit should be given as the wage of their labour.

18. And therefore, since he did not seek a reward for himself from man, he received it from God, as we read it written that after these words the word of the Lord came to Abraham in a vision, saying: Fear not, Abraham, I will protect you. Your reward shall be very great. The Lord is not slow to reward: he both promises quickly and bestows much, lest through some delay repentance at having despised present goods should steal upon weak spirits; and he repays as it were with a kind of generosity that pays interest, so as to restore more abundantly to him who was not captivated by the offer of present things.

19. Let us consider also what reward he asks from the Lord himself. He does not demand riches like a greedy man, nor a long span of this life like one afraid of death, nor power, but he seeks a worthy heir of his labour. What he says will you give me? But I am sent away without sons, and below: because you have not given me seed, my home-born servant shall be my heir. Let men learn, then, not to spurn marriage nor to join unequal partners to themselves, lest they beget children of such a kind that they cannot have them as heirs; so that, if they are moved by no regard for modesty, they may at least, out of concern for passing on their inheritance, strive for a worthy marriage.

20. But if Abraham's view does little to correct men, hear the oracle of God condemning an inheritance of this kind. This one shall not he says be your heir, but another who shall come forth from you, he shall be your heir. Whom does he call another? For Hagar too bore a son, Ishmael, but he does not mean him; he means holy Isaac. And therefore he added: who shall come forth from you; for he truly came forth from Abraham who was begotten in lawful wedlock. But through Isaac, the lawful son, we can understand that true lawful one, the Lord Jesus, whom at the beginning of the Gospel according to Matthew we read to be the son of Abraham, who showed himself the true heir of Abraham, illuminating the succession from its founder, and through whom Abraham looked up to heaven and recognized the splendour of his posterity, no less illustrious than the brightness with which the heavenly stars shine. For as star differs from star in brightness, so also the resurrection of the dead, the Apostle said, because, granting to men the fellowship of his resurrection, he made those whom death used to hide in the earth partakers of the heavenly kingdom.

21. And how was the offspring of Abraham spread abroad except through the inheritance of faith, by which we are likened to heaven, joined with the angels, made equal to the stars? Therefore he says: So shall your seed be. And Abraham, he says, believed God. What did he believe? That Christ would be his heir through taking on a body. That you may know that he believed this, the Lord says: Abraham saw my day and rejoiced. Therefore it was reckoned to him as righteousness, because he did not seek a reason but believed with most ready faith. It is good that faith should go before reason, lest we seem to demand a reason from the Lord our God as from a man. For how unworthy it is that we should believe human testimonies about another, and not believe the oracles of God about himself! Let us imitate Abraham, then, so that we may be heirs of the earth through the righteousness of faith, through which he was made heir of the world.

Chapter IV.

22. But perhaps someone may say: 'How can you set Abraham before us to be imitated, when he received a son from a handmaid? Or what does it mean that so great a man, whose great works we admire, was liable to this error?' And therefore, lest we should seem, in the manner of sailors, to have steered clear of this place, which most think full of shoals, it is my concern to explain its reason. I do not deny that Abraham received a son from a handmaid, so that you may know that Abraham was not of some higher nature and substance, but one of the number and the frailty of all men. Indeed, he was also called from the region of the Chaldaeans, who, we have learned, were more intent on vain superstition than the rest; and therefore he found greater grace with God, because he renounced what was behind and stretched himself out to what lay before, so as to follow God. For he is set before you for imitation, so that you too may observe that, if you renounce your sins, you can merit the Lord's mercy.

23. Yet it may trouble some that he was already speaking with God and went in to the handmaid, as it is written that Sarai said to Abraham: Behold, the Lord has shut me up so that I do not bear. Go in, then, to my handmaid, that you may make sons from her. And so it was done. But let us consider first that Abraham lived before the Law of Moses and before the Gospel: adultery did not yet seem to be forbidden. The penalty of a crime dates from the time of the Law, which prohibited the crime, and before the Law there is no condemnation of the guilty, but only from the Law. So Abraham did not offend against the Law, but came before the Law. God in paradise, though he had praised marriage, had not condemned adultery. For he does not will the death of the sinner, and therefore he promises what belongs to reward and does not exact what belongs to punishment. For he prefers to call us forth by gentle things rather than to terrify us by harsher ones. You too sinned when you were a gentile: you have an excuse. You came to the Church, you heard the Law: You shall not commit adultery: now you have no excuse for the fault. Yet since my discourse is with those who have given in their names for the grace of baptism, if anyone has committed so great a crime, let him know that pardon will be granted him, but as to one who has committed a crime; and let him know that for the future he must abstain. Indeed, to that adulteress whom the scribes of the Pharisees brought before him in the Gospel, the Lord forgave what had gone before, but he said: Go, and from now on see that you sin no more. What he says to her, he says to you. You committed adultery as a gentile, you committed it as a catechumen: you are forgiven, it is remitted through baptism; go, and after this see that you sin no more. You have one defence of Abraham.

24. The second is this: that it was not because he was inflamed by the ardour of some roving lust, not because he was captivated by the beauty of a wanton form, that he set the marriage bed below union with the handmaid, but from the desire to seek posterity and to propagate offspring. Even after the flood the human race was still scarce; it was also a matter of religion, lest anyone should seem not to have paid his debt to nature. Indeed, the daughters of holy Lot also had this reason of seeking posterity, lest the human race should fail. And therefore he cloaked a private fault with the plea of a public duty. Nor is it without purpose that the wife is introduced as the author of the deed, so that the husband may be excused and not be thought to have been carried away by a roving error, and at the same time so that women may learn to love their husbands and not to be agitated by a vain suspicion of a concubine, or to envy their stepchildren if they themselves have not received children. It was the good wife's concern to excuse her barrenness before her husband, and, lest she should be the reason her husband had no children, she urges him to go in to the handmaid. This Leah did, and Rachel afterwards. Learn, woman, to lay aside jealousy, which often drives women into fury.

25. But I also warn you, men, especially you who are making your way to the grace of the Lord, not to mingle with an adulterous body — he who indeed joins himself to a harlot is one body — and not to give women this occasion of divorce. Let no one flatter himself on the laws of men. Every unlawful intercourse is adultery, and what is not lawful for the woman is not lawful for the man. The same purity is owed by the husband as by the wife. Whatever is committed with one who is not a lawful wife is condemned under the crime of adultery. So you have observed what you ought to beware of, lest anyone show himself unworthy of the sacrament.

26. Hear this also: that intemperance of this kind dissolves the love of marriage; it makes handmaids proud, matrons wrathful, spouses discordant, concubines insolent, husbands shameless. As soon as the handmaid has conceived by her master, she scorns her mistress as though she were richer by her child-bearing; the mistress grieves that she is despised and accuses her husband as the author of her wrongs. Indeed, Sarai herself had given her husband power over her handmaid, and afterwards she says to him: I receive injury from you. I have now given my handmaid into your bosom. But when she saw that she had conceived, I was scorned she says before her. May God judge between me and you. How great the pain and how grievous the complaint of women is, the reading sets forth. Give a thoughtless and fickle husband, who does not know how to humour his wife, and he furnishes grounds for divorce. But Abraham, a moderate and prudent man, Behold he says your handmaid is in your hands: use her as it pleases you; for he preferred to keep his wife rather than the servant-girl. Yet even this is not a full remedy. The angry wife receives the power and uses the revenge permitted her too immoderately. But if Sarai did not keep moderation, which woman will keep it? And therefore it is written: And Sarai afflicted her, and she fled from her face. There are two things that Scripture includes, so as to express both the heavy indignation of the mistress and the swelling and pride of the handmaid. That Sarai afflicted her refers to the wrath of the one who afflicted; as for Hagar's fleeing, she would not bear the insult with a servant's patience, she who claimed for herself the eminence of sharing her master's bed. She resented the injury, she who had put on insolence. Indeed, when the angel asked her where she was going, she answered: From the face of Sarai my mistress I flee. And this too belongs to immoderate swelling, that she first spoke the name of Sarai and only afterwards indicated her as mistress. The one was put first as an insult, the other added to identify the person. The handmaid's insolence did not please the angel, and therefore the angel said to her: Return to your mistress. Surely it would not have escaped the angel if she had fled overcome by the violence of punishments, and he would rather have rebuked the cruelty of the one who beat her than the departure of the one who fled; but to show that she was fleeing as a proud woman, so as not to be subject to her mistress, he added: and humble yourself under her hands. I wish, therefore, that no one may fall into this vice; but if anyone has fallen into it, let him learn to humble his handmaid to his wife, lest, while he wishes to defend his handmaid, he shut out his wife.

27. So Abraham was both one of a gentile people and had gone in to the handmaid for the sake of posterity, because his wife, desiring to overshadow her barrenness, had been the author of that deed to her husband. And yet it is not without purpose that immediately after this God, because he approved his other merits or his repentance for this deed, said to him: I am your God. Earn your merit in my sight and be without complaint, as though he had not yet fully earned it, he who despaired of his barren wife's bearing and sought posterity from the handmaid. Be he says without complaint, that is, blameless, so that your wife may not complain of you nor anyone blame your deeds. He changes his name by the addition of a letter, so that from Abram he is called Abraham; that is, from vain father, as the Latin interpretation has it, he should be called lofty father, chosen father, or from a father he should become the father of a son. He was vain when he did not know God: he was made chosen after he acknowledged God. He was a father when he had offspring from the handmaid, but he was not the father of a son, because he had no son received in lawful wedlock. Sarai bore, and he became the father of a son. He is commanded to be circumcised when he is about to receive the inheritance of the true seed. Is not the circumcision of the flesh plainly a precept of purity, that one should cut away the lust of the flesh and rein in the desires left untamed by luxury and wantonness? For indeed by the word circumcision this is prescribed: that every stench of impurity be wiped away and the incentive of lust be taken away. We have used two defences.

28. There is also a third, which the authority of the apostle Paul grants us, who says: 'those things which Abraham did, in receiving offspring from a handmaid, were done as a figure and are spoken according to allegory'. It is allegory when one thing is done and another is figured, as the Apostle himself also teaches, saying: You who wish to be under the Law, have you not read the Law? For it is written that Abraham had two sons, one from the handmaid and one from the free woman. But he who was from the handmaid was born according to the flesh, and he who was from the free woman through the promise. These things are said through allegory; for these are the two covenants, the one from Mount Sinai, bearing children into slavery, which is Hagar, showing that two peoples flow from Abraham's generation: one of the Jews, which is a slave to the syllables of the Law, because it seems to have been begotten from the handmaid into slavery; the other the Christian, which has received the freedom of heavenly grace for the remission of sins. So what you think to be a sin you observe to be a mystery, by which the things that were to come in later times were being revealed. Indeed, he added: But you, brethren, are sons of the promise according to Isaac. And 'therefore' he says 'do not seek the works of the Law', since a man is not justified by the works of the Law except through the faith of Jesus Christ, and so that you may know that he is speaking to Christians: we too have believed in Christ Jesus, that we may be justified by the faith of Christ and not by the works of the Law. We acknowledge, then, that these things which happened in figure were not a crime for them, but they will be for us, if we will not take warning from what was written for our correction; rather let us strive that, since we are sons of the free woman, who is Sarai, we may not be slaves to the snares of the Law, since Abraham kept the free woman and cast out the handmaid.

29. At this point I know that many are troubled. For if circumcision is good, it ought to be kept even today; if it is useless, it ought not to have been commanded, especially by a divine oracle. But since the apostle Paul has said that Abraham received the sign of circumcision, surely a sign is not the thing itself, but belongs to another thing; that is, it is not the truth, but an indication of the truth. Indeed, he himself explained and expressed it, saying: he received the sign of circumcision, a seal of righteousness and of faith. Hence we understand, not unfittingly, that bodily circumcision is the sign of spiritual circumcision. So the sign remained until the truth should come. The Lord Jesus came, who says: I am the way and the truth and the life, because he circumcised not a small part of the body in sign, but the whole man in truth; he took away the sign, he brought in the truth, because after that which is perfect came, that which was in part was done away, and therefore the circumcision of the part ceased when the circumcision of the whole shone forth. For now it is not in part, but the whole man is saved: he is saved in body, he is saved in soul. For it is written: He who wishes to come after me, let him deny himself and take up his cross and follow me. This is the perfection of circumcision, that through the offering of the body the soul is redeemed, of which the Lord himself says: He who loses his soul for my sake shall find it.

30. There remains now that portion of the question, whether the part ought to have been sent ahead when perfection was to come. This point is easy to resolve, if we consider to whom the part was commanded and for whom perfection was reserved. For the part was commanded according to the Law to the people of the Jews, that stiff-necked people, that weak people, that people which did not know its God. Since, then, it could not bear the part, how could it have kept the perfection? Just as, if you instruct a little boy in letters, you must begin from the single elements of the letters, so that you lead him from single strokes to syllables, and from syllables in order to words and speech; nor can anyone sail the sea fearlessly except one who has first sailed on rivers. Indeed, if you wish to entrust some task, either of making a journey or of lifting a burden, to a boy and to an older person, are the burdens to be made equal, or the labour equal? So then you should know that the perfection of circumcision was reserved for those who, instructed by Christ, seemed fit for stronger things, so that both the faithful might be proved, of whom an innumerable multitude would take up the cross and devote their soul for Christ, and the unbelieving, who reckoned the scant blood of their own circumcision to be saving, might be unable to resist men who thought that salvation was sought in the immolation of the whole body.

31. But it must be considered that God called him while he was in uncircumcision, and to him, while he still remained in uncircumcision, the inheritance of a lawful son was promised, so that you may believe him to be not the father of the Jews only, as they themselves assert, but the author of all believers through faith. Sarai too, before her husband's circumcision, is blessed with no mean reward by the addition of one letter, so that she might have the primacy of virtue and of grace. From her he promises that nations and kings of the gentiles will come, so that in her a type not of the synagogue but of the Church might be established. But that Abraham laughed when a son from her was promised was a sign not of unbelief but of exultation. Indeed, he fell on his face — he who worshipped believed — and added: If a child shall be born to me at a hundred years, and if Sarah shall bear at ninety years. And he said: Let this Ishmael live in your sight. He is not unbelieving in the promises nor greedy in his prayers. That is: 'I do not doubt that you will do it, so that you both give a son to an old man of a hundred years and, as the author of nature, relax the bounds of nature. Blessed is he to whom these things are given; but yet if this Ishmael too, whom I have from the home-born slave-woman, lives in your sight, grace abounds to me.' Indeed, the Lord both approved his affection and did not refuse his petition, and he confirmed his promises.

Chapter V.

32. We have spoken of Abraham's devotion and of his faith, of his prudence, justice, charity and frugality: now let us speak also of his hospitality. For it is no mean virtue. Hence the Apostle too taught, by the authority of a twofold writing, that it ought to be present above all in a bishop, so that he may be at hand for those who arrive, and go out to meet them, and watch the roads, and be present to those who do not seek him, and seize those who pass by. Abraham was sitting before the door, sitting at midday. When others were resting, he was watching for the arrival of guests. Rightly did God appear to him at the oak of Mamre, because he was most zealously seeking the fruit of hospitality.

33. And looking up he says with his eyes he saw, and behold, three men were standing over him. And when he had seen them, he ran to meet them. See first the mystery of faith. God appeared to him, and he beheld three. He on whom God shines sees the Trinity. He does not receive the Father without the Son, nor does he confess the Son without the Holy Spirit. These things elsewhere more fully; now the purpose is to pursue the moral sense of the passage. He does not sit idle who looks far off. Nor was he content to have seen them: he ran to meet them. He hastened to meet them, because it is not enough to do rightly unless you also hasten what you do. The Law commands that the Passover be eaten in haste; for a quickened devotion has richer fruits. Learn, then, how unwearied you ought to be, so that you can be beforehand with a guest, lest someone else be beforehand and defraud you of the abundance of a good service.

34. Hospitality is a good thing; it has its reward, first of human gratitude, then, what is greater, of divine recompense. We are all guests in this sojourning; for we have a lodging to dwell in for a time: we move on quickly. Let us beware lest, if we have been harsh or negligent in receiving guests, the lodgings of the saints also be denied to us after the course of this life. Hence in the Gospel the Saviour says: Make for yourselves friends of the unrighteous mammon, that they may receive you into their eternal dwellings. Then too, while you are in this body, the necessity of travelling often arises. What you deny to others, then, you will decree against yourself, and by what you have bestowed on others you will make yourself seem worthy of it. If all were to follow that opinion of not receiving guests, where will there be rest for travellers? Leaving human habitations, then, we shall seek out the retreats of wild beasts, the lairs of animals.

35. But do you plead poverty? A guest asks of you not wealth but favour, not an adorned banquet but whatever food is at hand. Better is he says hospitality with herbs for friendship and favour than if you slay calves at the stalls with enmity. These things are pleasing to men, acceptable to God. Hence the Lord Jesus in the Gospel declares that whoever gives a guest a drink of cold water will not be without a share in heavenly rewards. Indeed, Jacob watered Rachel's sheep and found favour; he gained a wife. Then, how do you know whether you are receiving God when you think it is a guest? Abraham, while he offers hospitality to travellers, received God and his angels as guests. And indeed, when you receive a guest, you receive God, as it is written in the Gospel, as you read, where the Lord Jesus says: I was a stranger, and you took me in. For what you did to one of these least, you did to me. By the hospitality of a single hour that widow who received Elijah and fed him with a little food found perpetual nourishment for the whole time of the famine, and she received a wonderful reward, that the meal did not fail from the jar. Elisha too paid the rent of his lodging by granting the raising of her dead child.

36. Yet it is not only readiness in receiving that is sought, but also the diligence and affection of the one who receives. Let Abraham teach you both. He ran to meet them, he asked first, saying: Lord, if I have found favour before you, do not pass by your servant. Let water be taken and let your feet be washed, and refresh yourselves under the tree. And I will take bread, and eat, and afterwards you shall pass on, since for this you have turned aside to your servant. He saw three and called one Lord; he confesses himself the servant of him alone. Then, turning to the two whom he thought ministers, he longs to offer his service to them too, bound now not by the due right of servitude, but by the kindly name of diligence and by the practice of attendance.

37. And Abraham hastened into the tent to Sarah and said to her: Make haste and knead three measures of fine flour and make cakes baked under the ashes. A good husband does not suffer his wife to be without a share in a religious service, nor does he greedily claim the whole service for himself. Rightly, then, the claims both of devotion and of modesty are kept. What belongs to devotion he wishes to be shared; what belongs to modesty remains intact for Sarah. Before the tent the husband watches for the arrival of guests; within the tent Sarah guards a woman's modesty and performs women's works with her modesty safe. Outside the husband invites; inside Sarah prepares the banquet. Nor does Abraham hasten only himself, but he also tells his wife that she must hasten, showing her to be a partner in devotion and not unequal in faith.

38. Knead he says three measures of fine flour and make cakes baked under the ashes. In Greek they are called ἐνκρύφια, that is, hidden, because every mystery ought to lie hidden and be covered, as it were, by a faithful silence, lest it be rashly divulged to profane ears. With this the divine majesty is fed; on this affection it feasts, the affection of one who is sparing of speech and does not bring sacred things into the open. And in brief Sarah teaches the mystery of faith, making three measures of one fine flour; she bears the type of the Church, to which it is said: Rejoice, barren one who do not bear, break forth and cry out, you who do not travail. For this is she who cherishes the faith in her inmost spirit, asserting the Trinity of the same Godhead, adoring the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit with a certain equal measure and reverence, and celebrating them together in the unity of majesty, distinguishing them by the property of the persons. With this assertion of faith knead your devotion.

39. Let the woman offer fine flour, that is, the inner part of the spiritual wheat, or of that grain of which it was said that unless it falls into the earth it bears no fruit. Hence Mary too was the first to see the mystery of the Lord's resurrection, and she hastened to make known the message of sacred salvation, not indiscriminately to all, but to Peter and John alone. Let the man run to the oxen, let him take a calf and receive the sacrament of the Lord's passion with hastening and unwearied zeal, not slackened by slow idleness; let him hand it to a child, who keeps the innocence of tender age, knows no guile, does not know how to strike, and guards the purity of an uncorrupted body. Of him the Lord Jesus says: Unless you are converted and become like this child, you shall not enter into the kingdom of heaven. To this child and to those like him holy David also assigns the office of divine praise, saying: Praise the Lord, you children.

40. Nor is it without purpose that he ran to the oxen, took a tender and good calf, and set it before them with milk. Indeed, in Exodus, when he was declaring the Passover of the Lord, Moses says: The lamb shall be for you without blemish, clean, perfect, a year old, a male; you shall take it from the sheep and the kids. And you shall slay it he says the whole multitude of the synagogue, towards evening. Hence here too it is described as midday when hospitality is offered by Abraham to the Lord. But for the supper the calf is sacrificed and eaten with milk, that is, not with blood, but with the purity of faith. A good calf, as being one that would wash away sins; tender, because he acknowledged the yoke of the Law not with a hard neck but with a soft one, and did not refuse the gibbet of the cross. And rightly tender, he of whose head and feet and inward parts nothing was left, and not a bone was broken from him, but he passed wholly into the food of those who feast. Such a one the shadow of the Law prefigured for us; such a one the truth of the Gospel has shown.

41. They ate he says but Abraham stood under the tree. We observe that the office of humility is commended by his courtesy. Abraham stood, and you seize the first place at table. Indeed, that humility found favour, so that a son was promised him.

42. And he said to him: Where is Sarah your wife? He answered and said: Behold, in the tent. Did the Lord, who in what follows announces the coming destruction of Sodom, not know where Sarah was? He knew, but he wished to teach us how great the modesty of women ought to be, so that they may not turn the eyes of guests upon themselves by forwardly going out to meet them, but may perform their ministry with their modesty safe. Abraham too impresses on your ears that Sarah stays in the tent, so that you may learn what to require of a wife. Sarah, now of advanced age, guards a youthful modesty: therefore the Lord promised her a son. There had ceased he says for Sarah the courses of women. It was not idly added, lest you should think that she still had a woman's capacity to give birth.

43. But Sarah laughed. Consider it a sign of what was to come rather than of unbelief. For she laughed, though she did not yet know what she was laughing at, because she was to bring forth a public joy in Isaac. Therefore she denied that she had laughed, because she did not know; therefore she laughed, because she prophesied.

Chapter VI.

44. And the men, rising up, looked toward the face of Sodom and Gomorrah. Just as the visitation of the Lord is shown to those who fear him, so too the punishment of sin is laid up for the impious. Abraham was escorting his guests; to the grace of kindness he added his services. But the Sodomites, in place of the duties of devotion, were heaping up the outrages of impiety.

45. I will not hide he says from Abraham my lad what I am doing. Scripture certainly indicated above that Abraham was of advanced age, since he had come to ninety-nine years: how then does he call him a lad? But since it has portrayed him as forgetful of old age, an untiring watcher, nimble in running, most patient in standing, most eager in escorting, does not the name of lad seem to suit his services? Rightly is he called a lad who knew nothing of the fastidiousness of old age, and who offered the innocence and the readiness to serve of boyhood. And so to the just man is given the grace of blessing and the inheritance of posterity,

46. while the offence of sins is set forth. The cry he says of Sodom and Gomorrah is filled up. Great is the patience of the Lord, that he does not punish the sinner at once, but long delays, awaiting correction, and is not moved to take vengeance unless the sinner exceeds the measure. Hence the Lord Jesus too says to the Jews in the Gospel: fill up the measure of your fathers.

47. I will go down therefore, that I may see whether they will be brought to completion according to their cry that comes to me; and if not, that I may know. The Lord was not ignorant of the sins of Sodom, but he spoke words of this kind for your instruction, so that you might examine more closely the misdeeds of those on whom you judge that vengeance should be taken. I will go down he says that I may see, that is: you too take care to go down, go down with zeal for inquiry, lest there be anything that deceives one who is absent or lies hidden from him, so that you may detect the crime with your own eyes. Those placed far off can be ignorant of many things. But what cry does he mean, unless perhaps that to him from whom nothing is hidden all things cry out, and the crimes of individuals seem to cry out? Thus it is said to Cain: the blood of your brother cries to me, that is, your murder of your kin is not hidden, but cries out. And so God is, as it were, roused by the cries of our outrages, so that he who gladly pardons may at some time take vengeance.

48. Then, when Abraham asked that he should not destroy the just together with the wicked as if they were wicked, and inquired: if there are fifty just men in the city, will you destroy them? he answered: I will not destroy he says the city, if there are fifty just men in it, and I will preserve the whole place. And so, through the order of questions and the alternation of answers, even if he should find ten just men in the city, he nevertheless promises impunity to the whole people for the sake of the justice of a few. From this we learn how great a wall for his fatherland a just man is, and how we ought not to envy holy men nor rashly disparage them; for it is their faith that preserves us, their justice that defends us from destruction. Sodom too, if it had had ten just men, could have escaped perishing.

49. But what does it mean that the very men who had come to Abraham together with the Lord made for Sodom, unless that their crime might be heaped up, if the impious tried with greater sacrilege to do violence to those whom the just man had honoured? For as to his calling them men, the reason is evident: they bore the appearance of men.

50. They came to Sodom in the evening, to Abraham at midday, because the presence of angels shines upon the just man but brings darkness to the impious. Yet the fact that they came in the evening to him who was to be freed from the contagions of Sodom and from the destruction of the whole city can also be referred to the time of the Lord's passion. It was evening before Christ came, because the whole world was in darkness. It was evening for all whom the dark squalor of monstrous offences oppressed. The Lord Jesus came, redeemed the world with his blood, brought light. And the two angels came to Sodom at evening. Where grace is to be bestowed, Christ is present; where severity is to be exercised, only the ministers are present, and Jesus is absent.

51. Lot was sitting at the gate. The adversities of captivity had amended holy Lot and made him more careful. And so with the advance of age he had learned to imitate his kinsman. And so he sat at the gate, to receive those who arrived. Then he rose up to meet them. The more perfect man ran to meet them; this one rose up and worshipped with his face to the ground and said: behold, lords, turn aside into the house of your servant. And he compelled them to turn in, though they were saying: we will stay in the street. Here the holiness of the just man and the graciousness of the angels are commended. They did not wish their coming to seem too burdensome to their host; he, though he knew among whom he lived, nevertheless exposed his own house to the dangers, in order to take his guests away from them. Certainly, the more slowly they consented, the more fully, by testing him longer, they proved him.

52. And the men of the city of the Sodomites surrounded the house, from the infant to the old man, the whole people together. The equity of the divine judgement is established beforehand, lest anyone should perhaps say: what sin had the children committed, that all should be involved in the destruction? So there was no one just there, no one innocent. Hear Scripture testifying that they surrounded the house from the infant to the old man, the whole people together. No age was free from guilt — and therefore no one was free from destruction — and he who had not the possibility of committing the crime had the disposition. The strength of the old men was spent, but their mind was full of lust. Holy Lot offered the modesty of his daughters. For although that too was a shameful impurity, yet it was a lesser thing to have intercourse according to nature than to offend against nature. He set above the modesty of his own house the grace of hospitality, which is inviolable even among barbarous nations. Thus even there hospitality remains unharmed, where not even the bond of kinship is safe enough.

53. But the angels struck them with blindness, so that they could not find the door of the house which they longed to open. Here indeed the wonderful power of the angels is declared, in that blindness was poured over the impure and the doors of the house could not be found. But this too is shown, that all lust is blind and does not see what is before it. At the same time, in that holy Lot was drawn back into the house by the hands of his guests, he is shown to have been unmindful of danger and mindful of faith, not to have snatched himself from the danger but to have exposed himself to it.

54. A place is given to devotion, in that, when the overthrow of the whole region had been made known to him by the angels, holy Lot is presented both as having sons-in-law and as warning them to flee, so that he might not, by deserting them and not warning them, seem less devoted towards his daughters' husbands, nor might the cause of their error be laid to his charge, since they, deprived of the company of husbands, sought intercourse with their drunken father. Scripture therefore does not leave the holy man undefended, and he is presented as having both given his daughters to husbands and warned his sons-in-law. But it seemed to them that he was mocking them; and yet Lot still lingered, to persuade his sons-in-law, and he would almost not have set out to escape, had he not been compelled to go out by the angels urging him and holding his hands.

55. So he did not set out, but was led out, and received the command not to look back nor to stop in all that region, but to go up into the mountain. When this is said to him, it is said to all. If then you too wish to escape, do not look back, but before you. Look to where Christ is, who may say to you: go behind me, as he said to Peter: go behind me, so that he might follow Christ and see Christ. Behind is Sodom, full of outrage; behind is Gomorrah, teeming with vices, the region of crimes. Do not touch says the Apostle, do not defile, do not taste the things which all lead to corruption. Flee then from Sodom, leave it quickly, forsake the elements of this world, lest the dangers that threaten entangle you; do not stop as you flee, nor linger in all the region of vices. He who did not look back escaped; she who looked back could not escape.

56. The daughters of holy Lot, however, are excused, because they thought that that destruction had been not of the neighbouring region but of the whole world, and that they alone with their father had remained as survivors of all the peoples, and that therefore, lest the race of men should fail, they sought intercourse with their father, so that they might raise up the seed of human generation from their father. So it was not the vice of lust but the remedy of generation, which I do not think should be counted as a crime. For Eve too, taken from the man, upon whose rib the woman was built, bone of his bones and flesh of his flesh, was nevertheless joined to the man for the sake of the succession of the human race. Yet the awareness of the just man is withdrawn from this deed; drunk with wine, he did not know what he was doing. Hence it is no wonder if their opinion deceived the girls, since they thought that the peoples of the whole world had perished. The same excuse would not hold for holy Lot, who had heard from the angels that that place, not the whole world, was to perish.

57. Truly we learn that drunkenness must be avoided, through which we cannot guard against crimes. For what we guard against when sober, we commit unknowingly through drunkenness. It is a small thing that it inflames lust and kindles the desires of the body; it also undermines the mind itself, takes the spirit captive and wrests away the sense. Those who indulge in too much wine do not know what they are saying; they lie buried. And therefore, if they have done any wrong through wine, before wise judges their deeds are indeed pardoned, but they are branded as the authors of levity. How great is the very ugliness of it, that the strength is loosened and the gait totters!

58. Many think themselves strong: are they stronger than Lot, are they more continent than Noah? Scripture has certainly not set out the vices of the patriarchs, whom we read were overcome by wine, for any other reason than that you might learn what to beware of. The one lay naked, the other lay open to his daughters' error. And Noah the just was deceived, because the power of wine was still unknown; but in him you have been instructed, so that you might not be ignorant. Lot trusted himself to his daughters and, undone by an old age soaked in wine, committed incest unknowingly: you, drink in such a way that you are not taken captive. Let the patriarchs instruct you, not only by teaching but also by erring. Therefore the example of drunkenness was repeated, so that the lesson of caution might be confirmed.

Chapter VII.

59. Then Sarah's chastity is tested again, so that the chastity of all may be required. For Abimelech too had taken her to himself as wife, and God said to him in the night: behold, you shall die because of the woman. We observe that adultery is punished with death by divine judgement. And therefore he added: but she is living with a husband. Indeed every union of a man and a woman that is not celebrated with the lot of lawful marriage has its own guilt. For learn, you who are reaching toward the grace of baptism as candidates, as it were, of the faith, the sober discipline of continence. No one is permitted to know a woman other than his wife. And therefore the right of marriage has been given to you, lest you fall into the snare and sin with another's woman. You are bound to a wife; do not seek release, because it is not lawful for you to take a wife while your wife is living. For to seek another, too, when you have your own, is the crime of adultery, and this the graver because you think that authority for your sin is to be sought from the law. Guilt is more tolerable if it is hidden than if authority is usurped for guilt; and adultery is not only to sin with another's wife, but anything that lacks the right of marriage; yet this passage teaches that the crime is graver where the rights of a celebrated marriage are violated and a wife's modesty is undone. And therefore, when Abimelech pleaded that he had not known she was another's wife, since the husband himself had said that she was his sister, God answered him: and I knew that you did this with a pure heart, and I spared you, that you should not sin against me. For this reason I did not allow you to touch her. We learn that God is, as it were, the guardian and protector of marriage, who does not allow another's bed to be polluted, and that if anyone does so, he sins against God, whose law he violates and whose grace he breaks. And therefore, because he sins against God, he loses fellowship in the heavenly sacrament.

60. Perhaps it may trouble you for what reason Pharaoh was severely afflicted with torments by almighty God, as we read above, though he too did not know that Sarah, whom he had heard to be a sister, was Abraham's wife, whereas Abimelech received no punishment. But you should know that the king of Egypt was a leader of vices, who, the more licence he had, the more outrage he committed, while Abimelech was esteemed by God so faithful that he deserved to hear: and I knew that you did this with a pure heart, a king not of affliction, as the Egyptian was, but of fortification, as the interpretation of Gerar, over whose people he ruled, teaches. There is therefore no doubt that the indignation of the Lord was turned back by his other works, since the Lord is truly the judge of the inner conscience and the interpreter of spirit and mind. Then, he did not, like that Pharaoh when approached by Moses, refuse and despise the command of God, nor did he put off his compliance, but at once called Abraham, gave him back his wife, fined himself with a price because he had seen another's wife, and paid the dowry of modesty.

61. From this too it can be gathered that King Abimelech deserved gentler treatment, because Abraham prayed for him and obtained his request. For his wife and his maidservant gave birth, whom the Lord had earlier shut up because of Sarah, Abraham's wife. This equally belongs to the dispensation, so that Sarah's childbearing, granted by the promise of God, might be supported also by this testimony, since you observe that through God's displeasure even fruitful women become barren, and again by the Lord's will barren women are made fruitful, according to what is written: did I not make the barren woman and the woman who bears? says the Lord. And yet this may be taken as said of the mystery of the synagogue and of the Church, because the synagogue ceased to have offspring, being defrauded of the posterity of succession, and the congregation of the nations, which was barren while it did not know God, began to have eternal offspring. Hence it is also read: rejoice, barren one, who do not bear; break forth and cry out, you who are not in labour, for more are the children of the desolate than of her who has a husband.

62. And a son, Isaac, was born to Abraham, when he was a hundred years old. You too, if you are perfect, will have a posterity of gladness and an inheritance of exultation. And Sarah said: the Lord has made laughter for me; and whoever hears of it will rejoice with me. This is certainly not to be understood of this generation, which is liable to a great many mishaps, so that sometimes it would have been better not to have begotten, but of the generation by which each sinner who does penance, when he is redeemed from death, brings gladness to the angels.

63. And Sarah said: who will announce to Abraham that Sarah is suckling a child? A moral passage. Women are called to remember their dignity and to suckle their own children. For this is a mother's grace, this the honour by which they may commend themselves to their own husbands. Indeed husbands usually love more those children whom the mothers themselves have suckled at their own breasts.

64. And Abraham made a great supper when Isaac his son was weaned. This is no slight or ordinary thing. For Abraham did not lay on a great banquet because the boy had been withdrawn from his nurse's milk, but because Isaac was judged fit for the food of a stronger grace and the nourishment of virtue, no longer to be given milk to drink like the Corinthian, but strengthening the sinews of his mind with the more solid fare of the heavenly commandments.

65. Envy quickly follows prosperity. Sarah had given birth, had weaned her son: she saw the son of the maidservant playing with her son Isaac, and she said to Abraham: cast out the maidservant and her son; for the son of the maidservant shall not be heir with my son Isaac. This seemed hard to Abraham, to cast out his own son, even if it was the one he had received from a home-born slave woman. You too, do not join yourself to a maidservant, lest you receive a son from her and your wife not endure that he should become co-heir with her son; for you see that from this the grace of marriage is dissolved. Certainly, if you have fallen into it and have a son, cast out the maidservant and her son; for it is better that the maidservant should depart than the wife, and that the maidservant's son should be cast out than the lawful one. But if you hesitate, if you despise your wife's judgement and it seems hard to you, God says to you what he said to Abraham. For what he said to him he says to you, and he says it to all: let it not be hard in your sight concerning the boy and the maidservant. In all that Sarah has said to you, hear her voice, for in Isaac shall your seed be called. Nowhere else did he say 'hear the voice of your wife' except here, that is: 'you have done your wife an injury and have not soothed her feelings; you have received a son from a maidservant and have not honoured your wife's son. Can your seed be called in the son of a maidservant? Certainly not; in the lawful son is the true succession. But you fear, because he is your son, that perhaps, once cast out, he may perish and die. My grace will not fail him'. Our God feeds all, supports everyone, both the just and the unjust. Indeed he also rains upon the just and the unjust. As Abraham did, so you too do. Cast out the maidservant, so that your wife may remain at home secure and unoffended. Cast out the maidservant's son, so that he who has not the privilege of birth may not have a share in the inheritance.

Chapter VIII.

66. And it came to pass after these words, God tested Abraham. God tests in one way, the devil in another. The devil tests in order to overthrow, God tests in order to crown. Indeed he tests those whom he has proved for himself. Hence David too says: prove me, O God, and test me. He proved holy Abraham first and so tested him, lest, if he tested him before he had proved him, he should weigh him down. He proved him when he commanded him to go out from Haran, and found him obedient. He proved him when, relying on the title of faith, he freed his nephew, when he touched nothing of the spoil, when he promised the old man a son — and though he himself was a hundred years old, and though he considered Sarah's organs of generation as dead, yet he believed and did not waver in faith, he who could have wavered on account either of barrenness or of old age —; he proved him by the diligence of his hospitality. Having proved him, then, he judged that, as a stronger man, he should be tested with greater and, as it were, harder commands. And here indeed we are taught by example that a man is proved by true things, but tested by things contrived and feigned. For God did not wish the son to be sacrificed by his father, nor did he wish this offering to be carried out, he who provided a sheep to be sacrificed in place of the son; but he was testing the father's affection, whether he would put God's precepts before his son, and not bend the force of his devotion out of regard for fatherly love.

67. And he said to him: Abraham, Abraham! By the repetition of the name he rouses his mind, so that he might be more ready. Then he answered: here I am. And he said: take your most beloved son, whom you have loved, Isaac, and go into the high land, and you shall offer him to me as a whole burnt offering on one of the mountains, which I shall tell you. He does not allow the father's affection to be idle. From the beginning he goads him and pricks him with the stings of fatherly love, and adds the son's name to the name of the relationship and to the force of love. He did not think it enough to have called him son; he joined to it most beloved, whom you have loved, Isaac. What does it mean that he says: whom you have loved, and did not say: whom you love? We can indeed use in defence of divine Scripture the fact that it often puts past things for future or present ones, as you have in the Gospel: this is my most beloved Son, in whom I have been well pleased, though certainly the Father is always well pleased in the Son. And in the psalm you have: the Lord said to my Lord: sit at my right hand, though he is always seated. Yet we can also take most beloved of the present, and whom you have loved to mean that he signified him as beloved not by some recent impulse of love but by a love long ingrained and proved. For what increases for a time dissolves in time, but what has pleased for long or always cannot quickly be abolished. This too may seem not absurd, that we love more those who are about to die; that is: whom you loved before, as though he now loves him as one to be sacrificed. Nor does he idly add the name of holy Isaac, that is, him whom you received in old age, received from your wife as your only one, received as the reward of your faith, the recompense of your works, received from the promise of God, not from the fruitfulness of your wife, from whom you could not hope for another. You shall offer me a whole burnt offering, but first go into the high land. An interval is set between, lest the affection seem to be hurried on suddenly, so that by that delay the grace of fatherly love might creep in, the father's longing. He added: on one of the mountains, which I shall tell you. And here likewise, so that, while the old man climbed, the impulse might be broken, the right hand grow weary, the intention fail, and while he sought to learn the mountain, he might unlearn his preparation.

68. And rising up not only on the following day but even at dawn, so that the night seemed to have brought delays to the zeal of the hastening father, he saddled his she-ass and took with him two servants and Isaac his son, and he cut wood for the whole burnt offering. We are taught to bring everything prepared to the sacrifice; we learn also to claim for ourselves the preparation of the sacrifice, the office of ministry, and not to delegate it to others. Old Abraham, rich in cattle and abounding in slaves, did not seek troops of attendants; he himself also cut the wood and did not leave off services beyond his strength.

69. And he came on the third day to the place which God had told him. And he himself sets out as the third with two, leading his victim, and on the third day comes to the place of sacrifice. This number is a saving one and fitting for those who are to sacrifice. Thus later too Moses says to Pharaoh king of Egypt: we will go a journey of three days and sacrifice to the Lord our God, as he told us. And rightly the sacrifice of the Trinity is celebrated on the third day.

70. And Abraham, looking about, saw the place with his eyes from afar. He who hastens to fulfil a task searches carefully. Although he quickened his aged step with zeal, yet, thinking this too slow, he went ahead with his eyes. The functions of each of his members were vigorous, though aged members could not be vigorous. The sight of old men is usually dulled, so that they do not easily make out even things close at hand. This man not only saw the place, but even beheld it while placed far off.

71. Nor did he doubt that he had seen it, but said to his servants: sit here with the she-ass, and I and the boy will go on as far as there, and when we have worshipped, we will return to you. Rightly is there a type in the she-ass, because the truth too is in the foal of a she-ass. For by this animal is figured the people of the Gentiles, formerly subjected to a burden, now made subject to Christ. Isaac, then, is the type of Christ who was to suffer. He came on a she-ass, so that the people of the nations who were to believe might be signified. And therefore the Lord, when he came to undergo the passion for us, loosed the foal of a she-ass, on which he himself sat, now gentle and meek, entrusting its back to Christ. But his saying: I and the boy will go on our way shows that the father would not fail in so great a preparation, nor the son give way; or else that they would pass through the harshness of so great a deed by the remedy of fatherly love. He added: we will return to you. He prophesied what he did not know. He himself was planning to return alone, once his son had been sacrificed, but the Lord spoke through his mouth what he was preparing. He spoke guardedly with the young servants, lest, if the business became known, someone might hinder it or make an outcry with groaning or with weeping.

72. And he took the wood of the whole burnt offering and laid it on Isaac his son, and he himself took the fire in his hand, and the knife. The victim is consecrated by sacred ministries and commended as one to come, the victim of devotion that is first to be the bearer of a devout ministry. Isaac carried the wood for himself; Christ carried for himself the gibbet of the cross. Abraham accompanied his son, the Father accompanied Christ. Neither was Isaac alone nor Jesus alone. Thus, alone, he says: and I am not alone, because the Father is with me.

73. And Isaac spoke to Abraham his father, saying: father. And he said: what do you wish, son? The father's affection is struck by the words of fatherly love and beaten, as it were, by waves from this side and from that. The son calls his father, the father says: son, so that by the very sound of the words the father may recognize how impossible it is that he could strike the one whose wound he would wish to undergo himself. These names are wont to work the grace of life, not the ministry of death; these words are wont to rouse to fatherly love, not to death.

74. Isaac added, saying: behold the wood; where is the sheep for the whole burnt offering? Here too he prophesies by his speech, not by knowledge; for a sheep was being prepared by God for the sacrifice. Then Abraham answered likewise: God will provide himself a sheep for the whole burnt offering, son. The minister, not to be bent from the zeal of his devotion, is not afraid to call him son again and again. So firmly was he grounded in the solidity of his intention; and he thought himself the better father for this, and judged that his son would remain his forever, if he sacrificed him to God. And he prophesied not only what happened at once, that God provided himself a victim in place of Isaac and gave the son back to his father, but rather this, that this victim was not that of the divine plan: there would be another victim, which God would prepare for himself, to cleanse the whole world; that one would be more acceptable to all, for whose sake many fathers would offer their sons and would not fear to be separated from their sons in this world. Daily fathers offer their sons, that they may die in Christ and be buried with him in the Lord. How many fathers, when their sons were slain in martyrdom, have returned more joyful from their tomb!

75. Abraham came to the place predestined for the sacrifice and built there an altar and laid on the wood. What great efforts on the part of one about to sacrifice, lest he be thought to have been swept suddenly into sacrificing! And having bound the hands and feet of Isaac his son, he laid him on the altar upon the wood. The father binds his son with bonds with his own hands, lest the son, in shrinking back and startled by the force of the fire, should incur sin.

76. And the angel said: Abraham, Abraham. The divine voice in a way held his hand and forestalled the blow of the brandished right hand. He called not once only, lest he either not hear fully or think the voice a chance one. He called him back in the same way as he had commanded him. He repeated the call as though fearing that he might be forestalled by the zeal of devotion, and that a single call could not hold back the thrust of the one striking. Do not lay your hand on the boy, nor do anything to him; for now I know that you fear your God and have not spared your most beloved son for my sake, that is to say: 'I sought out your affection, I did not exact the deed. I tested your mind, whether you would not spare even your most beloved son for my sake. I do not take away what I myself gave, nor do I begrudge you the heir whom I bestowed on you when you had none.' Nor idly here too did he call him his most beloved son, to show that what he said above, whom you have loved, was said in such a way that you should not suppose he had already ceased to love him.

77. And Abraham, looking about, saw, and behold, a single ram caught in a thicket. Why a ram? Surely as one that stands foremost in the flock. Why suspended? So that you might observe that that victim was not an earthly one. For what cause suspended by the horns, except that it raised its flesh from the earth by a higher power? According to what is written: whose sovereignty is upon his shoulders. Who indeed is signified but he of whom it was said: he will exalt the horn of his people? Our horn is Christ, who excelled all, as we read: beautiful in form beyond the sons of men, he alone raised up and exalted from the earth, as he himself teaches us when he says: I am not of this world, I am from above. Him Abraham saw in that sacrifice, his passion he beheld. And therefore the Lord himself says of him: Abraham saw my day and rejoiced.

78. Hence Scripture says: Abraham called the name of that place 'the Lord saw,' so that today they say: 'on the mountain the Lord appeared,' that is, that he appeared to Abraham revealing the future passion of his body, by which he redeemed the world, and showing also the manner of the passion, when he showed it suspended. That thicket is the gibbet of the cross, and on this wood the most excellent leader of the flock, being exalted, drew all things to himself, so that he might be known by all. Hence he himself says: when you have exalted the Son of man, then you will know that I am.

79. From this too Abraham won the favour of God. Indeed this is the third blessing. For he received three full blessings: one after the victory by which he freed his nephew, when Melchizedek met him, when God said to him: look up into heaven and count the stars, if you can. So shall your seed be. And Abraham believed God, and it was reckoned to him as righteousness; another when Abraham was commanded to be so named and received the seal of circumcision; the third when he did not hesitate to offer his most beloved son to God as a whole burnt offering. This blessing in turn surpassed the earlier ones. For in those he promised that there would be a propagation of Abraham's seed, but in this he says: and in your seed all the nations of the earth shall be blessed, because you have heard my voice. Let us too, then, hear the voice of our God and obey his precepts, if we wish to find grace with him.

Chapter IX.

80. The passage that follows contains the death of a wife, the weeping of a husband and the duty of burial, by which a husband's affection is proved. And Abraham rose up he says from the dead, so that we may not cling too long to the dead, but may pay them as much duty as is enough. Abraham, moreover, hastened to pay the price for the place of the tomb, though it was being given free, so that we may build the tombs of our parents or kinsmen not in places belonging to others, but rather in our own; for often, when possessions are alienated, the burial places that are in those same places become saleable. But Abraham did this because there were not yet temples of God of this kind, in which the remains of the faithful are laid up for the Lord.

81. Abraham had grown old. Therefore, as befits a good father, he had to provide a wife for his son, but because of God's oracle he could not return to the place from which he had been commanded to go out. He was living, however, in the land of the Canaanites, and he shrank from seeking his lawful succession from that race.

82. And he called the elder servant of his house and told him to go to Haran and to seek a wife from among his kinsfolk for his young master. Learn from this that even servants of riper age are called boys by their masters or by any of their betters. Hence a certain poet too thought this usage worth following, whether he found it himself in the usage of those who think themselves learned and wise, or carried it over himself from our writers, or found it already carried over: Feed your cattle as before, boys, rear your bulls. Hence we too speak of boys when we also mean servants, expressing not their age but their condition.

83. Mark now the virtues of a good father of a household, and consider first what charge he entrusts and to whom, so that you too may so train your servants that they show a father's affection to your children and carry out their duties. One of the servants was found, but an elder one, to be chosen to provide a wife for his young master, and he was bound to swear by putting his hand under his master's thigh. By the thigh we understand generation. And the generation of Abraham is Christ. Hence too To Abraham were spoken says the Apostle the promises, and to his seed. He does not say: and to seeds, as of many, but as of one: to your seed, which is Christ, showing that through him there would be a holy sacrament for us, through him sure help.

84. He bound him, moreover, not to bring a wife for his master from the seed of the Canaanites, the author of whose race had not honoured his father and therefore passed on to his descendants an inheritance of cursing, so that we may learn that faith, and a certain inheritance from the stock of its author, is to be sought in those whom we wish to join to ourselves: for with the holy you will be holy, and with the perverse you will be perverted. If this is so in other matters, how much more in marriage, where there is one flesh and one spirit! And how can love agree if faith disagrees? And therefore beware, Christian, of giving your daughter to a Gentile or to a Jew. Beware, I say, of bringing home as your wife a Gentile or a Jewess or a foreigner, that is, a heretic or any woman alien to your faith. The first faith of marriage is the grace of chastity. If she worships idols, whose adulteries are proclaimed, if she denies Christ, who is the teacher and rewarder of chastity, how can she love chastity? Even if she is a Christian, it is not enough unless you are both initiated in the sacrament of baptism. You must rise together to prayer, and God must be besought with joined petitions. Another mark of purity is added if you believe that the marriage that has fallen to your lot was given to you by your God. Hence Solomon too says: from God he says a wife is prepared. Those unequal in faith cannot believe this, so that one would think the grace of marriage was bestowed on him by one whom he does not worship. Reason teaches it, but examples warn us more fully. Often a woman's allurement has deceived even the stronger husbands and made them depart from religion. Either have regard for love or beware of error. The first thing sought in marriage is religion. Therefore Abraham sought to give his son a kinswoman.

85. You too, seek a kinswoman. Who is one's neighbour? He who showed he says mercy. The Lord Jesus says this in the Gospel. You too, seek one who is near to the seed of Abraham and akin to your neighbour. The seed of Abraham is Christ; he is the neighbour of all, who showed mercy upon all, taking away the sin of the world. Learn what is to be sought in a wife. Abraham sought not gold, not silver, not possessions, but the grace of a good nature.

86. Then, asked whether, if the daughter were unwilling to come, he should lead his master's son there, Take heed to yourself he says that you do not take my son back there. The Lord God of heaven and God of earth took me from my father's house and from the land in which I was born, who spoke to me and swore, saying: To you I will give this land and to your seed; he himself will send his angel before you, and you shall take a wife for my son from there. If the woman will not come with you into this land, you shall be clear of this oath. Consider more carefully what this is to profit. It is not lawful for you to take a foreign woman. Certainly, if she makes herself a Christian, you will even have praise from her. But if she refuses to become a Christian, let not the desire of marriage turn you from the faith: so the reading instructs. Abraham advised that she be brought if she followed, that she not be sought if she stayed behind, and that his son not go there. Certainly the mercy of his Lord, who had led him away from his sojourning in the land in which he was living, would not be lacking, so as to go before the zeal of the suitor and incline the girl's mind. He said this as a prophet in the matter of his son, and as a moral teacher he taught us to hope in the Lord, who deigns to help one who seeks the increase of faith.

87. And the servant rose up and set out for Mesopotamia, and according to the order of the vow that the servant had vowed on setting out, Rebecca met him, having a pitcher upon her shoulder, a virgin very beautiful, whom no man had known. And she went down to the spring and filled the pitcher, gave the servant to drink, and watered all his camels. Then the servant of Abraham took golden earrings of a drachm each, and gave two bracelets into her hands — their weight ten pieces of gold — and asked her whether there was room for lodging and whose daughter she was. A moral simplicity is expressed, in that there was no room for ambition even in the seeking of a marriage, but the Lord, as the patron of marriage, fulfilled the request for the marriage. Yet we may behold the mysteries of the Church. Where is the Church found but in Mesopotamia? There she is sought, from there she is fetched, where she is enclosed by two rivers, the laver of grace and the weeping of penitence. For unless you have wept for your own sins, unless you have received the grace of baptism, the faith of the Church and a certain marriage bond are not acquired for you. The Tigris, that is, prudence, and the Euphrates, that is, justice and fruitful enlightenment, fortify her, separating her from the barbarian nations.

88. A virgin moreover very beautiful, whose comeliness no age corrupts. Very beautiful, because he too who acquired her for himself is very beautiful beyond the sons of men. Whom no man had known; for her union was with no man, but was owed to Christ alone. Having a pitcher upon her shoulder, because she washes the acts of all: and because she who washed her own consists of the congregation of the Gentiles, therefore you read that she went down to the spring and filled the pitcher and came up. That Samaritan woman came to the spring, as is written in the Gospel, but she did not go down — it seemed to her a well — nor did she fill a pitcher. Indeed she says: I have no pitcher. She had nothing with which to wash her acts. This woman alone went down, she alone knew the true spring, that is, not the spring of water but of eternal life, to which David said: For with you is the spring of life; in your light we shall see light. And therefore she had something to give to the thirsty, because she believed; for she who did not believe said to this Spring, which wished to give her drink: From where have you living water to give me? But this woman had enough to satisfy not only the servant but also the camels, she who is accustomed not only to water the just but also to fill the unjust. Therefore she received the golden earrings and bracelets that Abraham sent, as rewards of her merits.

89. Perhaps, daughters, you who are making your way to the grace of the Lord, on hearing this you too may be roused to have earrings and bracelets, and may say: 'How is it that you forbid this, bishop, that we should have what Rebecca received as a gift, and urge us to be like Rebecca?' But Rebecca did not have such earrings and bracelets as are wont to sow quarrels in the church, which often slip off: she had other earrings, which I wish you may have, other bracelets. Rebecca's earrings are the marks of devout hearing, and Rebecca's bracelets are the ornaments of deeds. She had such earrings as would not weigh down the ear but soothe it, such bracelets as would not load the hand with material gold but lighten it with spiritual action. Therefore she pleased both her brother and her parents in this adornment. You too, take the earrings that Abraham left you, take the bracelets that he sent. Hear the words of the Lord your God, as he himself heard them: carry out his commands, as he hastened to fulfil them.

90. A most beautiful passage, moreover, for instructing those on whom any task is laid, is that Abraham's servant did not eat the bread set before him until he had carried out his master's command. When this was obtained, he gave vessels of gold and silver and clothing to Rebecca. When the Church was betrothed, she received vessels of gold and silver, in which the treasure of faith might be; for there are vessels for honour, and there are also vessels for dishonour. Hear what the vessels are: We have a treasure in earthen vessels. Our bodies are earthen vessels, our faith is the treasure. And perhaps even the very bodies that hold the treasure are now golden, because they are full of prudence, and silver, because they seem to shine with the utterances of the heavenly command. And the parents too are honoured with gifts.

91. The girl is consulted not about the betrothal — for she awaits the judgement of her parents; it is not for a virgin's modesty to choose a husband —, but, already betrothed to a husband, she is consulted about the day of departure. And not undeservedly she made no delay; for rightly she had to hasten to her husband. Hence it is plain from where that line of Euripides was taken, which many admire. For he says in the person of a woman, who nevertheless wished to leave her husband and was being sought for another marriage: νυμφευμάτων μὲν τῶν ἐμῶν πατὴρ ἐμὸς μέριμναν ἕξει· τοῦτο γὰρ οὐκ ἔστʼ ἐμόν, that is: my father will take up the care of my betrothal; for this is not mine. Therefore, virgins, keep what even the philosophers themselves admired. But also, women, if any young woman who has quickly lost her husband fears to fall into the snare of her own weakness and wishes to marry, let her marry only in the Lord, so that she leaves the choice of a husband to her parents, lest she be thought of too forward a desire if she claims for herself the choice concerning her own marriage. She ought to seem sought by a man rather than to have sought a man herself. Let her put modesty first, before she marries, because modesty commends marriage itself all the more. But they imitate the words; the deeds they cannot imitate.

92. It is clear, too, that there is in this a glorious mystery of the Church, in that no one dared to call her before Christ; for to Christ alone was this prerogative of calling the nations reserved. Once called, however, she made no delay, and was therefore the more acceptable to the Lord, because the people of the Jews, which had been called to the supper, was not worthy to come, whereas the congregation of the Gentiles ran to meet him as soon as it saw itself summoned.

93. Know, then, that it is not without mystery that, being carried on a camel, she was coming to the bridegroom, because the people of the nations, rough with a certain beastly deformity of merits, which had no comeliness of its own form, was to receive the faith and the agreement of the Church. Nor is it idle that, when Rebecca was coming, she saw Isaac walking about and, when she had asked who he was and learned that he was the one to whom she was being brought as wife, she got down and began to veil her head, teaching that modesty ought to go before marriage. For from this marriage too has its name, because girls veiled themselves for modesty's sake. Learn then, virgins, how to keep modesty and not to go out bareheaded before strangers, since Rebecca, already betrothed, thought she should see her appointed husband with her head covered.

94. Who is that servant who provided for this marriage? Surely one of the apostles, and above all he who says: Men, brothers, you know that from days of old God chose among us that the nations should hear by my mouth the word of the Gospel, or he who was called the teacher of the Gentiles; for they, when they are read, gain for Christ a soul that believes what it did not believe before, and to the one who longs to see Christ they show him by their words. So Abraham, having celebrated his son's marriage, completed his days at a great age and in a good old age.

Book Two

Chapter I.

1. We have followed the moral sense of the passage with what simplicity of understanding we could, so that those who read may draw from it lessons of conduct for themselves: but just as, because the sword's edge is sharp on both sides, you fight on both sides, so too the word of God, which is sharper than any sharpest sword, piercing even to the division of the soul, you find ready and fit whichever way you turn, so that it passes through the soul of the reader to reveal the riddles of the prophetic Scriptures. Hence I think it not absurd to carry the sense up to higher things and, through the history of different persons, to unfold a certain progress of the form of virtue, especially since in Adam we have already tasted the beginnings of a deeper understanding. For we said that Adam is the mind, we indicated that Eve is sense, and by the figure of the serpent we expressed delight. But there the mind was turned aside to sin from the highest blessedness and a certain natural pleasantness of the virtues, through the deceit of sense and the allurement of delight; here, however, it is given us to behold the progress of the mind. For the lawgiver did this with foresight, so that, just as he showed the fall of the mind, that we might beware of those paths of error, so too he might indicate the progress of the mind and a certain return on high, so that we might learn how the broken mind can reform itself. For the Lord had cleansed the earth by the pouring in of the flood, he had washed away the filth of human frailty, but this was not enough for progress in virtue unless man were also instructed how he should rule and govern himself . . . . Abraham is brought in in the place of the mind. Indeed Abraham is also called passage. Therefore, so that the mind, which in Adam had given itself wholly to delight and bodily allurements, might pass over into the form and beauty of virtue, a wise man has been set before us to imitate. Indeed Abraham, as the Hebrews have it, is called according to the Latins father, because the mind governs the whole man with a certain fatherly authority, judgement and care.

2. This mind, then, was in Haran, that is, in the caverns, subject to various passions. And therefore it is said to it: Go out from your land, that is, from your body. He whose way of life is in heaven went out from this land. And from he says your kindred. The senses of the body are the kindred of our soul. For our soul is divided in two, into that which is rational and that which is irrational. And in that which is irrational are the senses; therefore they are kindred of the rational part, that is, of the mind. And from your house he says go out. The house of the mind is the uttered word. For just as the father of a household dwells in his house and has it in his power how he shall rule his house, so too the mind dwells in our speech and governs our words, and its force and discipline shine out in speech. As a good father of a household is judged from the very entrance of the house, so too our mind is weighed from our speech. Indeed it even strikes and recalls with the modulations of the voice.

3. Therefore let him who wishes to attain perfect purification separate himself from these three, from the body, from the bodily senses, from the voice, in which are all the passions of the body and the deceits of the senses, by which we are deceived and mocked. For in none of these three is the good; not in the flesh, although the school of Epicurus and many lovers of pleasure too burst themselves praising the delight of the body, nor in the senses, which are often deluded, nor in the sound of the voice, which mostly soothes the soul with false songs, is the perfect good; for these things are corruptible, but what is truly good is incorruptible. And the proof is plain. For when a man dies the flesh is corrupted, the senses perish, the voice is lost: the mind remains immortal, receiving an incorporeal life. Hence it is called into another land, full of blessedness, where it may discern not false things in place of true, as in this life, but the living substance of things, because, having shaken off a certain misty image of the body and of the senses and of the voice, it lays aside the corruptible darkness and with unveiled face surveys with its gaze the grace of the blessed life.

4. I will bless he says you and make you into a great nation. He pledges immortality when he promises a race — for the race seems to be immortal, while the persons of individuals are mortal, as of men, as of horses, as of bees, of which someone says: but the race remains immortal —; yet much better is this, that by a great nation he meant the perpetual posterity of the Church and that heavenly generation, which is truly great, so that . . . . . we die to sin, we are reborn to God.

Chapter II.

5. And Abraham went out, as the Lord had spoken to him. From here the Gentiles bring forth the maxim of the seven wise men, follow God, as their own discovery, though not Abraham only, I say, but even Moses was far earlier, through whom the Law was given, saying: You shall walk after the Lord your God. Abraham went out, then, in whom it was not so much his perfection as the devotion of his soul and the freedom of his mind that went out from the bonds of the body, from the allurements of delight. Indeed you have it thus: Abraham went out, as God had spoken to him. Above you have the Lord saying Go out, in which the open command of one who orders is expressed; here you have: as God had spoken to him. A certain affection, as of conversation, is conveyed; for he did all that had been appointed. Before the deed, then, God tells him, as one bound to obey; after the deed he speaks with him, as with a friend; for he is a friend of God who does what has been commanded. Hence the Lord Jesus too says in his Gospel: You are my friends if you do what I command you. I no longer call you servants. But, as I have said, the progress of the wise man set before us to imitate has been written as a proving, not as perfection. For the mind which fell in the first man is still reforming itself in Abraham, and therefore it gathers itself up by steps and increases.

6. Hence he also added: and Lot went off with him, that is, turning aside. For this is what the interpretation of the name signifies, because, just as travellers taking an unknown road are often misled by certain paths, so that they turn aside from the straight track, and yet, if they are prudent, they do not go astray, but, though hesitating, gather up their way by looking at the region itself, so too Abraham, though wavering, nevertheless followed the track of truth. He was often led on by a false appearance of goods, but he was not wholly turned aside; for it belongs to the perfect not to bend aside, to the prudent not to turn aside wholly. But he alone never bent aside of whom it is written: Behold, a virgin shall conceive in the womb and bear a son, and you shall call his name Emmanuel: he shall eat butter and honey before he knows or brings forth evil, he shall choose the good, for before the child knows good or evil, he does not trust malice, so as to choose what is good. This Abraham could not do, to choose the good before he knew evil, but he clung to the heavenly precepts so as not to turn aside from the truth. Therefore too he is described as having gone out from Haran at seventy-five years of age, because, with the seventieth number of remission completed, the sense is such that it can be bent. For the delights of these senses bring it about that our mind is not always upright, but sometimes bends itself, so that it hides itself not within the bodily caverns but within the hiding-places of pleasure.

7. Yet, though placed even in these burrows, he so escaped that he took his wife and his nephew and every soul that he had possessed in Haran. For the prudent and the continent are possessors of virtue and of a soul that chooses the comeliness of gentle character. But those who are lovers of the body are ensnared by its delights, because all the virtue of irrational creatures lies in the condition of the body, but that of rational ones in the virtues and disciplines of the soul. For this reason it is written that he possessed his soul, ruling it as free and subject to no servitude. This, then, is the teacher's intent: that, though placed even in those byways and windings, whether of an age still young, or of a discipline not yet more perfect, or of a place open to vices, he dwelt there not so bent towards sin that he could not move away. Indeed he defended his mind from that slippery possession and carried it over.

Chapter III.

8. And Abraham passed through he says as far as the place Shechem, to the high oak. Do these things not seem superfluous, unless you seek the reason, since he did not even pass over the height of the oak? But where there is reason, nothing is superfluous. For Shechem means either shoulder or neck, which is a sign of labour and exercise. Hence Jacob too, a man well exercised, gave it as a special gift to his son Joseph. Therefore, since exercise by itself cannot confer perfection without the endowment of nature, and the grace of nature is left destitute if exercise is lacking — for diligence is the support of the gifted —, that man is brought in, in imitation of whom you are formed, made, by exercise joined to the grace of nature, so much more firmly founded and more lofty that he passed through as far as the high oak. This tree, being lofty and also sturdy, is a sign that the soul of holy Abraham was not easily bent by the storms of this world, but remained on high, so as to lift itself from earthly inquiries to the height of divine knowledge.

9. Then at once God appeared to him. Nowhere above do you find that God had been seen by him. Hence it is clear that it must be referred to this: that as long as he was a Chaldaean, that is, not only in the region but also in the opinion of the Chaldaeans, he could not see God, whom he was seeking within the world. For the Chaldaeans say that the world is the higher god, as also the s . . . . . ticarum houses, and they assert that the things that are earthly are borne by the course of the stars and held in check by a certain bond. Hence they have also called the stars gods, because they believe that they have a certain dominion from on high, since there is a certain sympathy of the stars with earthly things. But they ought to have reckoned that one who suffers with others cannot also have, as a god, an imperial right or dominion over those things with whose suffering it falls sick, since it is itself also mortal and corruptible. The world too, since it was made, is certainly not itself God, but he is its maker and founder. Therefore, as long as the mind is bent by Chaldaean errors, it does not see God, whom it seeks among the things that are seen, not among the things that are not seen. But the things that are seen are temporal; for the things that are not seen are eternal. But God is not temporal; therefore he is not seen. Therefore that mind does not see God which follows the teaching of the Chaldaeans. Hence Abraham too did not see him at first. And how could he see him above whom he thought there was another? But when he moved over, not to another region, but to the true religion made ready for humility — for this is what Canaan means —, then he began to see God and to know that he is God by whose invisible power he perceived that all things are ruled and governed. This, then, Scripture teaches, that Abraham, moving away from the observation of the stars, saw God.

10. A confirmation of the testimony is added, in that in that place he built an altar to the Lord who had appeared to him. For this strong type was impressed on his soul, and a plain faith in the truth; for memory is at hand for a grateful man, forgetfulness creeps in on an ungrateful one. In the former, those by whom he is helped cling fast; from the latter, all that is bestowed slips away. He set up an altar, however, but he did not sacrifice. This might disturb you, did you not remember that, in the order of Scripture, progress is kept for this mind. And therefore he was waiting to learn from God the manner of sacrificing. For he perceived that the sacrifice of an irrational animal and a dumb beast did not seem a victim worthy of divine worship. He had not yet recognized in Isaac the type of the passion to come, Melchizedek had not yet given him the grace of blessing, so that he might know these things.

11. He withdrew he says from there to the mountain east of Bethel. The eminence of the mountain signifies an increase of devotion, and its ascent is a sign of richer progress. East, because he was prophesying that the sun of justice was to come, that there wisdom would prepare a house for herself and from there would predestine her rising through a virgin. He wished, then, to receive already the light of the mysteries to be known. For as the world is lit by the sun, so the whole mind will be lit by the splendour of wisdom. And rightly he set down 'east of Bethlehem'. For Bethlehem, in which Christ was born, is called the house of God. Hence God says through the prophet: And you, Bethlehem, are not least among the princes of Judah; for from you shall come forth a prince who shall rule my people. He did not say 'opposite Bethlehem', but called the tent itself Bethlehem; for the Church is the tent of the just. Now who would not marvel at those mysteries, that Bethlehem is by the sea, † aggeę (Aggai) on the east. For either the soul, which deserves to be called the temple of God, or the Church is beaten by the waves of worldly cares, but is not overthrown; it is struck, but not shaken, easily able to press down and calm the commotions of the waves and the uprisings of the bodily passions. It looks on the shipwrecks of others, itself untouched and free from danger, ever ready for Christ to shine upon it and to gain joy for itself by his illumination. For as the eyes are fed by the first light of day, so too our mind is nourished by the discoveries of wisdom and seems to grow bright with certain of its rays. For by the rays of the visible sun the lands are warmed, but invisible rays penetrate the inner recesses of our heart.

12. Again he built an altar and called on the name of the Lord. He signified the progress of faith in calling upon the Lord. This he added to what went before.

Chapter IV.

13. And Abraham went away and stayed in the desert. The mind is tested when it is in a kind of desert, where there is no wantonness of desires, no abundance of money, no expense of luxury. Would that I could be in this desert, bereft of every incitement of desires, abandoned by every zeal for sinning, stripped of the swelling of boastfulness! But because either God allows us to be tempted or the tempter assails us, when the mind seems to itself to be in the desert, at rest from every craving for earthly pleasures, it is driven into Egypt, where it can be pricked. For our flesh is the goad of the mind, and its passions are our prickings. That is our Egypt, that is, our flesh; that is affliction. Into it our mind goes down when it thinks of the things that are fleshly, but it goes up when it desires things invisible. And therefore Abraham too is said to have gone down into Egypt, that he might be afflicted. Our mind undergoes this: at times it separates itself from the body, it sets itself apart so as to act alone, desiring to attend to and cleave to incorporeal things; at times, because of the binding together of soul and body, it is defiled unto fleshly pleasures, to which the weak mind is subjected, while the stronger is not held by them. Afflictions, then, are crowns to the strong man, and infirmities to the feeble. Hence he too did not fear afflictions, by which he would rightly be tested, who says: For even when we had come into Macedonia, our flesh had no rest, but we were afflicted in all things: fightings without, fears within.

14. But famine compelled him to go down into Egypt. For a savage famine of the mind arises when the craving of this flesh overflows, so that it seeks the things that are adverse to salvation. For they reduce us to the straits of the body, when desire for what belongs to another creeps in, when luxury is a delight, and boastfulness dear to the heart. We are all tempted. Even the sober spirit is bent; it goes down into Egypt, that is, into the affliction of the body. Yet it goes down in such a way that it seems to dwell there for a time as a stranger, not to possess it as a citizen. For the just man said: I am a stranger in this land, and elsewhere: Woe is me, that my sojourning is prolonged.

15. Now Abraham, on going down into Egypt, that is, to fierce and barbarous ways that would not know how to defer to virtue, told Sarai, lest they should do harm through envy, not to say that she was his wife but to call herself his sister. And here is a great mystery of the sober mind, which is quickly envied. And therefore, to check envy, it ought to show itself more humble. Let it not claim for itself the sovereignty over all, let it not arrogate to itself alone wisdom as though it were its special possession. This is she whom Solomon acquired as his wife; for a wife is owed especially to one man. And so all desire to be seen worthy of such a union and grieve that anyone should be preferred to them, to enjoy so great a beauty alone. But a sister is joined to very many, by a kind of right or name of kinship. And therefore she keeps her true lover whole and safe from injury.

16. Hence the Egyptians, seeing her, who could neither discern nor recognize the beauty of virtue, judging her by common opinion, brought her in to the tyrant, that is, to the proud mind, which did not bear the weight of wisdom and was therefore afflicted. For when the word of virtue has entered a wicked soul, it convicts it, affects it with shame for its guilt and error, and torments it with grief for its fall. For while we are in some lust for sinning, the mind is covered over with certain clouds of folly, and its eyes are darkened by a kind of smoke of iniquity, so that it does not see the ugliness of the things it desires; but when every cloud has passed and the splendour of wisdom has shone out again, heavy torments are exercised in a kind of secret chamber of the guilty conscience. And so our mind is a sterner judge through the guilt of conscience and the judgement of repentance. But if, either sick through guilt or feeble through infirmity, it has not been able to bear and endure the presence of virtue, it sends her away and banishes her from itself, and does not suffer her to be turned over within it and to cleave to its thoughts. And as weaker eyes shrink from the light, so a feeble mind does not bear the brightness of wisdom. Such were the minds of the Gerasenes, who asked the Lord Jesus to pass out of their borders.

17. Then this king of Egypt too says to Abraham: What is this you have done to me? Why did you not tell me that she is your wife, but said that she is your sister? And I had taken her to myself as wife. And now behold, your woman is before you; take her and hurry back from here. Let us bring before our mind some intemperate man who, having beheld the grace of chastity and being captivated by a certain beauty of hers, thinks she should be pursued; then, not knowing her handmaids, with whom she walks attended and arrives surrounded, namely sobriety and moderation and modesty, sparingness in food, flight from wantonness, impudence and petulance, earnest caution, anxious watchfulness, suddenly, inflamed either by the heat of drunkenness or by the fever of the flesh itself or by the encounter with a comelier form, he does not at all restrain himself nor fight against the law of the flesh. Does he not say: 'I thought it an easier thing to pursue purity: it is above my shoulders, above my strength. Rare is the man to whom these things are joined. Farewell, chastity; withdraw, withdraw from the borders of my senses. Hurry back quickly whence you came. I cannot bear your presence; I am afflicted by grievous questionings, while I judge that you must be held, whom I cannot hold.'

18. Then, turning to someone who admonished him, who had been eager to bring into his mind the habit of purity, alleging that it would not be hard or impossible, but the companion of many, neighbour to the zealous, suited to the willing, 'What is this,' he says, 'you have done to me? Why did you not tell me that she is your wife?' — that is, one who is held not in a perfunctory way but in lawful wedlock, who carries with her a very great dowry, who brings in the heavy burdens of matrimony and the interest due on a hard union — 'but you said a sister, bound by no laws and a companion by nature, not proud and powerful by any right of a dowry estate. And so, unaware of her burdens, I had thought she should be joined to me and kept. But I have understood what weight and load there is in her. Behold your woman, that is, behold your persuasion before you; take her and hurry back. I do not want her before me, I do not want her in my thoughts. Take yourself off from here with your counsels, with your admonition; take yourself off quickly, hurry back quickly: I do not bear your delays, they are afflictions to me: it is enough that I was deceived before.' And he sent his nurslings, by which an over-intemperate mind mostly wanders, turning over in the spirit thoughts of luxury, ambition and avarice, and stumbling upon various allurements, to cast purity out and drive her far away, so that she might not make a return into those borders from which she had been expelled, whereby, now secure and free of any sterner judgement, it might not fear to be convicted in its sins.

Chapter V.

19. So Abraham departed from there, having with him his wife Sarai, that is, the ruling one, not one who serves. And therefore it is also said to him: Listen to Sarah your wife. For she who has stripped herself of the servitude of sins holds sovereignty, not servitude. The stronger mind, then, has with it the ruling virtue, that is, one that commands the senses of the body, not one that obeys them; which brought everything back with it from Egypt, lost there nothing of its disciplines, was not stained by intemperance, insolence, the immoderation of shameful deeds, was not stripped of the cloak of diligent sobriety, was not divested of the garment of modesty.

20. He was very rich, inasmuch as he lacked nothing good, he who did not covet what belonged to another, because he needed nothing that he would wish to be called his own. For this is to be rich: to have what suffices for one's will; for frugality has a measure, wealth has none, since its limit lies in the will of the one who seeks it. And he was rich in cattle, in silver and in gold. What does this mean? It does not seem to me that worldly riches are being praised in a just man. Hence in the cattle I understand the bodily senses, because they too are irrational; in the silver, speech; in the gold, the mind. Rightly was Abraham rich, because he ruled the irrational senses. Indeed he both tamed them and made them gentle, so that they became rational. He had speech splendid with the colour of faith, purified by the grace of spiritual discipline; he had a mind full of prudence. And therefore a good mind is compared to gold, because, just as gold surpasses the other metals, so a good mind in man is more excellent than the other portions of human substance. In three things, then, lies the wealth of the wise man: in sense, speech and mind. A kind of ascent has been made in order, as we also read in the Apostle: But there abide faith, hope, charity, these three: but the greater of these is charity. And so the mind too is the greater, because it is the mind that grinds the spiritual grain, so as to bring forth the cleansing of the senses and of words. Everywhere the character of the wise man is preserved.

21. Then Abraham is presented as having returned there, that is, to Bethel, from which he had gone down into Egypt, so that we may recognize that even the just, set in the house of God and intent upon the word of God, are indeed tempted by worldly afflictions, but are not estranged from the house of God and from the keeping of the heavenly commandments. To be content with one's own borders, not to be lifted up by abundance of riches when things flow according to the favour of pleasures: this belongs to the best mind; always to meditate on the beginning and the end, to go forth to that point and to set out from there: this is good. And the good is wisdom; for no one is good but God alone. From him we proceed, created through him; to him we return, because to be with Christ is far better. And that you may know that it is good for the beginning and the end to agree, the good Lord Jesus himself says: I am the A and the Ω, the beginning and the end.

22. Let our mind, then, be always with him; let it never withdraw from his temple, from his word. Let it be always in the reading of the Scriptures, in meditations, in prayers, so that the word of him who is may always work in us. And as, going forth daily to the church or applying ourselves to prayers at home, we begin from him and end in him, so let the whole day of our life here and the course of each day take its beginning from him and end in him; for just as to believe and to be initiated to God from the beginning of life is salvation, so perseverance too is necessary to the end. And this is the diligence of the best mind: that, intent upon the word of God, it does nothing irrational from which sadness may steal in, so that, continually well conscious of its actions, it may keep the joy of a good conscience. For what is good is without fear and without sadness, that is, full of security and of grace; for the possession of the just man is to be pleasing to God, but none of the foolish is pleasing. Therefore Isaiah says, 'when the good draws near,' 'sorrow and sadness and groaning shall flee.' John too in the Apocalypse: And he himself he says God shall be with them and shall wipe away every tear from their eyes, and death shall be no more, nor mourning nor crying nor pain any longer. For in the resurrection of the just there will be continual joy and grace, when that good has begun to be with his saints, when they shall rest in the bosom of Abraham, set in his tent, which is fixed within the house, or the word, of God and his grace; signifying that the innocence of the faithful gives thanks to its Author, they who have nothing to make them repent of having been in this world.

23. And so by the simple deeds of Abraham the proofs of great teachings are unfolded. Rightly is he rich, who even makes rich the disputations of the philosophers, who shaped their precepts from his conduct.

24. His riches, then, Scripture had set forth. It remained to learn whether Lot too, his nephew, was also rich, as being of the same line; but Scripture asserts that he abounded only in cattle. For it runs thus: And Lot, who walked with Abraham, had sheep and oxen and tents. He did not have silver, because he was not yet just; for the tongue of the just is silver tried by fire. He did not have gold, which he had who saw the back parts of Christ, of whom it is written: and his back parts in the appearance of gold. Abraham saw him, as the Lord testified, saying: Abraham saw my day and was glad. And therefore he deserved to have and possess the appearance of gold.

Chapter VI.

25. Now I think that point must by no means be passed over which seems to have troubled even the more learned: for what reason it is written thus: And Lot, who walked with Abraham, as though there were another Lot who did not walk with him, according to what we have received. And very many think the question is not solved. So, in order to satisfy them and not to depart from the rule of Scripture, we say: one person, two matters, in that two things are signified in one and the same man. In number he is one, in name twofold. For Lot means turning aside, as the Latin interpretation has it; and one turns aside both from good and from evil. So when Lot turned aside from evil, that is, from error, shameful deed, crime, he was joined to his uncle; when he turned aside from good, that is, from the just, the innocent, the holy, the devout, he was allied with shameful deeds. Rightly, then, did it say: And Lot, who walked with Abraham, because he had not yet chosen Sodom, he did not dwell with the authors of shameful deeds; for afterwards he began to dwell in Sodom. And therefore, as though changed from himself, he is understood as another, falling away not only from the just man but from himself.

26. Then, because he had already begun in his inclination to turn away from his uncle, the land could not hold them; for no spaces can be enough for those at discord. For the quiet and peaceable even narrow places are abundant; for those of discordant ways even spacious places are cramped. And because I said from the beginning that the mind of man is being formed here, which had not been perfect from the beginning but makes progress by increments and by certain steps, therefore it says: The land could not hold them, that is, one soul did not admit diverse motions naturally fighting against each other. It can happen, however, that at times not all things are perfect in one and the same person, and yet someone may be able to cover his vices or temper his motions, if either the good things are more numerous, with which he may cover the fewer vices, or he bends a sudden agitation by more mature counsel. But if on both sides many discordant and conflicting things come together, the dwelling of discordant virtues and passions in one soul must needs be dissolved. Figuratively, then, following the natural philosophers, it called the soul land. For Solomon too says: Like farmland is the imprudent man; which, if it is fertile with rich crops of grain, can hide the thorns; but if the thorns compete with the ears, there is no possibility of reaping.

27. Let us consider, then, who the shepherds are, and of what animals, and what the quarrel is between the shepherds of Abraham and the shepherds of Lot. Shepherds are the masters of flocks, either diligent and sober, not allowing the cultivated fields to be trampled by the print of the foot and scorched by teeth, or negligent and slack, who do not call back their flock to where grassy and not fruit-bearing places may be grazed, but let it wander freely through the various fruits of the field. So the skilful watchfulness of these shepherds is necessary, lest perhaps what happened through the carelessness of the negligent should be ascribed to the diligent. But because the discourse is not about visible things, let us first consider of what herd they are the shepherds. We can define these shepherds. Shepherds it says of beasts of burden; and we have understood that beasts of burden signify the irrational senses of the body. Who then are the shepherds of the senses but their teachers and, as it were, certain governors and leaders, or the counsellors of some discourse, or the thoughts of our mind? These, if they are skilled in the shepherd's discipline and hold fast to it, do not permit the flock of the senses to wander too far and to cling to useless or harmful pastures, but call it back with provident leading, apply the reins of reason and resist it when it struggles. But bad teachers or useless disputations allow them to be carried by their own impulse, to rush headlong into the precipice and into danger, to trample the cultivated land, to graze down what bears fruit, so that, if there are any fruits of virtue up to now in the same soul, they scatter these too. Hence, then, the discord of our thoughts. When the flesh fights against the spirit, and the spirit against the flesh, it is no slight battle, since the Apostle himself, the vessel of the Lord's election, says: I see the law of my flesh fighting against the law of my mind and making me captive in the law of sin, which is in my members. He himself had been unable to calm this battle, and therefore he fled to Christ, saying: Unhappy man that I am, who will free me from the body of this death? that is, so that I may not cling to the delights of the flesh. Who then is it that may free me from these bonds, and unite me, set free, to God, and turn my senses toward the sobriety of the soul rather than toward the drunkenness of the body? But because among men he could not find such a governor, turning to God, The grace he says of God through Jesus Christ the Lord. If the stronger man did not entrust himself to his own strength in order to escape the body of death, but sought help from Christ, what ought we, the weaker, to do? Abraham knew this battle to be grievous, and therefore he thought it must be guarded against at the beginning; for the wise man has a zeal for peace, while to the imprudent quarrels are dear.

28. Let there be no quarrel he says between me and you and between my shepherds and your shepherds, because we are men, brothers. We read that Abraham was the uncle and Lot his nephew: how does he call him brother? But notice that the wise man brings forward the causes of concord. Hence he put first: we are men. And all men are the offspring of one nature, conceived within her womb, and warmed and brought forth from one womb. Hence by a kind of right of kinship we are linked together as brothers, founded by one father and born of one mother, as brothers of the same womb. And therefore, since we are the offspring of a rational nature, we ought to love one another with mutual love as brothers of one womb, not to attack and persecute one another. But much more truly it is referred to the one soul, whose rational part has as kindred, as we said above, the irrational senses, while that which is rational has the fellowship of the virtues. Hence the vices and virtues of man are joined to each other by a kind of brotherly bond, because the former are of the flesh, the latter of the rational soul; and flesh and soul, of which man consists, are allied as by a kind of law of wedlock. Man, then, ought to bind his portions together as by a treaty and compel them to peace. But because there was no one great enough to conquer the flesh, therefore there came our peace, who made both one and, loosing the middle wall of partition, the enmities, in his flesh making void the law of commandments by decrees, that he might create the two in himself, making peace in one new man, that he might reconcile both in one body to God through the cross, slaying the enmities in himself. Rightly, then, did the Apostle call himself an unhappy man, since he suffered within himself so great a war, which he could not extinguish. Then, when he spoke of one small portion of the passions, that is, anger, Solomon: Better he says is a wise man than a strong one, and he who restrains anger is better than he who takes a city. Blessed then is he who has come through this war, and that not as a stranger and sojourner but as a citizen with the saints and a member of the household of God, whom, though placed on earth, earthly things do not shake.

29. This disposition holy Abraham desired to keep. And so, as a peaceable man, he first says: Let there be no quarrel between me and you. Then he says: and between my shepherds and between your shepherds. Third he set down: Behold he says the whole land is before you, that is: if it cannot be agreed, I yield everything; take the whole, if the dissension is about place or possession. But if there is no agreement in ways of life, depart from me. How much he put first, so as not to be forced to part! But this too belongs to virtue and discipline. For a man who came from the discipline of philosophy said before us that these four things are in a good man: first, that he labour to make all men his friends; second, that, if he cannot make them friends, at least he make no enemies; third, that, if not even that is within his reach, he depart with this resolve; but if anyone pursues him as he yields, let him avenge himself as he can. But those three earlier ones we recognize in Abraham, not in bare words but in true works.

30. But the fourth is not so, since even toward the one who withdrew he kept a kinsman's affection, so that he not only did not pursue him but even rescued and freed him when he was taken captive. Then the Apostle, while he teaches those three, by his precepts does away with the fourth, which philosophy had added. For he says, when he wished to shape the peaceable people of God: If it can be, as far as lies in you, having peace with all men; then, if this cannot be, at least neither discords nor enmities. And therefore he added: Not avenging yourselves, dearly beloved, in which that fourth too is excluded. Let us by no means wish to avenge ourselves; but give he says place to wrath. You have the third also, that you should rather withdraw and commit vengeance to God than exact it for yourself; although he wished this too to seem said rather according to the Law; for according to the Gospel you have above: Bless those who persecute you. You have these precepts to Timothy in the second epistle. For which cause he says I admonish you to stir up the grace of God which is in you through the laying on of my hands. For God has not given us a spirit of fear, but of power and of love and of sobriety. And again: But you, my dearest son, take strength in grace. You have, then, the precept of the first maxim, that he should bestow grace upon all; then, if he cannot win all by his precepts, that he beware lest he exasperate some by words, that is, lest he make enemies. Hence he says below: These things I bring to your mind, testifying before God: do not contend with words; it is useful for nothing but the subversion of the hearers. But the servant of the Lord must not quarrel, but be gentle toward all. Finely, a little after, he added and supplied the reason, which philosophy did not see: With modesty he says teaching those who resist, in case God should at some time give them repentance to know the truth. That third also, that we should withdraw from those with whom we cannot agree, you have added, when he commands him to speak the things that befit sound doctrine, then to turn aside from the battles of the Law, that is, first to sow grace, then to turn no one away by quarrelling, third that he avoid a heretical man after one correction. For he who is such is subverted and sins, since he is condemned by himself. How shrewdly he withdraws from our vengeance one condemned by his own judgement, as unworthy to be avenged upon! But David plainly removes the zeal for vengeance, saying: If I have repaid evils to those who repaid me.

31. So the mind of the wise man strives to correct the lapses or the irrational motions of the same soul and to join them to itself. For it can happen that things which at times displease are amended with grace. Squandering of patrimony, if it is cut back, has liberality without loss. Modesty is at times too slack; if it is strengthened, it has both the grace of a sense of shame and constancy of purpose. Agitation, if it is tempered, lays aside the harshness of indignation and takes on the praise of vigour. But if the mind cannot amend, let it not exasperate intemperance. He who has detected the ardour of lust, let him restrain it by marriage, lest, while continence is sought, unchastity creep in. And therefore the good teacher: I say he says to the unmarried and to widows: it is good for them if they so remain, even as I. But if they do not contain themselves, let them marry; for it is better to marry than to burn. There are some women left destitute by the untimely death of their husbands, and they cannot contain themselves: I will he says that the younger ones marry, bear children, be mothers of families, give no occasion to the adversary. But if delights please some, and they wish to grow wanton in Christ, affecting the glory of widowhood but not guarding its gravity, he judges that they are to be avoided, as you have it written: But avoid the younger widows.

32. Rightly, then, did Abraham wish to let his nephew go with good grace, whom he could not keep as he turned away from him. So too let the good mind separate and part itself from the headlong and sunken lapse of the irrational. If you he says go to the left, I to the right; or if you to the right, I to the left, that is, the things that are on the right for you are on the left for me, and those that are on the left for you are on the right for me. For to the imprudent man the things of the body are on the right. These he prefers, these he sets in the better part; riches too and honours he puts first; but the grace of attaining immortality he holds on the left, which for the wise man is on the right; for length of life is in his right hand. And the foolish man casts all the virtues of the soul to the left, but the prudent man places them at his right, and the things of the body at his left.

33. And Lot lifted up it says his eyes and beheld all the region of the Jordan. Boastfulness is a friend to those who turn aside from the truth. Then, as Abraham acted more humbly, who offered the choice, so Lot more insolently, who usurped the choice — virtue humbles itself, but iniquity exalts itself — he who ought to have entrusted himself to the more mature, that he might be safer; in short, he did not know how to choose. For first he lifted up his eyes and beheld the region, that is, that thing which was not first in order but third, that is, last. For first are the goods of the soul, second those of the body, that is, health, strength, beauty, grace of form; third are those that befall us, that is, riches, powers, country, friends, glory. The region, then, is placed in the third rank; for it is a matter of habitation.

34. He saw, then, the region which was watered, before God overthrew Sodom and Gomorrah, like the paradise of God and the land of Egypt, until you come to Zothopa. In this, unless you attend carefully, can you say that he erred in choosing the neighbourhood of the Jordan and the places that were watered like the paradise of God? Not, surely, according to the letter; but since the Jordan is also called descent. For he descends who has deserted the fellowship of virtue and chosen appearance, not truth. For paradise is the delightfulness of perfect beatitude, or the foundations of a fruitful soul, in which are the seedbeds of wisdom, of justice and of the other virtues; but the land of Egypt signifies bodily substance, whose seedbeds are the senses and the passions of the body. So, just as the green growth of the virtues has Christ for its fountain, and the richness of spiritual grace by which to abound, so intemperance is a kind of fountain of bodily passions, by which superfluous things are nourished.

35. Finely does Scripture say: Lot chose for himself, that is, turning aside, because God has set before us good and evil, that each may choose what he wishes. Let us not, then, choose what seems more pleasant in appearance, but what excels in truth, lest, when the option has been granted us to follow the better things, we lift up our eyes, enticed by the false beauty of pleasantness, and overshadow the truth of nature as with averted gaze.

36. But that the men in Sodom were savage and sinners in the sight of the Lord exceedingly: this is no slight dispensation, so that you may notice that the gentle God is moved to vengeance by the heavy bitterness of sins, and that not undeservedly Abraham could not obtain pardon for the Sodomites by entreaty, because they were wicked beyond measure. Very many are the more hidden the more wicked they are, who escape the tracking of men, where things are done without a witness, or a just man is circumvented by false testimony; yet he remains just before God, even if he is condemned by men, because God looks not at the outcomes of trials, nor at affairs interwoven with the contrivances of wickedness, but at the naked nature of affairs. But in the examination of men the error of false opinion mostly covers over the force of truth. Susanna remained exceedingly chaste before God even when she was condemned for adultery, because God did not examine the truth of the deed by the assertions of false witnesses, but questioned the conscience of the inmost mind.

Chapter VII.

37. There follows a passage by which we are clearly taught how much the mind advances once the superfluous things of the irrational portion have been drained off, and how much evil vices joined to vices bring. For not idly has Scripture set down: and God said to Abraham, after Lot had departed from him: look with your eyes and see from the place in which you now are, to the south-west and the north and the east and the sea, for all the land, as much as you see, I will give it to you and to your seed for ever. From this, as from a spring, the Stoic philosophers drew the tenet of their doctrine that all things belong to the wise man. For east and west and north and south are portions of the universe; for by them the whole world is enclosed. When God promises that he will give these to Abraham, what else does he declare but that all things are at hand for the wise and faithful man, and that nothing is lacking to him? Hence Solomon too says in Proverbs: the whole world of riches belongs to him who is faithful. How much earlier was Solomon than Zeno, the master of the Stoics and the founder of that very sect! How much earlier than Plato himself, the father of philosophy, or Pythagoras, the inventor of its name! But who is faithful but the wise man? The fool, after all, is changed like the moon, but the wise man remains unmoved in faith.

38. But perhaps you will say: how does the whole world belong to the wise man? Because nature itself gives him a share in all things, even if he himself possesses nothing. For wisdom is the mistress and possessor of all things, since she considers the gifts of nature her own, because they were given for the use of men, and she is in need of nothing, even if what is necessary for sustenance is lacking to her. For just as a musician his instruments, or a physician his medicines, or a shipwright the things that are necessary for fitting out a ship, even if at some time he does not have them, still has them by the very fact that he is able to use them, even if for a time the use of them is not at hand: how much more does the wise man, who lives according to nature, judge whatever belongs to nature to be his own! For he does not lose his right who remembers that he was made in the image of God and that it was said to men by the Lord God: increase and multiply, and fill the earth and have dominion over it, and rule over the fishes of the sea and the birds of heaven and all the cattle and all the earth and all the creeping things that creep upon the earth and who knows that wisdom is the mother of all things and herself possesses the whole world. Finally Solomon, who asked for wisdom and received it from the Lord our God: he himself he says gave me true knowledge of the things that are, so that I might know the ordering of the world and the power of the elements, the beginning and the end and the middle of all things, and the divisions of the times and the courses of the year and the arrangements of the stars, the natures of living creatures and the rages of wild beasts, the force of the winds and the thoughts of men, the differences of plants and the powers of roots, and whatever things are hidden and unforeseen. But these things are at hand for no one but the perfect man.

39. Finally, as long as Lot, that is, the deviation of character, clung to Abraham, he had not received the allotment of these things. But when, set free from a certain ambiguity and winding of deviation, he began to pursue the straight paths of the virtues with the continuous steps of his soul, he is sent as possessor into all the land, and it is said to him: arise and walk through the land in length and breadth, for I will give it to you and to your seed for ever. Therefore he who has merited wisdom and has not been the son of the handmaid, not the slave of sin nor subject to the succession of the flesh, but the son of the free woman, that is, of that Sarah who does not serve but rules, of good stock, of good nature, will acquire by the title of perfect virtue the inheritance of the universe. So it is said to Abraham: arise. He does not mean a bodily rising but a spiritual one, that is: arise, you who sleep, arise from earthly things, arise from bodily things, leave earthly things behind, look upon heaven and rise up from the dead, that is, from vain opinions and the disputations of the Chaldaeans. Gaze upon the world, gaze also upon him who is able to give the whole world. Into your possession, he says, I will give you the world, which you formerly believed to be God.

40. Walk through the land in its length and breadth. Certainly he could not, within a moment, walk through this land, enclosed by the empires of the Persians, from the shores of India too as far as the Pillars of Hercules, as they say, or the farthest borders of Britain. And he could have seemed lacking in devotion, as it were, as one who had not obeyed the heavenly oracle, if he had received a command to go round this land; but since his devotion is proved, because he had moved his tent only as far as the oak of Mamre, we can certainly understand the land, that is, perfect virtue, which would yield good fruits and fruitful discoveries and the first-fruits of thoughts, the vintage of merits, and would fill the inner house with grain, wine and oil: the land of the resurrection, which he promised to our fathers flowing with milk and honey, the sweetness of life, the grace of gladness, the splendour of glory, of which the firstborn from the dead, the Son of God, the Lord Jesus, was made the first heir. And therefore he did not say 'to your seeds' but 'to your seed,' in order to make known him who would be the first to acquire this inheritance for the human race.

41. We have learned the progress of the good mind, which, rising up out of the vice of slippery deviation, at once sought the reward of wisdom, the inheritance of justice. But how much attendant vices harm fickleness, the series of the readings that follow teaches. For those four kings, who triumphed over the five kings and carried off all the cavalry of Sodom, also took Lot, the son of Abraham's brother, and departed. The five kings are the five senses of our body, sight, smell, taste, touch, hearing; the four kings are the bodily and worldly allurements, since both the flesh of man and the world consist of four elements. Rightly they are called kings, because fault has its own dominion, has a great kingdom. Hence the Apostle says: let not sin reign in your mortal body. Our senses, then, easily yield to bodily and worldly delights and are captured by a certain power of theirs. For bodily delights and the allurements of this world nothing conquers but the mind which has become spiritual, cleaving to God and separating itself wholly from earthly things: every deviation is captured by them. Hence John says: woe to those who dwell on the earth! He certainly does not include all men who will then have run the course of this life — for there are, even placed on earth, those whose way of life is in heaven — but those whom affection for an earthly way of life and the favour of this world have overcome. Therefore we are not inhabitants but sojourners of this earth. For the sojourner bears the hope of a temporary lodging, but the inhabitant seems to place all his hope and the use of his substance there where he has thought he must dwell. And so he who is a sojourner of the earth is an inhabitant of heaven, but he who is an inhabitant of the earth is a possessor of death.

42. Abraham numbered three hundred and eighteen of his home-born servants . . . . as far as Hobah, which is on the right of Damascus. And the number is a number of life. For in it is life, if we believe in the passion in the name of the Lord Jesus. For this is the interpretation of this name which we have mentioned, Hobah, that is, life. And it is beautifully said to be on the right of Damascus; for the lambs are on the right, but the goats on the left. The trained mind knows whom to take to itself to bring the battle to its end, with what arms to equip them, under what standards to lead them. It does not carry before it images of eagles or dragons, but it advances to battle in the cross of Christ and the name of Jesus, strong in this sign, faithful under this standard. Rightly, then, is it a trained mind, which has received the true wisdom of the just man. But justice is skilled in correction, and by reproving it calls sinners back and curbs the onsets of the passions.

43. That is why Scripture says that he called back all the cavalry of Sodom, that is, he held the reins, he put on the bridles of reason, he called back the fault, he brought error to a halt. For the horse does not know how to stand still: swift to the charge, lifting up its neck, neighing for lust. What is so like sin? For fault boils up at its first onset and outruns every thought of what is right, and leaps out with an untimely motion, so that reason can hardly call it back. It is carried headlong and throws its rider into this sea of this world, refusing with swollen neck the yoke of correction. There is a particular form of lust which changes a man's voice, corrupts the words of the lover, and betrays itself by its own utterances. Finally the Lord God says to Judah through Jeremiah: now shall be seen your shame and your adultery and your neighing and the estrangement of your fornication upon the hills. This cavalry the just man brought back, and he also converted the character of the one who was turning aside and called them to himself, so that those who had deviated might become his imitators; because our senses run back to the discipline of the mind, he also recovered the substance.

44. He certainly does not mean patrimony, but the vital substance of the soul, in which there is precious wealth, not stubble, not hay, in which there is the splendour of faithful speech, in which the wealth of our hope subsists. For this is our true substance, which is rich in the abundance of wisdom; this is the immortal substance, whereas the use of the body or of accidents is of a day rather than of long duration. Hence some rightly think that patrimony is not called substance; for we do not subsist in it, since even those who lack money do not, for all that, lack the substance of life.

Chapter VIII.

45. Of Melchizedek we have spoken fully in the moral treatise, in which the mystery too was by no means passed over or left aside; but in this place it is enough to point out only this: that the mind full of prudence and justice is the more devoted to the worship of God, and pays its tithes in him, in fruits according to what the earth brings forth, and according to a loftier prudence, so that it offers to God all the perfection of its senses and works and claims nothing for itself. For it cannot rule itself unless it is propped up by divine favour. Indeed, when it thinks it has conquered, it is tempted and assailed. This the reading expresses and teaches: that our mind must always keep guard against the passions of the body, as though on watch. For what does it mean when it says: The king it says of Sodom went out to meet Abraham and said: Give me the men, but take the horses for yourself, except that after these victories over luxury a certain force of lust can steal upon the rational mind, to pour irrational passions into it?

46. But it belongs to the perfect mind to take nothing of earthly things, nothing of bodily allurements, to abstain from earthly things. Therefore Abraham says: I will take nothing of all that is yours. He turns aside, as it were, from the contagion of intemperance; he flees, as it were, the stain of the bodily senses; he rejects worldly delights, seeking the things that are above the world. This is to stretch out the hands to the Lord; the hand is the working virtue of the soul. This he stretches out not to the fruit of an earthly tree but to the Lord, who made he says heaven and earth, that is, the intelligible and the visible substance; for the intelligible usia is heaven, the visible or sensible substance is earth. He signifies, then, that he stretches the virtue of his soul to the higher things, so that from that intelligible substance he may put on the loftiness of the contemplative life, looking not at the things that are seen but at those that are not seen, that is, not at earthly, not at bodily, not at present things, but at incorporeal, eternal, heavenly ones; and that from this visible substance he may take up the grace of the practical and civic discipline.

47. To these things he has joined the oracle of the Lord, who says: Do not fear, Abraham; I will protect you. Your reward shall be very great. I ask, why after the outcome of the war? The time for pledging a reward was now. For he would have done something less wonderful had he attacked the enemy in pursuit of God's promise. Sure of victory, he would have gone forth to a triumph, more as one invited than as one eager for glory or ready to avenge the grief of his devotion. The purpose of a devout mind does not seek a reward, but holds as its reward the consciousness of a good deed and the accomplishment of a just work. Let narrow minds be enticed by promises, let them be roused by hoped-for rewards: the good mind, which has taken up the contest without the bond of a heavenly answer, gains for itself the fruit of a twofold praise, so that it lays up the credit both of a most confident fortitude and of a most complete devotion. This is fitting to be judged of holy Abraham, because he both thought it right, in his just griefs, not to disdain the divine favour, and struck down the enemy in contempt of the danger, which he thought it glorious for him to undergo for the avenging of devotion. In this also is proclaimed the justice of God, who bestows recompense on devout minds not out of the necessity of a promise but out of regard for his own equity, judging it fitting that those who serve as soldiers without any recompense of human reward should have a prize laid up in the goodness of him to whom they have judged their souls ought to be devoted; and at the same time because for the glories of war a prize is provided from the profit of the victory itself or from the favour of men, whereas the prize of devotion and frugality, of purity and of the other, as it were private, virtues is paid by God. The things that are manifest to men, men themselves reward; but not all things are manifest: some are manifest, others uncertain, and above all the hidden things of the heart. Hence he too says: The uncertain and hidden things of the heart you have made manifest to me; and of these God is the beholder and searcher. He would not, then, have promised Abraham a great reward, had he not judged his soul pure from all contagion of sins.

48. Yet the holy and prophetic mind has a greater care for a perpetual posterity; for it desires the offspring of wisdom and the inheritance of faith. Therefore he says: What will you give me? But I am sent away without sons. He desired the offspring of the Church, he sought that succession which would be not servile but free, not according to the flesh but according to grace. And so a divine answer of this kind rang out, by which he was taught and heard: Look up to heaven and count the stars, if you can count them. And he said: So shall your seed be. And Abraham believed God, and it was reckoned to him as righteousness. What did he believe? That is: not only the multitude of the peoples believing in Christ, but also the splendour of heavenly grace and the resurrection of immortal life that was to be bestowed on the offspring of the Church. And what does it mean when it says: But he led him outside? The prophet is led outside, as it were, so that he may go out through the doors of the body and the straits of the enclosing flesh, and see the infusion and, as it were, a kind of descent of the Holy Spirit. We too must go out of these straits of our lodging, cleanse the place of our soul from every defilement, cast out the filth of malevolence, if we wish to receive the spirit of wisdom, because wisdom will not enter into a malevolent soul. And Abraham believed, not enticed by the testimony of gold or of silver, but because he believed with the heart unto righteousness. In that in which his merit was proved, in that the reward was paid in full.

49. And so at once the Lord gave testimony to his faith, saying: I am your God, who brought you out of the region of the Chaldaeans, to give you this land, that you might be its heir. And because he had laid aside the pursuits of the Chaldaeans, Abraham asks: How he says shall I understand that I shall be its heir? That is: I have already rejected the divinations of the magi; teach me how I may know that I shall be heir of that land. He who asks how he may know does not doubt that he can know when God makes it manifest, but wishes to perceive the manner of acquiring the knowledge. For in the Gospel too, when Mary had heard from the angel that as a virgin she would bear a son, she answered: How shall this be, since I have not known a man? And rightly she answered, that is: since what belongs to nature is not at hand, because she who has not been joined to a man does not usually give birth, I ask how, beyond the ordinances of nature, I can bring forth as a virgin?

50. And the Lord God said to him: Take for me a calf three years old, a she-goat three years old, a ram three years old, a turtle-dove and a dove. I would pass over the interpretation of a sacrifice of this kind, did I not notice that a scruple arises from it for some, because a kind of rite of haruspicy seems to be described, in that after the slaughter the animals were divided and set face to face with each other, and Abraham sat down by them. But if we consider the force of the question put before and of the answer that follows, we shall be able to perceive that the teaching of this sacrifice agrees with our hope and faith. For the calf is a ploughing animal, given over to earthly labour. The she-goat is figured in riddles after the likeness of waters, because in Greek αἴξ received its name παρὰ τὸ ἀίσσειν, from what means to be borne along with a rush. For it runs just as water does: we can judge this either from the sound and course of rivers or from the violent waves of the sea. And the ram is compared to the air, because this animal is found to be more useful to the human race than all other animals, since it also furnishes us with the use of clothing, just as the air supplies us with the life-giving substance of this breath. Hence too I think this order was made, that he should first say: Take for me a calf and a she-goat and should name the ram in the third place, because those first ones, that is, the heifer and the she-goat, are compared to the material elements, the lands and the sea, and because they are called feminine, whereas the ram is a male animal, vehement by nature and violent with its horns. And likewise the breath of this air is life-giving, as a male author and cause of things that beget, moving the generative powers of the lands and, as it were, mingling itself with them in a kind of union. So of these three animals one kind is mystically figured with reference to the earth, another to the sea, another to the life-giving air.

51. This teaching is natural. But a moral one also concurs and is at hand. For in all men there is flesh, sense and word. Our flesh is the heifer: it toils to sow, it toils to gather, it toils to give birth; it is wearied by countless labours. Hence the Greeks too called the heifer δάμαλιν, ἀπὸ τοῦ δαμασθῆναι αὐτήν, because it is tamed by hardships. It performs the labours of male oxen with the plough and the yoke, and it shows forth the labours of females in giving birth and in its abundant udder. Our flesh too is put under the yoke of the necessities of this life, is shaken by frequent pains, and grows old, bowed by a kind of childbearing of many hardships. Now who does not know that the virtue of the soul is the more vehement, the soul to which the bodily substance clings, as though wedded to it, in the course of this life? But our senses, in the manner of she-goats, leap out as with a kind of bound and graze on the steeper places, themselves by the force of their impulse stirring up commotions of the soul and shaking it. They are ready at every occasion, whether at the meeting with a woman's beauty or at the scent of some sweetness; they are moved swiftly by hearing and touch alike, and through them they also bend the constancy of the soul and, as it were, estrange it from its own nature. Hence many think that it is called ἀφορμήν, because ὁρμή means impulse, since the cause of our being bent and estranged arises from a certain impulse of the senses. And it has the appearance of the female sex, as our senses also have; for in Greek too they are called αἰσθήσεις, a feminine word. Like animals that have given birth, they are quickly emptied when they have poured out the offspring of their generation and delight, and when their desires are stirred again they bring back new impulses.

52. In the ram, however, is found a likeness of our word and speech, because it is vehement, just as our speech too is effective in operation and is a kind of cause of our adornment and covering. The ram is such a cause through the use of clothing, and leads the flock in a certain order, just as a certain order of our life and practice is unfolded by the word. But I think we ought rather to understand that Word which is the Word of God, with which this ram seems to have no slight kinship, because the Word has clothed us with the true covering of his own fleece and brings us into the house of eternal salvation, he who offered himself to be sacrificed for us, who was led as a sheep to the sacrifice, and as a ram before the shearer is voiceless, so he did not open his mouth. From him we have a certain order both of our substance and of the sacred redemption, because through him we were created and redeemed. So there is a twofold cause through the Word, natural and moral: natural because he created, moral because he redeemed. Philosophy too establishes a twofold form of itself in the word, natural and moral — for the rational is the portion of both — natural according to the creation of the world, which it assigns to the word, moral according to justice and consistency of living, whose life and reason come from the word.

53. For this reason, when eight days had been completed from the virgin Mary's giving birth, they brought our Lord Jesus to Jerusalem, to present him to the Lord according to the Law and to give as an offering a pair of turtle-doves or two young doves, because in the dove there is spiritual grace, and in the turtle-dove the nature of incorrupt generation or the purity of an immaculate body. Rightly, then, are the turtle-dove and the dove commanded to be taken for the sacrifice after the ram, so that you may understand that incorrupt purity and spiritual grace cling to the Word. And by the very fact that he set down birds, we can understand the flights of heavenly merits. For there are the birds of heaven, which come and dwell in the branches of that tree which has sprung up from a grain of mustard seed, to which the kingdom of heaven is compared. And Ezekiel says that the heavens were opened to him, and that among other things he saw also one wheel upon the earth joined to the four living creatures. And below: I heard he says the voice of their wings, like the voice of many waters and like the voice of the Sufficient One, and when they went, like the voice of a word, like the voice of a camp.

54. From this some have drawn it off into the books of philosophy, on the ground that heaven itself is like a winged thing. Indeed Plato said that heaven is a winged chariot, from what the prophet had said: When the living creatures went, the wheels joined to them went too, and when the living creatures lifted themselves from the earth, the wheels were lifted up. But the prophet did not call heaven itself a bird; he said that there are birds in heaven. So too David says: The heavens tell the glory of God, that is, the heavenly powers; or else, when the beautiful element is beheld, its maker is proclaimed. But the prophet is describing the soul, whose four motions are like horses, λογιστικὸν θυμικὸν ἐπιθυμητικὸν διορατικόν (rational, spirited, desiring, discerning). These are the four living creatures, that is, the man λογικόν, the lion θυμικόν, the calf ἐπιθυμητικόν, the eagle διορατικόν. Therefore reason has been placed first, so that the rest may follow reason. Therefore with the man, on the right, is the lion, that is: with reason, on the right, is the commotion. When these living creatures are lifted up, the wheels too are lifted up. And the wheels are the life upon earth by which we live. If the four motions of our soul are lifted up, our life too is lifted up. And so he added: For the spirit he says of life was in the wheels. It is rather the soul, then, that is the chariot, the soul which says in the Song of Songs: You made me the chariots of Aminadab, that is, of our God. So the prophetic description does not agree with the tradition of philosophy. Indeed the prophet says that he heard the voice of the wings. These wings are the virtues, which with the greatest and a double beating of prudence, fortitude, temperance and justice resound a sweet beauty, the song of life. Plato, however, borrowed the notion that certain sweet sounds of the stars are produced by the revolution of the heavenly sphere, following renown and display rather than truth. For although our Origen too, that is, a man devoted to the Church's ministry, asserts that there is, through the motion of the planet stars, a certain indescribable harmony of that most sweet heavenly sound, yet many of his writings testify that he too indulges the tradition of the philosophers very much. I have written this in order to separate the interpretation of this sacrifice both from the tradition of haruspicy and from that of philosophy. Let others wish to prove their learning; I, following the Apostle, prefer to seem timid rather than learned, for he says: See that no one plunders you through philosophy and empty seduction, according to the tradition of men, according to the element of this world, and not according to Christ.

55. As to his saying: Abraham divided the bodies and sat down by them, lest anyone think it an inspection of entrails, I do not deny that the words belong to haruspicy — for so I hear — but since the reading asserts that a division was made of the bodies of the four-footed animals and not of the birds, surely, if there had been an inspection of these, a division of the birds too would have been made, so that the inspection might take place. What if this agrees with our faith? And so let us seek what the teaching of the word gives us beyond haruspicy. We said above that in the heifer we take the earth, in the she-goat water, in the ram air. This is gathered from the very name, in that they are commanded to be taken for the sacrifice three years old, because the earth itself is divided among three kinds of itself — for it is either mainland or island or peninsula — and water itself into three, because it is either sea or rivers or lakes; for springs or wells are private matters, by no means worthy of a general and public division; wells lie hidden, springs give origin to the others. The air too has the divisions of the seasons, spring, summer and winter. And this is the division of the world. To what does this serve? That we may know that God is the author of these things and the ruler of all, who has given order to all things and has distinguished them by that division, so that from these you may gather that God can bestow on you what you devoutly ask, and fulfil what he pledges.

56. And so Abraham, because he had answered the Lord who promised him the inheritance of the land: Whence shall I know that I shall be heir of this land?, is instructed through those kinds of victims to believe that God is above the world, who has divided all the things of the world with provident distinction, but that the things which are divided are dissolved afterwards, while the things which are not divided — for he did not divide the birds, that is, the turtle-dove and the dove — are never dissolved. For faith remains whole, which in the manner of the dove rises on high, surveying the things above and flying round heaven on the spiritual oarage of its wings. To the turtle-dove too is compared that mind which, after the habit of this bird, is nourished in secret places, seeking that intelligible and undivided substance of the Trinity, shunning a kind of rabble of creatures, not mingling itself with a bodily company, and setting itself apart from every stain of the passions. This sacrifice is asked of you. He who offers such victims, faith and purity of mind, the grace of simplicity, the affection of charity and peace, recognizes himself as heir of that blessed land, as the Lord too declared more openly in the Gospel, saying: Blessed are the peacemakers, for they shall possess the land.

57. Take another division. Our flesh too has all its members divided by the very ordering of God. There are two eyes, two ears, two cheeks, divided nostrils, a double row of teeth. Breasts, shoulders, hands, sides, thighs, knees, feet, shins: are they not all in pairs, so that all our functions may seem to be supported by doubled aids? The soul too admits a division of its parts. For διορατικόν, that is, the higher parts, as if certain opposite eyes, is the rational and the irrational. The rational itself is divided into mind and speech, its sensible part into hearing and sight, by which the grace of this life is heaped up. For smell and taste seem to provide a necessary service for the use of life. The nostrils, by receiving the life-giving inhalation with continual breath, feed the substance of man with a kind of continuous food; taste is produced by drink and meals; while the fifth sense, that is, touch, is as it were mixed in with those four. Smell and taste are rather certain nourishments of the body, by which the service of this flesh is sustained, while sight and hearing help the mind. These are the divisions which have been divided by the supreme maker according to our flesh and soul.

58. Hence we must gather that this world too is arranged as though through certain paired members and, as it were, ἀντιπρόσωπα (facing each other): the earth into mountains and plains, as some parts of our body are higher, others flatter. The shoulders stand out, and the upper parts of the feet or of the hands, while the sides and the lower parts of the neck are like worn and hollow valleys. This may also be understood of the palm of the hand; for who doubts that in the very heel of the foot some parts stand out while the middle parts are curved inward? Water is salt in the sea, sweet in the river or in springs. The air is cold in winter, temperate in the months of spring, hot in summer. These things, then, the maker divided; but our mind, which, borne like the birds on the oarage of diverse virtues and of its own vigour, flies up above heaven, he did not divide, because it clings to the Trinity, which divides all things and alone is undivided. Hence the philosophers hold that the higher substance of this world, which they call ether, does not consist of an admixture of the other elements, but, being splendid and shining with much light, and admitting nothing of the filth of the earth, nor of the moisture of the waters, nor of the cloudiness of the air, nor of the redness of fire itself, they assert to be of a certain fifth usia, and to be, as it were, the winged mind of this world, swifter and purer than the other parts. For the other parts are mixed with one another and compounded; but we think nothing exempt and free from material composition except that sole substance of the venerable Trinity, which is truly pure and simple, of a sincere and unmixed nature, although some think that a brighter light is of that fifth usia, the light of which David said that God is clothed about with light as with a garment, and of which the Apostle wrote concerning the almighty God himself that he alone has immortality and dwells in light inaccessible.

59. But for what reason he said that the birds came down upon the divided bodies of the heifer, the she-goat and the ram, I do not easily find, unless it is that all things earthly, maritime and even aerial are full of snares and disturbances. For these birds seem to have come down upon the bodies for the sake of food. And by nature the more violent and those stronger in might rush upon the weaker, and fall upon them as though upon dead bodies, often rushing in unexpectedly; or, as I think more truly, because the prince of this world and the birds of heaven, the spiritual wickednesses which are in the heavenly places, assail with grievous onset those who are divided by worldly anxiety and care, and tear them like the corpses of the dead with harsh tooth. For of these it was said: Leave the dead to bury their dead, because they are of the kingdom of the devil, who is divided against himself. But those who are of the kingdom of God, to whom Jesus says: The kingdom of God is within you, these are not divided, because they cling to God, for he who clings to a harlot is one body; for the two, it says, shall be in one flesh. But he who clings to the Lord is one spirit. These, then, are not dead bodies, for the birds of heaven to eat them, but they are spirits; for his angels God makes spirits. Indeed they did not come down upon the dove and the turtle-dove, because these birds had not been divided; for the just are not divided, to whom it is said that they should be simple as doves. And so David says that the sparrow has found itself a house, and the turtle-dove a nest for herself, where she may lay her young. These things Abraham was watching, these he was considering with a deep and spiritual gaze.

60. And so it is written: Abraham sat down by them, not as an interpreter of haruspicy but as an interpreter of heavenly revelation, searching out the signs of the divine working. For the mind directed to the grace of Christ saw that this world is full of iniquity, which flies as it were from the height of heaven and crushes the lowest of the earth, but that chastity, faith and sincerity are subject to no passions, while avarice and the anxieties of the world, by which those who have the pleasures of riches are choked, are torn apart and divided. Hence riches too have been called the cares and thoughts of this world, because they divide the mind, split it in different directions, drag it into parts, and do not allow it to be incorrupt and whole. So the man of peaceable mind sat by and considered how far he could ward off at close quarters those evils which are brought upon men; for the mind of the wise and just man is eager to heal human misfortunes and to ward off and cut away the troubles of our souls.

Chapter IX.

61. Then what follows shows with what spiritual and prophetic affection Abraham did this. For at sunset an ecstasy fell upon Abraham, and behold, a great dark fear lay upon him. Ecstasy is wont to come upon prophets, as you have it that the prophet said: I said in my ecstasy: every man is a liar. For the mind of the prophet goes beyond, as it were, certain bounds of human prudence when it is filled with God; and first it empties itself of the thoughts and disputes of this world, so as to offer itself pure and emptied to the spiritual grace that comes to it, and so that the Holy Spirit may come upon it, pouring himself in with great force, so that the mind of man is suddenly troubled. Thus the angel came to Mary — and he had come with care and with grace; and yet Mary was moved at his coming in. Hence the angel says to her: Fear not, Mary; for you have found grace with God. And behold, you shall conceive in your womb and bear a son. We know, then, that when the grace of God comes upon the prophetic mind it rushes in suddenly, and hence we read that the Holy Spirit lay upon the prophet and fell down upon him, because he undergoes ecstasy and is troubled and fears and is overspread with certain darknesses of ignorance and want of prudence, as we also read in the Acts of the Apostles that a light from heaven shone round about Saul, and he fell, and was troubled with horror of spirit, and heard a voice from heaven saying: Saul, Saul, why do you persecute me? For he who begins to hear divine things ceases to see worldly things. Hence you ought not to wonder at Abraham's fear and darkness, as though they had befallen him contrary to his virtue or merit, since you observe that such things, when they are joined to them, accord with the custom of the prophets, so that they may know things to come.

62. And straightway you have it said to him: knowing you shall know that your seed shall be a sojourner in a land not its own, and they shall be oppressed with servitude, and they shall harm them and humble them, behold, four hundred years. Rightly did a great dark horror come about, because great oracles were being delivered, since a precept was being given concerning the people of the ages. How could the human mind grasp this so easily, above all a mind that was being admonished to be a sojourner in this land? For it was not so much things that were to come that were being declared, as things that we were to do that were being prescribed. For your seed he says shall be a sojourner, whether because all of us men ought to be sojourners in this land — for Abraham is the father of all — or because the true seed of Abraham is a sojourner in this world; for that is the true seed of which it was said: in Isaac shall your seed be. Thus he who acknowledged himself the heir of Abraham says: I am a stranger in this land and a sojourner, as all my fathers were. For he who has been a sojourner here is a citizen in heaven; but he who has thought that the whole substance of his soul must be established in this land, and has exulted in acquiring for himself the inheritance of this land, will be shut out from the kingdom of God. Hence the Apostle says to faithful men, citizens of that Jerusalem which is in heaven, and sons of the Church: So now you are no longer strangers and sojourners, but you are citizens with the saints and members of the household of God. This land, then, is not ours, the land of which the prince of this world can say, showing all the kingdoms of the earth: To you I will give this power, if you fall down and worship me: all things shall be yours which strangers claim for themselves; and they oppress the people of God with servitude, so that they try to harm his servants and to humble the saints of God. Thus the Son of God himself thought that nothing of this world was to be claimed for himself; hence he says: the prince of this world comes, and in me he will find nothing. Different masters, then, wish to hold us in servitude: the devil advances, his angels harass us, the passions and motions of the body disquiet us like enemies of our own house and within. Without, battles; within, fears; without, battles; within, desires. For the substance of the inner body is alien to the purity of the heart, and therefore it assails it, or at least fights back against it. The war, then, is daily, and within the same camp the battle is heavy, until the merciful God judges the devil and his ministers, quenches the passions and subjects them to the diligent mind, and requires our souls from all the authors of our offence and our peril, he who says: the blood of your souls I will require from the hands of all the beasts. And John saw and said that death and hell were cast into the lake of fire.

63. And so the just will go out in such a way as to leave nothing of their own in this land, lest their spoils remain with the inhabitants and possessors of this land. They will also depart from this land of Egypt in such a way as to carry off with them the vessels which they took from the Egyptians as a loan, whether of gold, he says, or of silver, to use for a time, and to despoil the Egyptians. These vessels they received by the will of the Lord, and they will carry them off with them, because they are sons of the resurrection, to whom it is said: not a hair of your head shall perish. These vessels the Egyptians gave, vessels that were taken from the land of affliction, and some are of gold, others of silver, because every creature of God is good, and above all that of man, the most excellent on earth, which God honoured by breathing into his face the spirit of life and setting him over all living things. These are those vessels of which the Apostle says: we have a treasure in earthen vessels. This is the garment of the Egyptians with which our soul is clothed, so that it may withdraw from here the richer and be freed from that in which it laboured hard here. For not only does every creature groan and travail, but we also, until the redemption of our body comes. The Lord has care of his work; he has care of a nature that in most respects is ready for reason and benevolence. For neither do we allow trees to be cut down and perish whose bearing has burst forth for us around their first fruits, if in a later age either the wind strips them or the sun scorches them, so that in supplying and furnishing strength the substance of the old soul fails through droughts; but we put off their testing to better breezes. And therefore our God, the rewarder of good will, having regard also to the first bearing and the natural disposition, allows the whole patrimony of our soul to be gathered and reserved, to test it by the mild season of his coming advent.

64. Mindful, then, of his gift, which he willed to be immortal for the just, if sin had not crept in, and a grievous sin, which made it not expedient to live long, he says to Abraham: but you shall go to your parents in peace, nourished in a good old age. He allows us to depart from this world, so that by the withdrawal of the soul this body may be dissolved into its own earth and there may be an end of sin, and then that it may be formed anew through the resurrection by the grace of the divine generosity. And therefore he says to Abraham: you shall go to your fathers. Some have thought that the fathers are the elements of which our flesh consists while we live, and into which we are dissolved. But we, who remember that our mother is Jerusalem, which is above, which is free, which is the mother of all of us, as the Apostle says, assert that those are the fathers who went before in merit and in order of life. There was Abel, the devoted victim; there was the devoted and holy Enoch; there was Noah: to them Abraham's passage is promised. For he passes over who, departing from this life, migrates to another life, the life which the mind of the wise and just man lives, who is nourished in peace; for the foolish man is nourished in war and in discords, the just man lives in a good old age. He did not say 'long' but good, because the just man grows old well, but none of the unjust does, though he should live a longer life than the long-lived stags. For to live long is common to the wise and the foolish, but to live well is proper to the wise man, whose old age is venerable, and the age of old age is a spotless life, not a lengthy one he says, nor reckoned by the number of years, nor by grey hairs on the head, but by understanding. He, then, grows old well who has understood well.

65. But in the fourth generation they shall return. The history indeed seems to fit the Jews, who passed into Egypt and went out of Egypt. For four hundred and thirty years were their years completed there; but not all of them lived a hundred years and the years beyond, as Moses or Joshua son of Nun did, so that the time of the fourth generation should fit. Hence let us rather seek something mystical, in that the tetrad is apt for all numbers and is a kind of root and foundation of ten, and also the middle of the hebdomad. Thus Psalm 93 (94) is headed "for the fourth day of the week", because this number is the middle of those that come before and after it: for three precede it, first, second, third, and three follow it, fifth, sixth, seventh. He who sings this psalm passes through the life of this world as with fitting numbers, as one square, stable and perfect. In four books the Gospel is full and perfect. There are four mystical living creatures, and also four parts of this world, from which the sons of the Church, gathered together, have spread the most sacred kingdom of Christ, coming from the east and the west, the north and the south. On a fourfold side, then, the holy Church has risen. The decad too rises from this number. For if you join the numbers from one up to four, in this way you will make ten. Count one, add two to it: they make three. Add three to three: they make six. And to six add four: they make ten. The tetrad, then, fills up ten; the decad embraces every number. There are also four ages of man: boyhood, adolescence, youth, maturity. Little by little wisdom rises and is grounded. And so the summit of prudence comes in the fourth order of the ages. And rightly, even if a man was formerly under the king of Egypt, yet at the age of riper counsel he goes out from his power and acknowledges the Law that he must follow. Then the sea of this life becomes passable for him. A like reasoning holds for the things that spring from the soil. When the seed has been sown, it is loosened in the ground and first breaks out into a root, then sprouts; the fruit is formed and afterwards ripens. The trees themselves too first bear fruit, then the fruit itself grows, with the passage of time changes its colour, and in the fourth order, that is, the last, is brought to completion. So then let us too, in this land of affliction, flee from forming bricks, but with groaning and tears call forth the mercy of the Lord, that he may send us Moses and Aaron, that is, the Law and the priest, but that true priest and prince of priests, of whom, though he lived among men, it was said: behold, the spirit before our face, Christ the Lord, and that he may free us from the land of Egypt, so that we may celebrate the Passover of the Lord. Let us also bear the fruit of faith from boyhood itself, increase it in adolescence, colour it in youth, complete it in old age. For already the axe is laid to the roots of the trees, so that he who makes fruit may bear it. Let the reaper also find our crop grown and ripe, so that he may store ripe fruits in his barns, lest winter overtake them unripe, the wind shake them down, the rain spoil them.

66. I, however, think that what is rather signified is the middle time between the first coming of the Lord and the second, which will be the day of judgement. For this reason too a great dread came upon such a man at sunset, because, as the world was now setting, a future sacrifice was being declared, by which the world would be redeemed, and faith would be the victim, which would not be divided from the sons of Abraham — for those who divide it are not sons of Abraham. Faith is compared to the kingdom of heaven; for like is the kingdom of heaven to a grain of mustard seed, and the kingdom of heaven is like a merchant, who bought a field and found in it a precious pearl. The kingdom of heaven is undivided, because the kingdom of the Trinity is one. It is therefore perpetual and eternal, because it is undivided; for every kingdom divided will easily be destroyed — chastity dedicated to the Trinity would also be a victim, after that sacrifice: of the heifer, for labours, because it is a priestly victim; of the she-goat, because it is a victim for sins; of the ram, because it is for the whole world and for the very beings of the air or of heaven, not only for men nor only for lambs, but also for kids, which it strips of the stench of a sinful generation; the oracle testifies that the sons of Abraham would still be in the land of affliction, so that in this contest of many and grievous struggles they might prove themselves, and so go out with much furniture of gold and silver vessels, with rich spoils, and that their souls might return and go out possessing precious bodies of various virtues, filled above all with the treasure of purity from the passions, and about to take, at the judgement of Christ, vengeance on the devil and his ministers and the others who wished to harm them. Then there will be a great dread even for the just; for no one is without sin. All will have something to fear, because sins will have been filled up, so that when sin has superabounded, grace may superabound.

67. Again, when the sun was now at its setting, a flame came to be. And behold, a smoking furnace and lamps of fire, which passed through the midst of those divided pieces. Even if anyone doubted about what went before, what follows would confirm it, when we read that a flame came to be at sunset, to light up the evening times of the world, to shine in the darkness, to reveal what was hidden. Then straightway a smoking furnace was seen, by whose likeness human life seems to be expressed, entangled and wrapped up in the iniquities of this world, not having the brightness of true radiance and the splendour of pure fire. Within, like a furnace, it seethes with various desires and pants with certain fires of longings; without, it is covered over, as it were, with a kind of smoke, so that it may not see the face of truth. And so the eyes of the soul are overshadowed and covered with a kind of mist, so that the sight of the mind is confounded by a sort of ball of smoking cloud, so that it cannot behold pure things, until the Lord Jesus directs the heavenly lamps, that is, the radiance of his glory, when they shall not need he says the light of a lamp and the light of the sun, because the Lord himself will be light to all, and he himself will illuminate all the things of this world, whose division was learned above and is now made manifest in the light, so that not through a mirror in an enigma, nor in part, but face to face the solidity of truth and that which is perfect may be seen.

Chapter X.

68. After this there followed the oracle of God, saying: to your seed I will give this land from the river of Egypt as far as the great river Euphrates. He who had shown the glory to come had also to promise the merits of the virtues that were to be conferred; for he himself is both the helper of those who labour and the rewarder of the innocent. Egypt here is the name not of a region but of a river. For so the ancients called the Nile, whether because it had itself given its name to the region or had received it from the region's name. Thus the Greek poet too testifies that it is so, saying: στῆσα δʼ ἐν Αἰγύπτῳ ποταμῷ νέας ἀμφιελίσσας ('I stationed the curved ships in the river Aegyptus'). But it is narrow and mean to think that he pledged earthly things by heavenly signs. And so let us consider whether he did not perhaps promise perfect blessedness and the consummation of good merits. For perfect blessedness seems to consist of these three, the goods of the body and of the soul and the accidental goods, which the Greeks called ἐκτός (external), so that of the body there should be purity, patience or temperance, of the soul prudence and justice. The river Egypt, then, seems to signify bodily things, whence that river itself was also called Gihon, because man was fashioned from the earth; but the Euphrates signifies the things that are of the soul, in that justice is the fount of the other virtues, the one that illuminates the other virtues. For prudence without justice does harm; fortitude too, unless justice tempers it, is an intolerable insolence, nearer to fury than to reason, to domination than to liberty; sobriety and temperance are private goods and of no use to anyone, unless you cultivate devotion with just reverence toward God and with a faithful mind: justice alone it is that embraces all the virtues and commends them all. Accidental goods, too, are business dealings and trades or farming, the fitting gains of labours and of rural works. There are also accidental goods of the body, soundness and the comfort of good health, comeliness, strength, which befall with time and change with age.

69. You should not think this threefold grace a slight one. For you have perfect perfection in the Gospel. For when the Lord Jesus says to that lawyer: you shall love the Lord your God, he commands that the justice of the soul be kept. For if it is just that parents be honoured, how much more ought honour to be paid to the parent of all? Again, when he says: you shall not kill, you shall not commit adultery, you shall not steal, you shall not bear false witness, he admonishes that the virtues of the body be kept. And in the later passage, when he says: there is no one who leaves house or parents or wife or children for the kingdom of God and does not receive seven times as much in this time; in the world to come he shall possess eternal life, does he not promise an increase of the accidental goods together with the reward of soul and body?

70. These things, which holy Scripture expresses in simple words, Aristotle and the Peripatetics proclaim and extol in a kind of lofty buskin, and their own people testify that it is also a doctrine of Pythagoras. But which of them equalled Abraham in time, which of them equalled God in authority and wisdom, God by whose oracle Abraham acknowledges the gift of this threefold grace?

71. And foreign nations are given to him as if for discipline, so that the mind most observant of equity may cut back vices and correct errors. Yet the mystery of the Church is declared more evidently, because through his apostles, who are Israelites, whose fathers . . . . and from which fathers Christ according to the flesh was made under the Law, the Church was to be gathered out of the peoples of the nations who were to believe. These he signified by the number ten, not idly, but to show that they, faithless before, when they had filled up the measure of impiety, would surely obtain the crown of faith.

72. Then it follows that Sarai, the wife of Abraham, had been barren. And she had an Egyptian handmaid, whose name was Hagar; and that this pertains to the Church we have shown with apostolic examples in that exposition which we wrote on the moral sense. For the Church seems barren in this world, because she brings forth neither worldly things nor present things, but things to come, that is, not those which are seen, but those which are not seen. Her handmaid is the synagogue, or every heresy, which begets slaves, not free sons. And therefore Hagar means sojourning. For she cherishes the hope of a temporal sojourning; she does not hold the grace of a perpetual possession. And so, lest her handmaid grow insolent through bodily childbirth and claim for herself the right of the Church, it is said there: cast out the handmaid and her son; for the son of the handmaid shall not be heir with my son Isaac.

73. But there is also a Sarah in each one and a Hagar in each one. Sarah is true virtue, true wisdom; but Hagar is cunning, as it were the handmaid of the more perfect virtue; for one wisdom is spiritual, another is the wisdom of this world. Therefore she is also described as an Egyptian, because philosophical learning abounded in Egypt. Thus Moses too was learned in all the wisdom of the Egyptians, but he cast it away, preferring to the treasures of Egypt reproach for the name of Christ. For if he had judged that wisdom to be of any moment, he would certainly not have said: I pray, Lord, I am not worthy, before yesterday and the day before, nor since you began to speak to your servant; for I am of slender neck and slower tongue. Thus, as to one unlearned, he is answered that he would be taught by the Lord: I will open your mouth and instruct you in what you ought to speak.

74. And it is a splendid mystery that the Law was not full enough to persuade the peoples and to call the nations, or because it was to be closed until the coming of Christ, who, expounding to us the prophetic oracles and bringing forth the testimonies of the old reading, opened, as it were, a kind of mouth of the Law, so that the cry of faith might reach the whole world. Hence Sarai too says mystically: the Lord has shut me up, so that I do not bear. Go in, then, to my handmaid, and you shall have a son from her, so that you may recognize that the Church of God was always in predestination, and the fruitfulness of faith ready to burst forth when the Lord should command, but reserved by the will of the Lord for a fixed time. Thus it is written: in an acceptable time I have heard you, and in the day of salvation I have helped you. We observe, then, that the faith of the Church was hastening, but her fruitfulness was shut up. By this word it is shown that she was awaiting the times of her bearing, because what is shut up is wont to be opened. For what reason it was shut up the Apostle teaches you, saying: for God has shut up all things in unbelief, that he may have mercy on all, so that the grace should be not of him who wills nor of him who runs, but of God who has mercy, lest you should justify yourself, but that you should ascribe all things to God, who called you. Let no one, then, grow sluggish and put forward an excuse for his sloth, so as to believe the later, because it is written that it is not of him who wills, nor of him who runs; for let him consider what has been added: but of him who has mercy he says, of God. Show yourself such, then, by good pursuits and ready faith, that God may have mercy on you and call you, as he called the Church, saying: I was made manifest to those who did not seek me, I appeared to those who did not ask about me.

75. And rightly were the lower things set first, so that the better might follow. The handmaid bore slaves, so that the Church might make them free and call the peoples from slavery to liberty, from guilt to innocence, from offence to grace. If you look also at the order of individual men, not all have begun from perfect things, nor is perfect virtue first in time with all, though it is older in merit. It belongs, then, to a prudent mind to consider how long the soul is imperfect. Let it weigh with itself what it has, so that it may exercise itself at least in the lower disciplines of the virtues, until it grows strong by the practice of exercise; but when it has stripped itself of the wrappings of error and untied itself from every offence, showing its purification completed, then let it strive in its own order to bring forth great offspring.

76. Moreover it is said to Abraham: be without reproach, to him to whom was given the holy spirit of wisdom, well-moving, spotless. The soul of the wise man, then, ought day and night to keep its watch set forth in unbroken exercise, never indulging in sleep, intent on God in perpetual vigils, for the comprehension of the things that are and the knowledge of their several causes. But wisdom is also the interpreter of things to come; she knows things past and judges of things to come, she knows the subtleties of words and the solutions of arguments, she knows signs and wonders before they come to pass, and the outcomes of times and of ages. He, then, who has acquired her cannot but be good and perfect, because he both has every virtue and is an image of goodness. Hence the sophists of this world also drew from it a definition of the wise man of this kind, that the wise man is a good man, skilled in speaking.

77. Let us return now to the gift of God, than which nothing is fuller. For what is better than wisdom, what worse than vanity, what more baneful than superstition? Therefore, as to one to whom he had promised the fullness of perfection, he says: I will increase you greatly and set you among the nations, and kings shall come from you, because to the one who is faithful the whole world is of riches, and he is increased, not diminished like the fool. Abraham is set among the nations, that is, his faith is transferred to the nations and to the kings of the world, who have believed and subject themselves to the Lord Jesus, to whom it is said: kings shall offer you gifts. Nor is this absurd, because from the race of Abraham there will be not only kings in dignity, but also those kings who are not slaves to sin; nor does malice conquer those over whom death has no reign. We have learned too that the findings of the good mind are royal and princely, a mind which, like Abraham, has no yield of middling offspring, but abounds in royal ones. To it all the earth has been given for a possession, so that it may have dominion over the body and not be captive to the pleasures of the flesh, but that the flesh, as though bound by due service, may serve the mind. But according to the person of Abraham the mystery of the Church is evident, the Church which has possessed the whole world by the inheritance of faith; he is rightly called the chosen father of sound, the father of faith, the father of devout confession.

Chapter XI.

78. And because he is called to the perfect, he receives the oracle of perfection. You shall circumcise he says every male of yours, and you shall circumcise your flesh; but perfect circumcision is spiritual. Indeed the reading also teaches this when it says: Circumcise the hardness of your heart. And here many take it thus, so that it means: Circumcise every male of yours, that is, your mind; for nothing is stronger than the mind. Next, because the male is also called holy: Every male that opens the womb shall be called holy to the Lord. And what is holier than the mind, which gives the seeds of good thoughts, by which it opens the womb of the soul shut up by the barrenness of not bearing, so that it may be able to bring forth those invisible generations, namely in that spiritual womb of which Isaiah says: In the womb we have conceived, and we have been in labour with the spirit of salvation? Circumcision of the heart, then, which is intelligible, and circumcision also of the flesh, which is sensible, is commanded: the one in truth, the other in a sign. The circumcision is therefore twofold, because abstinence is required both of the spirit and of the body. Indeed the Egyptians circumcise males in the fourteenth year, and the women among them are said to be circumcised in the same year, because, namely, from that year the passion of the manly impulse begins to burn and the menses of women take their beginning. But the giver of the eternal Law requires the sign of carnal circumcision in males alone, because in the use of intercourse the man is more vehement than the woman, and therefore he wished to break his impulse by the sign of circumcision; or because men believe that they go astray lawfully if they abstain only from adultery, and think that the use of harlots is allowed as if by the law of nature, whereas apart from marriage it is lawful neither for a man nor for a woman to be joined with another. But by a higher interpretation this is laid open: that if the mind is purged and circumcised, stripped of superfluous pleasures and thoughts, it restrains the soul to its own purity and, when the soul has been steeped in pure senses, makes it the bearer of good offspring.

79. Now the Law orders that the boy be circumcised on the eighth day, certainly by a mystical precept, because that is the day of the resurrection; for on the Lord's day the Lord Jesus rose again. So if the day of resurrection finds us circumcised and stripped of the superfluities of sins, washed from all filth, clean of bodily vices: if you go forth from here clean, you will rise again clean. Circumcise yourself, then, not in the flesh but from carnal vice, and circumcise not only your home-born servant but also the one bought with a price. If you refer it to particulars, the home-born servants are the natural motions; those bought with a price are those acquired by reason and teaching. Both these and those, like young shrubs, need cleansing and the cutting back of their luxuriance, so that they may not straggle like barren shoots and the useless overshadow the fruitful; or, as a vine overloaded with every fault labours in vain, so we must beware lest our mind too, taken up with many things, not only fail to bring forth good offspring but also degenerate into a great many useless ones; and at the same time that, like a pruned vine, it may not easily run wild nor quickly fall apart, but be preserved for posterity. For the gifted, too, bring forth many things which it is useful to circumcise, and those who have attained learning by diligence ought to see their ignorance in themselves. But the meaning of the mystery is clear. For the home-born servants are the Jews; those bought with a price are the Gentiles who have believed, because the Church was redeemed at the price of the blood of Christ. So both Jew and Greek, whoever has believed, ought to know how to circumcise himself from sins, so that he may be saved. Let both the member of the household and the foreigner, both the just man and the sinner, be circumcised by the remission of sins, so that sin may work no longer, because no one ascends into the kingdom of heaven except through the sacrament of baptism. Nor will the justice of an earlier time profit, if at the end of life one has abandoned justice. Therefore Paul says: You were bought with a price; do not become servants of men. For these are contraries, because servitude is contracted through sin, and sin is remitted through a price.

80. We think, then, that these things are abundantly enough for understanding by a simple exposition. And therefore we do not discuss with empty care the cubes of geometry, nor the square number of philosophy, nor the Pythagorean confession, as they say, nor the numbers of the hebdomad, which they call ever-virgin; nor do we shape the world with the geometer's rod, nor seek heaven in the dust, nor enclose the globe within narrow abacuses; but we open the true mysteries, that the one salvation is the resurrection of Christ. Let us then be planted together in the likeness of his death, so that we may merit fellowship in the resurrection. And let our old man be nailed together to the cross, so that the body of sin may be destroyed.

81. Excellently, moreover, the Law orders that males be circumcised in the first wailings of infancy, even the home-born servants, because just as sin is from infancy, so circumcision is from infancy. No time ought to be empty of protection, because none is empty of fault. Even the infant must be called back from sin, lest he be polluted by the contagion of idolatry, and lest he grow used to adoring an idol and kissing an image, violating the parent's nest, wounding devotion. At the same time, lest anyone be puffed up because he seems to himself just, Abraham is ordered to be circumcised in the course of a riper age. So neither the old proselyte nor the infant home-born servant is excepted, because every age is liable to sin, and therefore every age is fit for the sacrament.

82. And my covenant he says shall be in your flesh. Perhaps it may be retorted at this point: 'How do you call circumcision spiritual, when the oracle says: the covenant of circumcision shall be in your flesh?' As though temperance were required of the soul alone and not of the passions of the body. For a certain purity is required of sight and of hearing and of smell and of taste and of touch and of the voice itself, because a too forward gaze also has guilt, and therefore it is written: Do not give heed to a deceitful woman, nor be taken by the eyes, nor be carried off by the eyelids; and in hearing itself there is guilt, if a harlot seduces you and binds you with much flattery of speech and with the snares of her lips. And in touch itself there is guilt, and therefore it is said to you: Do not be much with a strange woman, nor be held fast in the embraces of one not your own; and in the voice there is fault; for his own lips are a very strong snare to a man, and he is led away by the lips of his own mouth. And do not eat much even of honey, lest you vomit. It is necessary, therefore, that the moderation of all the senses be keen, lest either impulse drag us into vice, or excess harm us, or delay become an offence.

83. Not idly, moreover, nor superfluously do many seem to be troubled at the passage that follows, because the Lord spoke thus: Since the male who has not been circumcised and has not circumcised the flesh of his foreskin on the eighth day, that soul shall perish from its kind, because he has broken my covenant. For it is thought harsh that the negligence of the parents should turn to the harm of an infant of eight days, so that his soul should perish, when the Law itself has appointed cities even for the homicide who has nevertheless committed the deed of killing a man not voluntarily, so that by fleeing to them he may merit impunity for the blood. How then is account taken there of an accidental killing, and here no account taken of infancy, in which there could be no crime either of neglect or of will, unless perhaps some think that the parents are punished more heavily in the death of a son? But it is thought unjust that for the fault of the guilty a penalty should be inflicted on the innocent, or that the innocent should be punished on account of the penalty of the guilty, or that one unlike in desert should be made a partner in the punishment. Hence some think that he is speaking of the parent being cut off, that it is his soul that perishes, not the little one's. But this is very doubtful, although what he says supports this assertion: because he has broken my covenant. Therefore this seems rather to be referred to one who understands than to an infant. Others think that the Lord God is threatening heavier things to the parents even by his silence, so that the elders may fear the more when not even the infant is spared.

84. But to me it is clear enough that it was said of the mind of each one. For we have said that by the name of the male the mind is signified, because the vigour of the mind is strong and draws the soul into union with itself, and is more vehement, as being superior in sex and in manly strength. So this is the reason: that every mind which has not been circumcised from bodily superfluities and purged by the solemn rite, so as to strip itself of the vices of the passions, shall perish. Not the flesh, he says, shall perish, not the man, but that soul shall perish, because it could have been saved if it had had purification; but, bare of protection and made weaker by the filth of an uncircumcised heart, it could not preserve the salvation of its kind. Now every kind seems immortal. As man is the genus and the individual the species, man is always spoken of, the individual not always; or rather, the individual does not fail. He fails who does not have faith. The person of one fails; the condition or the name of men does not fail. So from what is lasting and harmless the sinner is led down into what is temporal and harmful, and he ought to ascribe to the infancy of his mind that he was incautious and intemperate, or did not acquire the remission of sins. Unless one has been reborn of water and the Holy Spirit, he cannot enter into the kingdom of God. Certainly he excepted no one, not the infant, not one prevented by some necessity: though they may have that hidden immunity from punishments, I do not know whether they have the honour of the kingdom.

85. To Sarai (Sara) also one letter is added, that is R, so that she was called Sarah (Sarra). And surely it is right that this, as in the earlier case, be weighed not by the addition of one letter. For God's gift is not one letter, but the virtue of the letter, which expresses the grace of the divine gift. For Sara means ἀρχὴ ἐμή, that is, my power or my sovereignty . . . . beginning or of a king . . . ., but Sarra is called in Greek ἄρχουσα, in Latin 'she who rules.' The one is mortal, the other immortal; the one specific, the other general. For there is in me prudence, in me purity, in me virtue, in me justice; they rule me alone and are lords over me, and they are mortal; for when I die they are dissolved, and they too die. But the prudence which is spoken of generally, the purity, the fortitude and the other principal virtues, principal however in a general way, and certain immortal queens: in these is the power, that immortal sovereign principle, as the Church is queen, who rules not me alone but all. So we see the species turned into the genus, the part into the whole, corruptibility into incorruptibility, all of which it is certain befit the Church; for the reason here is not specific but general, nor is it the salvation of a part but of the whole. And therefore, with these going before, when his prudence has led each one to this principal salvation, spread through all, in which is the fount of wisdom and justice, there is sought a generation and that offspring of perfect joy whose name is Isaac; for no pleasure is better than the grace of an amended conscience. From this the Epicureans drew their view that pleasure is the highest good, but they reckoned it by the defilement of the body rather than by the sobriety of the mind.

86. But what does it mean that he says: Abraham fell on his face and laughed? Here too reverence is signified, because he feared to offend God as if by a free laugh, although the laugh declared the joy of a just man who was rejoicing at such great promises. For this was not the laughter of one who doubted but of one who believed. At the same time, because all things fall before God and are changed and pass away, that substance alone stands ever unchangeable. Or perhaps in this mystery also Abraham prophesied the Lord Jesus, that through the taking up of the Lord's body and through the resurrection the grace of so great an oracle was to be fulfilled. He worships, then, not the element of earth, of which it was said: And worship his footstool, for it is holy. For where the body is, there also are the eagles, which worshipped him who dwelt in the body.

87. And he said he says in his heart: If a child shall be born to one of a hundred years, and if Sarah at ninety years shall bear. The Greek has set down, in a middle sense, τῇ διανοίᾳ (in the mind), so that we may judge that he spoke to his heart as to one wavering within itself: 'If a child shall be born to a man of a hundred and a woman of ninety shall bear,' that is: the age for begetting has passed; but to God all things are possible, and therefore this too is easy, to call back the years of youth for the old, to pour back their strength, to give fruitfulness to the barren.

88. Nor must this be passed over, that when a lawful generation had been promised him, Abraham answered God: Let this Ishmael live in your sight. It belongs to the just man to intercede even for sinners, and therefore let the Jews believe at least this, that he intercedes for them too, if only they believe. For this is to live in the sight of God: to carry on affairs worthy of the word of God; for the eyes of the Lord are upon the just.

89. Hence the Lord also says: Yes. Behold, Sarah your wife shall bear you a son. And concerning Ishmael I have heard you. When he says Yes, he confirms the promises; for it is a word of confirmation. And therefore he first confirms the future generation of the Church, so that the prophet might know to be true what God said he had heard concerning Ishmael, foreseeing that blindness was to befall Israel in part, until the fullness of the Gentiles should come in, and so all Israel should be saved. So, just as the testaments of men first put forward the heir and afterwards set out the legacy, the inheritance for the better, legacies for the inferior, so in the testament of the Lord, from which we too have received this custom, the heir is written down, good by nature, noble, begotten in lawful wedlock; the inferior is presented with a legacy.

90. Now the generation is promised for the following year, so that you may notice which generation the Lord promises, that is, not that of Sarah's bodily womb, but this offspring of the Church which was to come. Indeed further on he also says: I will return to you in time to come, and Sarah shall have a son. In this we can take both: both this assembly of the Church and the resurrection of the faithful.

91. That Ishmael also is circumcised in his thirteenth year has an evident reason: because he who is about to begin to use the knowledge of a woman ought first to cut back in himself the heat of lust, so that he may abstain from superfluous unions and reserve himself only for lawful union.

92. It befits the mind of the wise man also to be hospitable, so that it may impart its grace to others too, and share the fruit of its prudence with others as well, and so feast on the good foods of teaching and set out its banquet for those who desire it,

93. then, besides, that it know nothing but to live according to nature, in whose institution and order is the Law of God; that it know how to mingle itself with no perverse desire, prefer union with wisdom alone, and not know how to put the glory of this world and a certain inheritance of present praise before the commandments of God; and that, as it sacrifices its own interests on the altars of the Lord, so it neither receive nor dread the fire of judgment, but rather labour even to rescue others.
