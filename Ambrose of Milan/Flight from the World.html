Flight from the World

Latin source: Ambrose's treatise on flight from the world, a sermon reworked into a book at Milan (about 391-394), from the critical text of C. Schenkl, Corpus Scriptorum Ecclesiasticorum Latinorum 32/2 (Vienna, 1897), pp. 163–207, verified against the page images and collated in part with the Benedictine edition as reprinted in Migne, PL 14, with the edition's misprints corrected; in one place the text departs from Schenkl with the other manuscripts and the Benedictine edition, as his own apparatus prefers (Chapter III §16, inluminat, "enlightens", for his inluminauit). Scripture is translated as Ambrose quotes it, from his own Old Latin text; italics mark the Scripture he quotes. The chapters are Schenkl's and the section numbers are the Maurist ones shared by all editions, running through the whole work. The few Latin words Ambrose comments on are given in parentheses; Psalms keep the Latin (LXX) numbering, with the Hebrew number in parentheses. Schenkl's supplements are read into the text, the one lacuna he prints (Chapter III §16) is marked with dots, and his critical apparatus is not translated. This English translation is released into the public domain.

Chapter I.

1. Frequent is our discourse about fleeing this world, and would that our disposition were as cautious and careful as the discourse is easy! But what is worse, the enticement of earthly desires frequently creeps in, and an outpouring of vanities takes hold of the mind, so that the very thing you strive to avoid is what you think about and turn over in your mind. This is difficult for a man to guard against, and impossible to put off. Accordingly, the prophet testifies that this is a matter of prayer rather than of accomplishment, when he says: Incline my heart to your testimonies and not to avarice. For our heart and our thoughts are not in our power; poured over us unexpectedly, they confound the mind and the spirit and draw them elsewhere than you had proposed: they call us back to worldly things, put in things of this world, heap on pleasures, weave in enticing things; and at the very time when we are preparing to lift up the mind, we are for the most part cast down to earthly things by the vain thoughts that have been put in.

2. But who is so blessed as always to ascend in his heart? And how can this be done without divine help? In no way at all. Accordingly, the same Scripture says above: Blessed is the man whose help is from you, O Lord: ascents in his heart. Blessed indeed is he whom delight does not call back, whom pleasure does not bend, who does not look back to the things below, which not even Lot's wife herself could escape. Warned by this example, the Apostle, forgetting the things that are behind and reaching out to those that are before, hastened to the prize and deserved to reach it, because he saw before him Christ, by whom he was being called to the crown of justice. But he reached it who denied himself to himself, that he might gain Christ. Accordingly, it was not he himself who lived, but Christ lived in him.

3. For who, amid so many passions of this body, amid so many enticements of this world, can keep his footstep safe and undefiled? The eye has looked and turned aside the sense of the mind; the ear has heard and bent its attention; a scent has breathed upon it and hindered thought; the mouth has tasted and brought back crime; touch has made contact and kindled a fire. Death has come in through the window, said the prophet. Your window is your eye. If you look on a woman to lust after her, death has come in; if you hear the words of harlots, death has come in; if luxury takes your senses captive, death has penetrated. And therefore let him who wishes to ascend follow not the glad things of the world, not the pleasant, not the delightful, but things full of grief and weeping; for it is better to go into the house of mourning than into the house of joy. Accordingly, not even Adam would have come down from paradise had he not been deceived by delight.

4. Beautifully, then, does David, who had learned by experience that even a man's very glances are perilous, call him blessed whose whole hope is in the name of God. For thus he does not look upon vanities and madnesses, if he always strains toward Christ, always beholds Christ with the inner eyes. Hence, turning again to him, he says: Turn away my eyes, that they may not see vanity. The circus is vanity, because it profits nothing; vain is the swiftness of horses, because it is a liar as to safety; the theatre is vanity, every game is vanity. All things, he says, are vanity, said Ecclesiastes, all that are in this world. Accordingly, let him who wishes to be saved ascend above the world, seek the Word with God, flee this world, leave the earth; for he cannot perceive that which is and always is unless he has first fled from here. Hence the Lord too, wishing to draw near to God the Father, said to the apostles: Arise, let us go from here.

Chapter II.

5. The Law also teaches you that the world must be fled and God followed. For what else does it teach when it says: You shall appoint cities for yourselves as a refuge, and they shall be refuges for you, to which the manslayer may flee for refuge, everyone who has struck down a soul unwillingly; and below: Six cities shall be refuges for you. Three cities you shall give beyond the Jordan, and three cities you shall give in the land of Canaan? Openly, indeed, refuges have been set forth for those who take refuge because of this crime of manslaughter; but let us consider it more deeply. There are four things which warn us that the secrets of this reading must be looked into more profoundly. The first: for what reason, out of those cities which fell by lot to the Levites as their portion, six cities were given as a refuge to those labouring under the crime of manslaughter, and why the cities of the other tribes were not also assigned to this office. The second: for what reason six in number; for this number, of neither more nor fewer cities, does not seem to have been prescribed idly. The third: why three cities were appointed beyond the Jordan and three in the region of Canaan, to be a refuge for those who sin. The fourth: why a reckoning and definition of time was laid down, within which the manslayer should dwell in the city of refuge, until the high priest dies, and after the death of the high priest the manslayer should return to the city of his dwelling. We must, then, speak of each, and resolve the questions in the order in which we have set them forth.

6. Fittingly, therefore, we must first explain why the cities of the Levites were given as a refuge. And it is clear that this was suitably provided, because the Levites are fugitives from this world, so that they may please God; they leave fatherland, parents, children and all kindred, so that they may cleave to the one God. Indeed, to Abraham too it was said: Go out from your land and from your kindred and from your father's house. But perhaps you say: He was not a Levite. But he had Levi in his loins, as we read written in the Letter to the Hebrews. And the Lord speaks to Levites when he speaks to his disciples, that is, to the apostles: If anyone wishes to come after me, let him deny himself and take up his cross and follow me, although it has now been said to all: But you are a chosen race, a royal priesthood, a holy nation, a people for adoption. The fullness has come; the remnants have ceased. For Christ has called all; the way forward lies open to all, so that all may follow; to all the kingdom is set before them, and eternal life.

7. Therefore he whose portion is God ought to care for nothing but God, lest he be hindered by the duty of some other necessity. For whatever is given to other duties is plucked away from the worship of religion and from this office of ours. For this is the true flight of the priest: the renunciation of his household and a kind of estrangement from those dearest to him, so that he who longs to serve God denies himself to his own. Rightly, then, did the sanction of the eternal Law commend fugitives to fugitives, so that those who have forgotten this world should receive those who, condemning their sins and their works, seek forgetfulness of their former life and desire to wipe out the worldly things they have done. The minister of his holy altar, therefore, is a fugitive from his own. Hence the Lord, as the prince of priests, giving a pattern to the Levites in his Gospel, said: Who is my mother, or who are my brothers? That is: I do not acknowledge a mother, I do not recognize brothers, I do not know my kin. My mother and my brothers are these who hear the word of God and do it. The minister, therefore, knows only the word of God, since he knows those in whom the word of God is at work. And therefore he is an exile from the world: a fugitive from the body, a fugitive from the passions, he renounces all things, so that he may remain alone, as Elijah said: And I am left alone. But he was not alone, with whom Christ was. And the Lord himself was left alone, but I am not he says alone, because the Father is with me.

8. There is also this reason, that the Levites are ministers of God. And therefore their cities are assigned by the Law to manslayers in flight, because it is their right to carry out the divine commands concerning those who have committed a mortal crime. The Church does not know how to publish laws of her own: she knows that the priest should obey the Lord's command. Hear a Levite saying: I have delivered such a one to Satan for the destruction of the flesh, so that the spirit may be saved in the day of our Lord Jesus Christ. Struck, then, by the Levitical sword, let the sense of the flesh die in us, so that our soul may live. For unless the sense of the flesh has been slain, there can be no fruit of eternal life.

9. Now the refuges of the six cities are so ordered that the first city is the knowledge of the Word and, after his image, a pattern of living. For whoever has entered into that knowledge is safe from punishment, according to what the Lord also says: Now you are clean because of the word which I have spoken to you. And elsewhere: Now this is eternal life, that they may know you, the only true God, and Jesus Christ whom you have sent. This city, then, is as it were the metropolis, next to which lie the other five cities of the Levites. The second city is the consideration of the divine operation by which the world was created. The third city is the contemplation of the kingly power and of the eternal majesty. The fourth city is the beholding of the divine propitiation. The fifth city is the contemplation of the divine Law, which commands what is to be done. The sixth city, too, is the portion of the Law which prescribes what is not to be done. How great the abundance of the divine mercy, how great the riches of his loving-kindness, that, considering the pursuits of each one and the frailties of the human condition, by which we are led into fault even unwilling and reluctant, and, overcome by enticements, frequently commit offences that are not voluntary, he should set before us various refuges, namely of six cities, so that by the same number by which the world was formed a remedy might be provided against worldly vices and the shipwrecks of this world!

10. The first remedy, then, is this: if a fault has crept upon any man of good purpose, he should, if he wishes to have perfect security, hasten without any delay to the very citadel of all things, where the Word of God is, in that bosom of the Father, that is, in the secret and inner recess of God, where the fountain of wisdom is, from which, instead of death, he may draw the immortal drink of eternal life. The second remedy is this: he who cannot have a foretaste of the knowledge of that good, and is slower either in natural talent or in the comprehension of faith, since it is by swift vigour of mind and sharpness of talent that one strives toward the heights of knowledge, should at least consider the works of the Lord and, from the things that have been made, contemplate the author of so great a work; since from these good things which are in the establishment of this creation — for they are very good, as the Lord said — that supreme and eternal good is comprehended. What order of things, what discipline, what grace? Is not even a slow talent provoked by these to love its author? For if we love our parents because they begot us, how much more ought we to love the creator of our parents and our own author! Therefore the operative power of God: even if it is not seen, it is nevertheless judged from its works, and his works betray the worker, so that he who is not comprehended is understood. Hence the Lord also says: If you do not believe me, at least believe the works. Good, therefore, as a refuge is this city too, which testifies to the grace of its builder and stirs up our affection, so that we may be the more desirous of him who is seen to have bestowed on us such great beauty in this fabric. The third order is the contemplation of the kingly power, so that we may be subject to the King, if we do not defer to him as to a parent. For through fear of one who presides, he who is ungrateful for salvation often becomes obedient to power, so that he who would not and could not acknowledge the grace of devotion may acknowledge the necessity of sobriety. Necessity, therefore, corrects the one whom devotion ought to have called forth.

11. These are the three cities beyond the Jordan, given as a refuge to the more perfect prudence, so that we may first flee fault by the bent of our mind, conformed to the image of God. For so we were created, God saying: Let us make man after our image and likeness. This, then, is the law of that first city. Next, if through the frailty of the flesh and the enticements of the world we cannot so form our mind, let us lighten sin by reverence for the Father who begot us and by the dutifulness of offspring; for love, indeed, covers a multitude of sins. Whoever, then, could not be after the image of God, let him be unto the fullness of love. That city shuts out fault; this one looses it. This, indeed, is the law of the second city: You shall love the Lord your God from your whole heart and from your whole soul and from your whole strength. But if, again, there are some of narrow and petty spirit who cannot receive the abundance and the grace of love, you have the third city, so that fear of the divine power may make you sober and dread may bend you. And this is the third law: You shall worship the Lord your God, and him alone shall you serve. These, then, are the chief virtues, and therefore of the few, not of the many, placed beyond the Jordan, so that you may express the archetype as an image does, love him as a son loves his father, worship him as a subject worships his king.

12. But those who have crossed the Jordan — now the Jordan is called their descent —, those, then, who have gone down from those higher virtues to these lower things, that is, those in whom integrity, love and humility have wavered, have refuges nearer to man which they may seek, so that those who are liable to offences and bent by vices that are not voluntary may hope that the Lord can be reconciled to them if they ask forgiveness, may hope that they can be corrected if they follow the precepts of the heavenly testaments, by which we are either formed to innocence or called back from fault. These, then, are the following cities on this side of the Jordan: that we may propitiate God to ourselves, follow what he commands, and avoid what he forbids. Let there be, then, an eagerness to propitiate the Godhead, obedience in following his precepts, caution against forbidden transgression, by which we may revere the propitiatory mercy of God and his nomothetic (law-giving) providence, either by compliance with what he has ordained or by turning away from what he has forbidden.

13. There remains the fourth point, what it says about the death of the high priest: for until that time that manslayer shall be in the city of refuge, until the high priest dies, in which the interpretation, according to the letter, sticks fast. First, the flight itself is determined by chance rather than by the equity of any judgment; then, in equal cases the outcome is unequal; for it could happen that the high priest should die on the day after that manslayer had taken refuge. And what sort of sentence rests on uncertainty? Therefore, since the letter sticks fast, let us seek the spiritual sense. Who is this high priest but the Son of God, the Word of God, whose advocacy on our behalf we have with the Father, who has no part in any offences, voluntary or accidental, in whom all things that are on earth and that are in heaven hold together? For all things are bound together by the bond of the Word and are held by his power and consist in him, since in him they were created and in him dwells all fullness. And therefore all things abide, because he does not allow to be dissolved the things which he himself has bound together, since they consist in his will; for he restrains all things by his command as far as he wills, and rules them, and binds them with a natural concord. The Word of God, then, lives, and lives above all in the souls of the devout, and the fullness of the Godhead never dies. For the everlasting Godhead and the eternal power of God never die. Yet he dies to us if he is separated from our soul, not that he is corrupted by death, but that our mind is loosed and stripped from union with him; for true death is the separation of the Word and the soul. And then at once the soul begins to lie open to voluntary sins.

Chapter III.

14. We have these kinds of virtues, not sketched in outline but clearly expressed, in the Apostle, when he says: So, as far as is in me, I am ready to preach the Gospel to you also who are in Rome. For I am not ashamed of the Gospel; for it is the power of God unto salvation for everyone who believes, to the Jew first and to the Greek. For the justice of God is revealed in him, that is, in him who has believed. Then he added: from faith to faith, as it is written; but the just man lives by faith. In whom, then, is the justice of God revealed, if not in him who is conformed to the image of the Son of God? You have the first precept: after the image of God. The second: that the invisible things of God are understood through the things that have been made, that is, his everlasting power and Godhead are known by his works. This is the operative power. The third concerns the imperial power, because the Word of God is kingly and judicial and full of priestly justice, reserving the reward of good deeds and the requital of evil ones for those who will come to his judgment, of whom the Apostle says: But we know that the judgment of God is according to truth against those who do such things. And so he who has known the truth and justice of God ought not to do the things that are worthy of death. There follows the goodness of God, propitiatory indeed, of which he says: Or do you despise the riches of his goodness and patience and long-suffering, not knowing that the goodness of God leads you to repentance? That is: it ought to lead you. It calls you forth, because God is good, so that you may hope for the pardon of your sins; for he who is ready to forgive does not wish to punish. He has also added legislation, that is, the nomothetic power, so that, if anyone is relaxed by the contemplation of the divine goodness and is provoked more to negligence than to repentance, he may follow the law, since whoever he says have sinned without the law shall also perish without the law, and whoever have sinned in the law shall be judged by the law.

15. Now the law is twofold, natural and written: natural in the heart, written on tablets. All, therefore, are under law, but under the natural law; but it does not belong to all that each one should be a law to himself. He is a law to himself who does of his own accord the things that are of the law and shows the work of the law written in his heart. You have what the good things of the law are, which, however, we ought not only to know or to hear perfunctorily, but also to do; for not the hearers of the law are just before God, but the doers of the law shall be justified; you have learned also what the evil things are. First, nature itself is the teacher of good work. You know that one must not steal, and if your servant has committed a theft against you, you beat him; and if anyone has made advances to your wife, you think he must be prosecuted. What, then, you blame in others, you commit yourself: you who preach that one must not steal, steal; you who say that one must not commit adultery, commit adultery. There followed also the Law which was given through Moses, and through the Law the knowledge of sin. You have received also what you should avoid, and you do the things which you know are forbidden. And what does the Law work, except that the whole world should become subject to God? For it was enacted not only for the Hebrew, but it also called the stranger, since it did not shut out the proselyte. But since the Law was able to stop the mouths of all and was not able to convert the mind, therefore the last remedy of that final city was owed, in which there should be a saving refuge, so that the death of the prince of priests might free us from all dread of death and strip us of fear.

16. Who is he but the one of whom it has been read: Behold the Lamb of God, behold him who takes away the sin of the world? Whom God has set forth as a propitiator through faith in his blood, for the showing of his justice. And understand that he himself is the great prince of priests. The Father swore concerning him, saying: You are a priest for ever. Rightly for ever, because all the others, being temporal, are under sin, but this one has an inviolable priesthood. All are under death, but this one is ever living, since how could he who is able to save others perish himself? For such a one, he says, befitted (decebat) us. The expression is correct, since among those who have had a choice of words and expressions something of this kind is found, when someone says: a loftier place than befitted the victors. I have not passed this by, so that we may know that the Apostle uses natural words rather than common ones or those formed according to art. Such a one, then, he says, befitted us: a prince, a priest, just, holy, innocent, undefiled, set apart from sinners and made higher than the heavens. This, then, is the Word, which, dwelling above the heavens, enlightens all things. Hence also he is read to have been anointed by nature by God the Father, because the Word is the light which enlightens every man coming into this world. This is the Word of God, in whom is the great priesthood, whose intelligible garments Moses describes in that vestment of the prince of priests, because by his power he has put on the world and, as though clothed with it, shines in all things. He put on also the kinship of the human race by taking up this body, saying to the Lord . . . . and ineffable love, pouring himself into all by the spirit and the fullness of his Godhead, of whose fullness we have all received, so that we may know the love of Christ that surpasses knowledge and be filled unto all the fullness of God. For Christ is the head of all, from whom the whole body is brought forth and joined together by mutual connection, receiving its increase in the building up of itself in love. This, then, is the Word, which surpasses all, which says to Moses at the building of that ark of the testimony: And you shall put into the ark the testimonies, whatever I shall give you, and you shall make the placing of the mercy seat; and below: You shall place the mercy seat upon the ark, above, and you shall put in the testimonies which I shall give, and I will make myself known to you from there and will speak to you from above, that is: from there where he is above the heavens, where he is with the Father, from there I will speak to you.

Chapter IV.

17. Let us, therefore, upheld by the support of faith and lifted up by its oars, approach that seat of grace, fleeing this world and its contagion. And this is to flee: to abstain from sins, to take on the form of the virtues after the likeness and image of God, to stretch out our strength to the imitation of God according to the measure of our ability. For the perfect man is the image and glory of God. Hence the Lord also says: Be perfect, as your Father also, who is in heaven, is perfect. This, then, is to be like God: to have justice, to have wisdom, and to be perfect in virtue. For God is without sin, and therefore he who flees sin is after the image of God. There is no doubt, then, that he who abstains from sin flees. Hence the Apostle also cries out: Flee fornication. For the enticements of sins pursue us, lust pursues us: but you, flee it as a raging mistress who, if she has seized you, allows you to rest neither by day nor by night, but harasses, burns and inflames you. Flee avarice, lest it seize you within. Flee faithlessness, lest it entangle you in its nets. Hence the Lord also says: But when they pursue you in this city, flee into another. Amen I say to you, you will not finish the cities of Israel until the Son of Man comes. For although he seems to urge flight upon us because of the weakness of the flesh, yet he flees better who flees worldly enticement, so that he is not held by anxiety for his riches, nor by the contemplation of his treasure, nor by desire for this life, but with the intent of his mind set straight hastens to the glory of the heavenly kingdom, hurries to the crown, and is not called back from the passion of his own body by gazing on earthly things.

18. Flight, therefore, is a death, either enacted or foreshadowed. And perhaps he expresses those cities of lawful refuge, so that we may flee to the summits of the virtues, which elsewhere he dispenses as rewards to the good money-lender, saying: Because you have been faithful in a little, you shall have power over ten cities. The Law knows six cities; but because he who could say: I have not come to destroy the law, but to fulfil it, has fulfilled the Law, he grants a more perfect number of reward.

19. Let us not, then, be ashamed to flee; for this is a glorious flight, to flee from the face of sin. So Jacob fled at his mother's urging; for Rebekah said: Arise and flee into Mesopotamia. So Moses too fled from the face of King Pharaoh, lest the royal court should defile him, lest power should ensnare him. Indeed, he esteemed the reproach of Christ more precious than the riches of Egypt. So David too fled from the face of King Saul, from the face of Absalom. Indeed, in fleeing he added increase to his devotion, for he both spared the one who lay in wait for him and asked for the safety of the parricide. So the people of the Hebrews fled, so that their faith and life opened a way for them amid the waves. That flight was a path of innocence, a way of virtue, a taking up of devotion. I dare to say that so Jonah too fled into Tarshish, not by flight of the body but by ascent of the mind, for he ascended even to the likeness of Christ, so as to become a type of Christ. For as Jonah was in the belly of the whale three days and three nights, so he says will the Son of Man also be in the heart of the earth three days and three nights. For if he had not fled thus, he would never have been heard from the belly of the whale.

20. But if you doubt, let Rebekah teach you how blessed is the flight which Jacob undertook. Rebekah urged him: Flee she says into Mesopotamia. And Isaac says: Rise and go into Mesopotamia, into the house of Bathuel. In hymns or oracles Bathuel is called wisdom by most, as has been written before us, but in Latin interpretation it means "daughter of God". Into the house of wisdom, therefore, Jacob is sent, and he is admonished to take a wife from the daughters of Laban, who dwelt in Charrae, which means caves, in which there is a figure of the senses, which are as it were in the caves of the body, as sight in the eyes, hearing in the ears, smell in the nostrils, taste in the mouth. For he who delights in this world and dances in the pleasures of the body is liable to the passions of the senses and dwells and lodges in them. Hence Rebekah also says that he must dwell with him a few days, not for a long time, lest he be coloured by bodily pleasures and be captured by the enticements of the world;

21. but she persuades him to dwell there, so that, as one eager for discipline, he may learn the powers of the senses and, as it were, certain sites and regions of the flesh, so that he may know himself and know the vehemence of the flesh, what has been created and for what cause, and how each sense works. Whoever has looked he says on a woman to lust after her. The eye sees wrongly thus. Let the eye, then, see and perform its function, and not be directed to a fall at the command of a slippery mind, so that it brings back vice instead of duty. A short time, then, is given for knowing, or rather for experiencing, the senses, and perhaps the first beginnings of tender youth, and at once he is called back, lest he totter too long on slippery ground and the inner footsteps of the mind be submerged by a kind of divided flood of this body and of the world. But when he has either gained experience or has stood upon unstable, uncertain and wet ground, he is called back by his mother, patience or perseverance, who says: I will send to you and fetch you from there, so that even there, on slippery ground, you may find the safe harbour of wisdom, which will not allow you to toss as in a shipwreck, and, having returned, may know how to persevere in the worship of God, and may be in the assembly of the Gentiles, signifying that by his faith the Church of the Gentiles was to be gathered.

22. Instructed, then, by these disciplines of patience and perseverance, Jacob set out and acquired for himself a union with wisdom, the partner of his purpose, with a rich dowry of prudence, so that with her he might run through the seasons of his life without any stumbling. With the treasure of wisdom added, then, he established for himself a flock of varied sheep, but that rational flock, shining with the diversity of very many virtues. Hence also he cut back the boasting of his flesh, which the numbness of the thigh signifies, without prejudice to the interpretation of the sacred mystery. By these virtues, then, as by certain steps, his mind ascended into heaven, and he knew the secrets of God and was strengthened and filled, so that Laban, searching his house, found nothing empty with him, nothing void, no idols, no likeness of vanity. For with him there was not an image but the truth, not a likeness of sloth but the solid form of justice and the intelligible expression of living virtue. And so Laban searched his spiritual house and did not find likenesses; for it was full, not of figures but of deeds.

23. He could, however, have found the foundations and the summits of the virtues, had he not brought with him blindness of heart and the darkness of a faithless mind. Indeed, the Sodomites, their mind suffused with that blindness of depravity, could by no means find the door of holy Lot. For how could an ungodly mind see either the going out or the coming in of a holy man? The Lord himself also says in the Gospel: for the prince of this world comes, and finds nothing in me. How could he find nothing in him in whom the fullness of the Godhead dwelt, and dwelt bodily, from whom power went out and healed all? How could he find nothing in the solidity of virtue, in the abundance of wisdom, understanding and justice? You yourself said, Lord: I am full; you yourself said: Put your hand into my side, and be not unbelieving but believing. He who did not believe put in his hand and found you Lord and God. So he was not empty; rather it is that prince of this world who is blind and void, who knows how to see only his own things, knows how to find only his own things, and does not know how to recognize the things which are Christ's.

24. Or if it is as most have it: he does not find nothing in me, that is: he will not find wickedness in me, because wickedness is nothing; he will not find anything dead, because he is not dead. But how will he find anything dead in him who gives life to the dead and calls the things that are not as though they were? He will not find, he says, sin in me, I who have taken away the sin of the world. For how does he have nothing who has all things, and, what is more, has all things which the Father has, as he himself says: All things that the Father has are mine?

Chapter V.

25. Let us flee, then, from here, where there is nothing, where everything that is thought magnificent is empty, where even he who thinks himself to be something is nothing and does not exist at all. I saw, he says, the ungodly man exalted on high and lifted up beyond the cedars of Lebanon; and I passed by, and behold, he was not. You too, pass by like David; pass on like the good servant, so that it may be said to you: Pass on, recline. Pass over like Moses, so that you may see the God of Abraham, Isaac and Jacob, and see the great sight. This is the great sight. But if you wish to see, loose the shoe from your feet: loose every bond of iniquity, loose the bonds of the world, leave behind the shoe, which is earthly. For this reason Jesus sent out the apostles without shoes, gold and silver, or money, so that they might not carry earthly things with them. For he who seeks the good is praised not for his shoe but for the swiftness and comeliness of his feet, as Scripture says: How beautiful are the feet of those who bring good news of peace, who bring good news of good things! Loose, then, the shoe from your feet, so that they may be beautiful for bringing the good news. Loose, he said, not bind. Loose it, so that you may pass over and find that the ungodly man whom you used to admire on earth is nothing and can do nothing. Pass over, then, that is, flee from the earth, where wickedness is, where avarice is. For this reason David says to you: Turn aside from evil and do good. To turn aside is surely to flee; and evil is on earth, the good is in heaven. Hence he also added: Seek peace and pursue it; peace is in heaven. Indeed, coming from heaven, he said: Peace I give to you, my peace I leave with you. Since, therefore, evils are to be fled and turned aside from, and evils are on earth, iniquities on earth, let us flee earthly things, lest iniquities seize us, as they seized even holy David, as he himself testifies, saying: My iniquities have overtaken me, and I was not able to see, because the eye of the soul is blinded by the smoke of iniquity, so that it does not see the things that are full of light. So, then, Laban too could not see the good things of Jacob, nor the prince of the world the glory of Christ.

26. But perhaps you will say: Why then was Jacob sent to Laban, if Laban is blameworthy? If we consider the name, in Latin it means "white". So Jacob is bidden to go out to brighter things. But since he was carnal, we better understand the brighter things of this life. As one not yet perfect, he goes first to him with whom he might for a time exult in his light. But Laban had worse sons, that is, a name more tolerable than his works. And therefore, by his mother's counsel, by the divine oracle, and by the will of Jacob himself, a lover of discipline, he is deserted and abandoned.

27. Holy Rachel, moreover, that is, the Church or prudence, hid the images, because the Church knows nothing of empty ideas and the vain shapes of images, but knows the true substance of the Trinity. In short, she abolished the shadow and manifested the splendour of glory. Let us, then, who seek the sun, leave the shadow behind; let us who follow the light forsake the smoke. Smoke is iniquity, because as smoke is to the eyes, so is iniquity to those who practise it. Life is a shadow; for a shadow is our present life on earth, as Job said. And what is there here but temptations? All time is spent in anxiety, all life in troubles. In the midst of snares, he says, you will walk. And another complained of snares stretched for him, but hidden, in the way in which he walked. That he might not be caught and fall, he wished to flee like a sparrow, but the snare was not yet broken. Flight, he says, has perished from me. He had wings weighed down by that dark water in the clouds of the air, and perhaps he could not fly. And so he sought to receive those wings, that he might fly away and be at rest, as it is written: Who will give me wings like a dove, and I will fly and be at rest? For where there is flying, there is rest. Indeed elsewhere he says: If you sleep among the lots, the wings of a dove covered with silver.

28. But perhaps you will say: How then does he say here that flight has perished from him, who had said earlier: If I take my wings before the dawn? Is this a contradiction? Far from it. For many are the contests of the just. Does an athlete wrestle only once? How often, after many crowns, is he defeated in another contest! How often does he who has often conquered at times waver and stick fast in uncertainty! And it often happens that a strong man engages with strong men, and greater contests arise, where there are greater proofs of strength. So David, when he was seeking flight to get away from the adversary and was not finding his wings, was wavering in a doubtful contest; since, where he has his wings in his power, the title of the psalm is Unto the end, that is, to the perfection and consummation of victory. Indeed, as a victor he says: Lord, you have proved me and you have known me. You have known my sitting down and my rising again. Rightly did he have the faculty of flying who had received the wings of rising again; but this one is still in the cave, that is, in the flesh, in a kind of cavern of this body, contending with King Saul, the son of hardness, and with the power of that intelligible prince who is not seen but is understood. Hence also the title of the psalm is Understanding; yet David, though placed in the flesh, hemmed him in, but he earned the power to hem him in by praying. Indeed he began with the voice of prayer.

29. Add this too, that that psalm is said in the person of the Saviour, this one in the person of David, who did not have victory in his power but hoped for it from Christ. And yet he too, after he had spread out his hands to God like the wings of his soul, and had taken refuge in the Lord, and had asked for the guidance of the Holy Spirit, so that he might know the way by which he could ascend, saw the heavens bowed so that Christ might descend, and asked that he would lift him up with his hand. Or perhaps he no longer sought his own wings for this reason, that, having become more perfect, he longed for the hand of Christ.

30. Whoever, then, wishes to be lifted up by the hand of Christ, let him first fly away himself, let him have his own wings — he who flees the world has wings — and if he does not have his own — lest perhaps he alone should have his own who has the power of flying —, if, I say, he does not have his own, let him receive them from him who has. He, then, who flees the world flies. Behold, he says, I have gone far off in flight and remained in the wilderness. David, then, flew away like a night-raven in the dwelling, like a solitary sparrow in the house. But if you refer it to Christ, he flew away in the passion of his body, so that under the shadow of his wings he might protect the peoples of the nations. He flew away in his Godhead, he remained in body and dwelt in the desert, so that the children of the desolate woman might become more than those of her who had a husband. Let us, then, follow that body, so that we too may rise again; for where the body is, there too are the eagles.

31. Let him who cannot fly like an eagle flutter like a sparrow; let him who cannot fly to heaven fly to the mountains; let him first flee the valleys, which are quickly spoiled by moisture, and pass over to the mountains. Abraham's nephew passed over to the mountain of Zoar and was saved; but she who could not ascend, turning back through a woman's feeling, lost her salvation. Draw near to the eternal mountains, says the Lord through the prophet Micah, arise from here, for this is no refreshment for you because of uncleanness. You have been corrupted with corruption, you have suffered persecution. And the Lord says: Then let those who are in Judaea flee to the mountains, where are Mount Zion and the city of peace, Jerusalem, built not of earthly but of living stones, and ten thousands of angels, the church of the firstborn, the spirits of the perfect, the God of the just, who in his own blood has spoken better than Abel. For the one cried out for vengeance, this one for pardon; the one accused his brother's sin, this one forgave the sin of the world; the one exposed the crime, this one covered it, according to what is written: Blessed are they whose sins are covered.

Chapter VI.

32. But let him who flees flee swiftly, lest he be overtaken; let him swiftly plunder this world, as the Hebrew plundered Egypt. Let whoever is in labour give birth, lest, with the womb of the mind laden and weighed down by iniquities, he be unable to flee; and let him flee not as one carrying a nursing child but as one carrying a child unencumbered, not bearing a little one but presenting one perfect in Christ; let him flee not as one keeping holiday on the sabbath, but as a workman at his business, nor as one barren in the cold, but as one rich at the harvest. For this is why it was said: Pray that your flight may not be in winter or on the sabbath. Flight, then, is fruitful in virtues, not exhausted of merits. This flight knows nothing of the cold of fear, the trembling of death, the cramping of anxiety, the idleness of slackness, the holidays of wantonness, the torpor of sluggishness; but it requires an untiring traveller to the heavenly life, a vigorous candidate for the kingdom above, a rich husbandman who gathers in his fruits and, in gathering them, carries them off as spoil.

33. For what else is asked of you, O man, but that you fear God, seek after him, walk after him, lay hold of his ways? With what, he says, shall I lay hold of the Lord? Shall I lay hold of him with burnt offerings? Not with ten thousands of kids, not with thousands of rams, not with the fruits of ungodliness is the Lord reconciled and are sins redeemed; but in a good life the Lord's grace is laid hold of. It has been told you, he says, O man, what is good; or what else does the Lord require of you but that you do justice, and love mercy, and be ready to go with your Lord? So the Gospel says to you: Arise, let us go from here; the Law says to you: You shall walk after the Lord your God. You have learned how to flee from here: why do you delay? The Gospel says to you again: Brood of vipers, who showed you to flee from the wrath to come? And it says this to those who were coming to the baptism of repentance.

34. So repentance is a good flight; the grace of God is a good flight, in which is the taking up of the one who flees; the desert is a good flight, to which Elijah, Elisha and John the Baptist fled. Elijah fled the woman Jezebel, that is, the outpouring of vanity, and he fled to Mount Choreb, which means drying up, so that in him the carnal flood of vanity might be dried up and he might know God more fully. For he was at the brook Chorrad, which is knowledge, where he might draw the flowing abundance of divine knowledge, so fleeing the world that he did not even seek food for this body except what the birds brought him as his ministers, although his food was for the most part not earthly. Indeed, for forty days he walked in the strength of the food which he had received. Surely so great a prophet was not fleeing a woman, but this world; nor did he fear death, he who had offered himself to the one seeking him, and who said to the Lord: Take back my soul, bearing a weariness of this life, not a desire for it; but he was fleeing worldly enticement, and the contagion of a stained way of life, and the sacrileges of an ungodly and transgressing nation.

35. Solomon too, under the figure of that woman, sets forth the corruption of this world and teaches that the arts of the harlot must be turned aside from. This is the strange woman, the fornicator, against whom he exhorts you to guard yourself, and not to turn your heart aside to the ways of the world, but to place it in the hand of the Lord, in which is the heart of the king. For he who rules himself, which is worth more than commanding others, has his heart in the hand of God, and God turns it wherever he wills. It is no wonder if he turns it to good, he who is of perfect goodness. Let us, then, be in the hand of God, so that we may seek the good, that incorruptible and unchangeable good of which the prophet Amos says: Seek good and not evil, that you may live; and so the Lord God almighty will be with you, as you have said: We have hated evil things and have loved good things. Where, then, the good God is, there are the good things which David desired to see and believed that he would see, as he himself says: I believe that I shall see the good things of the Lord in the land of the living. For those are good things which remain forever, which cannot be corrupted by any change of time or age.

36. He who has sought God and found him is among those good things. For where a man's heart is, there also is his treasure; for the Lord is not wont to deny a good gift to those who entreat him. Since, therefore, the Lord is good, and good above all to those who wait for him, let us cleave to him, let us be with him with all our soul, with all our heart, with all our strength, so that we may be in his light and see his glory and enjoy the grace of the delight on high. To that good, then, let us lift up our minds, and let us be in it and live in it, let us cleave to it, which is above every mind and every consideration and enjoys perpetual peace and tranquillity; and peace is above every mind and above every sense. This is the good which penetrates all things, and we all live in it and depend on it; but it has nothing above itself, but is divine; for no one is good but the one God. What, then, is good is divine, and what is divine is good. And therefore it is said: When you open your hand, all things will be filled with goodness; for through the goodness of God all things that are rightly called good are granted to us, things with which no evil is mixed. These good things Scripture promises to the faithful, saying: You shall eat the good things of the land. Let us, then, be like that good, so that we may obtain the things that are good, the good which is without iniquity and without guile and without harshness, with grace, with devotion, with sincerity and benevolence, love, justice. And so goodness, like a fruitful mother, embraces all the virtues.

Chapter VII.

37. There is, then, no slight reason why we ought to flee from here: that we may come from evil things to good, from uncertain things to things trustworthy and full of truth, from death to life. The Lord himself also, by saying: I have set before you good and evil, life and death, showed that the good is life, but eternal life. For if this life is subject to the world and to its malignity, surely that life is good which is not corrupted and changed, which degenerates through no vice, which is acquired by virtue. Let us flee, then, the wickedness of this world, in which the days themselves, he says, are evil, and let us flee untiringly. And so Isaiah cries: Be strong, you slackened hands and loosened knees, that is, be strong, knees not of the body but of the soul, so that the footstep of the mind, directed to the highest places of heaven, may be lifted up, so that its course may be firmer, its life riper, its grace fuller, its prudence more circumspect.

38. For this is to flee: to know where you are heading, to lift oneself up from the world, to lift oneself up from the body, lest again someone exalt himself in vain and, puffed up by the mind of their flesh, they do not hold the head, and it be said of them: They fled and did not see. But this is to flee from here: to die to the elements of this world, to hide one's life in God, to turn aside from corruptions, not to be defiled by desires, not to know the things that belong to this world, which lays various sorrows upon us, empties when it has filled, and fills when it has emptied. And all these things are void and empty, and in them there is no solid fruit. The rich man has died and has nothing, because he is not rich toward God; and therefore he is foolish, because wisdom is the worship of God, and to abstain from evils is discipline.

39. Who, then, would not flee the place of wickedness, the workshop of depravity, which knows not how to perish? Indeed, it was not idly that a mark was placed upon Cain, lest anyone should kill him: so that it might be signified that wickedness is not extinguished and removed from the earth. Cain feared that he would be killed, because he did not know how to flee. For wickedness is increased and heaped up by its very practice, and it is without measure, without end, contending by guile and fraud; and it is betrayed by its deeds and by the blood of those it has slain, as Cain too was betrayed. So wickedness dwells on earth and wanders here, and therefore we pray that the will of God be done on earth as it is in heaven, so that innocence may be here too. So wickedness, since it has no place there, goes about here, rages here and pours itself out, not drowned by that flood of the world, not burnt up by the fire of Sodom. Indeed afterwards it sprouted more grievously in its seedbeds, even to the point of laying parricidal and sacrilegious hands upon the very author of the salvation of all. The Law condemns the deed; it does not take away wickedness. The Lord Jesus himself condemned sin and put off its author, so that there might be one through whom the just would be proved. For since God did not make evil, and the malice of the devil sowed it in, God put off vengeance, so that the devil might be conquered by those very ones whom he had deceived.

40. The deceived, then, are put to exercise, so that they may both pay the price of their credulity and take up the pursuit of virtue and the diligence of caution. Hence he says: Be shrewd as serpents. Why as serpents? So that he too may be stripped of what is his own, and he who wished to strip others may lose what belongs to him, not his poisons but the merits of his nature. Indeed, he is cast down when you have ascended. For it is written: I saw Satan falling like lightning from heaven. Not lightning, he says, but like lightning, because he lost his own light, which he had before he wished to take your light away from you. But perhaps you will cite what we read of the Saviour, that as the lightning flashing from under heaven, so will be the coming of the Son of Man. Rightly is it here too 'like lightning', because he is above the lightning. For the lightning is from under heaven, but the true light is above heaven. So Satan is like lightning, because he lost what he had; but you have received back what you had lost.

41. For the sentence has not been loosed for him as it has for you; for the grace of Christ, which loosed you, bound him fast; for the curse remains, directed against the serpent because of your deception. For thus it was said to him: Cursed are you by all the beasts of the earth. For he who was the foe of the good is the common enemy of all, and he is condemned on behalf of those whom he had not yet harmed, since he who harmed man, to whom all those things are subject, harmed all things. For he broke the common law, since he himself, with the rest, is made subject to man. Therefore his curse is weighed down by the common hatred and execration of all. And the kind of condemnation is not death but a lasting punishment. Upon your breast, he says, and belly you shall walk. For the guilty conscience had to be pressed down, wickedness trampled, and the fraudulent secret banished, as it were, from the face of God; at the same time it is shown that wickedness is earthly, which bends one back down to the earth. And so he added: And you shall eat earth all the days of your life.

42. By these words the nature of the serpent seems indeed to be described, but rather every vessel of wickedness is described, every serpent of depravity, who casts himself down upon his belly and shuts his poison up within himself and rolls it about inside in his breast, slippery in his thoughts and walking upon his frauds, entangling himself in his own wiles, always stirring and agitating his poisons when he thinks, trampling also his belly, that is, the seedbed of his heart. Hence David says beautifully: Sinners have been estranged from the womb, they have gone astray from the belly, they have spoken falsehoods. Their fury is after the likeness of the serpent, like the deaf asp that stops its ears, which will not hear the voice of the charmers and of the sorcerer, who are charmed by the wise man. For this reason, too, that seems beautifully said which we read in a prophetic book: I am in pain in my belly, my belly! because wickedness is there where innocence ought to be, and that part labours the more which ought to be the calmer in us, and it is trodden down by the footprints of malice, goaded by its claws, shaken by a certain advance and growth of depravity, there where the generative seed of an eternal posterity lies. This seems indeed to be referred to his grief that the sons whom he had begotten and taken up had perished, but much more to his not having in his belly the seedbed of the word. Therefore he speaks of his belly as of something most precious. For precious is the womb of the mind, in which a fruitful crop of counsels is wont to spring up from the seed of the word, and in which certain members, as it were, of the whole life and of the virtues and disciplines are wont to be formed.

43. But to return to our theme, namely that God judged that wickedness should for the meantime be held in check rather than abolished: he says to the serpent: And I will put enmities between you and the woman, and between your seed and the seed of the woman. She will watch for your head, and you for her heel. Where there are enmities, there is discord and the eagerness to harm; where there is the eagerness to harm, there wickedness is placed. So there is discord between the serpent and the woman. Wickedness underlies discord; therefore wickedness has not been taken away. Indeed, it was granted the serpent to watch for the heel of the woman and of her seed, where he might harm and pour in his poison. Let us not, then, walk among earthly things, and the serpent will not be able to harm us. Let us put on the shoe of the Gospel, by which the serpent's poison is shut out and his bite blunted, so that we may have our feet shod for the Gospel. And perhaps this is why Moses is bidden to loose the shoe from his feet, that he might put on the shoe of the Gospel; or else because the preaching of the Gospel was due not to Moses, that is, not to the prophets, but to the apostles. This is the sentence pronounced against the serpent, of which we spoke above; let us consider also of what kind was the one directed against man.

44. Cursed is he who is the author of the fault, but this one is not cursed, who was deceived by another's fraud; yet, because he did not keep the commandment of God, he is condemned to the toil of his works — the earth indeed is cursed, but in the works of the sinner — and he is cursed until he is dissolved into the earth. For this reason, then, Jesus took on flesh, so that he might abolish the curse of sinful flesh; and he was made a curse for us, so that blessing might swallow up the curse, integrity sin, pardon the sentence, life death. For he took on death too, so that the sentence might be fulfilled and the judgement satisfied: the curse of sinful flesh until death. Nothing, then, was done against the sentence of God, since the condition of the divine sentence was fulfilled; for the curse was until death, after death grace. We are dead, then, to the world: why do we still contend about the world? We have died with Christ: why do we still seek the business of this life? We carry about the death of Christ in our body, so that the life of Christ too may be made manifest in us. We live, then, no longer that life of ours but the life of Christ, a life of innocence, a life of chastity, a life of simplicity and of all the virtues. We have risen with Christ: let us live in him, let us ascend in him, so that the serpent may not be able to find on earth our heel to wound.

Chapter VIII.

45. Let us flee from here. You can flee in mind, even if you are held back in body. You can both be here and be present with the Lord, if your soul cleaves to him, if you walk after him in your thoughts, if you follow his ways by faith, not by sight, if you take refuge in him. For he is a refuge and a power, to whom David says: In you I have taken refuge, and I have not been deceived. Since, then, God is a refuge, and God is in heaven and above the heavens, surely we must flee from here to there, where there is peace, where there is rest from works, where we may feast on the great sabbath, as Moses said: And the sabbaths of the land shall be food for you. For it is a banquet, full of joyfulness and tranquillity, to rest in God and to see his delight. Shall we, then, who have taken refuge in God, return to the world? Shall we who have died to sin seek our sins again? Shall we who have renounced the world and its use stick once more in its mud?

46. Let us flee from here, because the time is short. Hear how you are to flee: And let those who have wives be as if they had none, and those who weep as though they were not weeping, and those who rejoice as though they were not rejoicing, and those who buy as though they were not possessing, and those who use this world as though they did not use it; for the form of this world is passing away. Let not our works, then, pass away with the passing form of the world, lest we ourselves also pass away; but let us remain in the truth. If we remain in Christ, we remain in the truth, and we shall remain with him and shall not pass away, but we shall say: The blessing of the Lord be upon you; we bless you in the name of the Lord. For those who pass by cannot say it, because those who passed by the way did not say: The blessing of the Lord be upon you, as the prophet said. So if we wish our works not to pass away, let us not pass by the commandment of God, let us not pass by the diligence of seeking the Lord Jesus, of earning his grace. That woman did not pass him by. She, going even into the house of the Pharisee, in which Christ was reclining, poured out ointment upon his feet.

47. Let us not pass by progress in any discipline, as Joseph too, as a youth, did not pass it by, because he was seeking his brothers and hastening to the place where they were pasturing the sheep; and when he learned that they were in Dothaim, he went on there. By that word Dothaim is signified a fitting failing, that is, a failing of vain opinions, not moderate but perfect and full, in which lies the progress of the soul. For the wise man, when he diminishes, adds; for worldly opinions fail, and they are compared to weak women. Hence it is beautifully said, to show that Sarah had come to progress, so as to bring forth laughter and gladness: There had failed, it says, in Sarah the ways of women. Good, then, is the failing of desires, the failing of vanity, because it is the addition of truth. Hence the holy man too says: My soul has failed for your salvation. For she was to bear him whom God had granted and therefore had also promised; she was to bear gladness and that sober inebriation of joyfulness; she was to bear offspring sooner than expected, by the swiftness of natural talent, pregnant with wisdom. Indeed, of other Hebrew women too you have it that the Hebrew women give birth before the midwives come, because the souls of the just do not wait for arts and disciplines for the various kinds of discovery, nor do they require aids to bringing forth, but of their own accord pour forth their offspring and anticipate expectation.

48. Nor was Jacob less quick to find than to carry out; when his mother had told him to bring food in to his father, we see that kinds of definition were at his command. For he both found quickly and testified that God was the author of what he had found, in which are signified both the palm of diligence and the richness of natural talent. The first definition, then, lies in discovery. What is found is sought, and what is sought belongs to time, and what belongs to time surely belongs to diligence. But what anticipates the use of time, God pours in, God gives; and what God gives belongs to nature, not to diligence. Natural talent, therefore, is of divine gift, and what is of divine gift is of nature. Natural talent, then, belongs to nature, discovery to diligence: the former is without time, the latter needs time. And so the former is at hand at the moment, the latter is searched out over a space of time; the former is referred to what is above us, the latter to us.

49. So when his father sought the food of the word — for man does not live by bread alone, but by every word of God; such food Isaac sought, such food Peter hungered for when he saw the mysteries of the Gentile people who were to believe — Esau did not have at hand the food of a spiritual and swifter talent. While he hunts and seeks and prepares to offer a very hard and rustic discourse, Jacob anticipates him with a swift discovery and with a gentle and almost homely answer, serving a sweet word with which to soothe and delight him. Hence his father, in wonder, says: What is this, that you have found so quickly, my son? Jacob answered: What the Lord your God delivered into my hands. The first definition is in the question, the second in the answer. So the father, finding complete everything that belonged either to natural talent or to diligence, concluded with the consummation of a definition, saying: Behold, the smell of my son is as the smell of a full field, which the Lord has blessed. For a field has both the natural talent of fertility and the temporal diligence of cultivation, and rightly is there fullness in that which lacks neither. At the same time, since he added which the Lord has blessed, he seems to have preferred the grace of nature to the labour of cultivation. And Esau confirmed it, saying: Rightly was his name called Jacob; for he has supplanted me now twice and has taken my birthright.

50. But perhaps you will say: In what did he cause delay, he who was sent by his father to hunt? But consider that his father too declared that he could not have ready what was being sought spiritually. And therefore, since he was failing in natural talent, he required him at least to add the help of diligence. He also admonishes him to speed, saying: Take your bow and arrow. Yet he too receives the fruit of diligence who recognizes that the gift of natural talent is higher.

51. And not undeservedly did he bring home-grown food, he who gained for himself union with wisdom from among the household of his own seed. Good is diligence with wisdom, and discovery with natural talent. Indeed, holy Isaac, having union with wisdom close at hand, was walking in the field, or rather was withdrawing himself. Let us too first be united with wisdom, and so go out into the field, desiring to seek and to find. For many seek badly without wisdom. And therefore Cain, because he had not received prudence from God, sought badly and went out badly into the field; Abel well, who fulfilled the perfect gift of sacrifice; for good is the sacrifice of wisdom, good victims are faith and every virtue. Indeed, Wisdom slew her victims and mixed her wine in the mixing bowl. And so, to give the drink of faith to the foolish Gentiles, she called them together to her mixing bowl, saying: Whoever is foolish, let him turn aside to me; and to those lacking sense she said: Come, eat of my loaves and drink the wine which I have mixed for you. This mixing bowl Plato thought fit to transfer into his books, and to its drink he called forth souls; but he did not know how to fill them, since he was serving a drink not of faith but of faithlessness.

Chapter IX.

52. Let us then flee from here, as holy Jacob fled from his fatherland; for he knew that the true fatherland is the one above. Let us flee as harts to the fountains of waters: let our soul too thirst for those for which David thirsted. What is that fountain? Hear him saying: For with you is the fountain of life. Let my soul say to this fountain: When shall I come and appear before your face? For God is the fountain; but let him who desires this fountain pour out his soul upon himself, so that he leaves nothing to the possession of the flesh, but the soul overflows everywhere.

53. Beautifully did Susanna pour it out, so that the fires of the body, the fears of death, the desires of life could not steam up into it. The soul poured out upon her quenched every carnal desire, every worldly pursuit. It could even have quenched the flames of those monstrous old men and shameless elders, had not a flood of lust overflowed in them. When they threatened her with slander, should consent to adultery be refused, Susanna groaned and said: Distresses are upon me on every side. For if I do this, I shall perish by eternal death; but if not, I shall not flee from your hands. Yet she judged it better to flee the crime rather than the danger. So she wept when the crime was charged against her; she wept when adulterers claimed for themselves judgment over a modest and chaste woman, not lamenting death, but she wept for the slander on her chastity, the injury to religion, and she poured out upon herself not her body but her soul; for she would have poured out her body if she had yielded to carnal things. Finally, when she was condemned to death, she cried out as judge of the accused, arbiter of the slanderers, and by the authority of an innocent conscience she summoned the divine knowledge to her judgment, not stirred by fear of death, but lofty in the power of a censure that convicts. Susanna therefore fled the world and entrusted herself to God, fleeing to that citadel of the eternal city which embraces the whole world, since all things are within God.

54. Paul too fled, so as to get away, let down through the window in a basket — for he knew that a threefold cord of broom cannot be broken — but he fled so that he might preach the Gospel of the Lord in the whole world, and therefore he was caught up into paradise. Let us too flee through the window, hearing the precepts of the Lord and keeping them with sober sight and chastity of the eyes.

55. Let us flee as Lot did, fearing the crimes of Sodom more than its punishments. For more devout, surely, is he who fled the contagion of crimes, who shut his house to the Sodomites, and, while dwelling among them, did not know those whose shameful deeds he was ignorant of and whose disgraces he turned away from, and in his flight did not look back at those whose way of life he did not desire. He then flees as Lot did who renounces vices, who disowns the customs of the inhabitants, who does not look back behind him, who enters that higher city by the entrance of his thoughts and does not withdraw from it until the prince of priests dies, who took away the sin of the world. He died indeed once, but he dies for each one who is baptized into the death of Christ, that we may be buried together with him and rise again with him and walk in the newness of his life.

56. You flee well, if your heart does not imitate the counsels of sinners and their thoughts. You flee well, if your eye flees cups and bowls, lest it become livid while it lingers over wine. You flee well, if your eye turns away from the strange woman, so that your tongue may keep the truth. You flee well, if you do not answer the imprudent man according to his imprudence. You flee well, if you take the step of your feet away from the mouth of the foolish. For under bad guides one quickly goes astray; but if you wish to flee well, make your ways far from their discourse.

57. The prince of priests has died for you, for you he was crucified, that you might cleave to his nails. For in that flesh he took up both you and your sins; the handwritings of your offences were fixed to his gibbet, so that you now owe nothing to the world, which you once renounced. And rightly you owe nothing, you who are able to say: For the world is crucified to me, and I to the world, so that you no longer fear death, if you bear Christ, in whom you can say: Where, O death, is your victory? Where, O death, is your sting? For when that old man of ours was fixed to the cross, sin was destroyed, the sting blunted, guilt made void, so that we may cease any longer to serve shameful deeds. For the old man too has gone away. But now it is no longer the old man in us, but there is a new creature, having in itself the likeness of Christ, with whom we were buried together in the likeness of his death and so have taken on the image of his life; we have received the wings of spiritual grace.

58. So then, fly in such a way that it may be said of you: Who are these who fly like a cloud, and like doves with their young? so that your clouds may drop justice like dew and your doves bring forth simplicity. Sail in such a way, as men crossing over the world, not wandering through it like the ships of Tarshish, that you may direct your course into intelligible harbours and carry home the riches of the sea. Hasten in such a way that it may be said of you: They have become swifter than eagles. For you see that one must flee from the wrath to come, which they will be able to avoid who through repentance have set up for themselves the hope of escaping and have drunk in faith in the reconciliation to come, through our Lord Jesus Christ, to whom is the kingdom from the ages, and now and always, and for all the ages of ages. Amen.
