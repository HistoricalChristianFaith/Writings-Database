Explanation of the Creed

Latin source: the sermon in which Ambrose of Milan explains the creed to the candidates for baptism when it is handed over to them (late fourth century), apparently taken down by a hearer, from the text of the St Gall manuscript (Stiftsbibliothek, Cod. Sang. 188, seventh or eighth century) printed by C. P. Caspari, Alte und neue Quellen zur Geschichte des Taufsymbols und der Glaubensregel (Christiania, 1879), pp. 213–222, verified against the page images and collated in full with Ballerini's edition (Milan, 1883), which prints the two other surviving copies, a Bobbio–Vatican manuscript and a lost Lambach manuscript. Caspari prints the manuscript with its scribal errors; they are corrected where his notes or his discussion name the right reading, and where the manuscript itself, checked on its images, shows that his print departs from it. Two passages the manuscript loses by homoeoteleuton are supplied from the Bobbio–Vatican copy, and one word it omits from the other copies, inside ⟨ ⟩. Scripture and the creed are translated as Ambrose quotes them, from his own Latin; italics mark them. The hearer's stage directions (the candidates sign themselves; the creed is recited) are in square brackets. The Latin words symbolum and symbola are kept where Ambrose explains the name. The section numbers are Caspari's. Other English translations: R. H. Connolly, The Explanatio Symboli ad Initiandos: A Work of Saint Ambrose, Texts and Studies 10 (Cambridge, 1952); Boniface Ramsey, Ambrose, The Early Church Fathers (London: Routledge, 1997). This English translation is released into the public domain.

1. Now that the mysteries of the scrutinies have been celebrated up to this point, inquiry has been made lest any uncleanness should cling to anyone's body; through exorcism a sanctification not only of the body but also of the soul has been sought and applied. Now it is the time and the day for us to hand over the creed: the creed which is a spiritual seal, the creed which is the meditation of our heart and, as it were, an ever-present guard, surely the treasure of our breast.

2. First, therefore, we ought to receive the reason for the name itself. It is called symbolum in Greek, but in Latin "contribution"; and merchants especially have been accustomed to speak of a symbola, their pooled contribution, when they bring their money together: packed together into one, as it were, out of the contribution of each, it is kept whole and inviolate, so that no one may attempt to commit fraud against the contribution, no one against the trade. In fact, among the merchants themselves this is the custom, that if anyone has committed fraud, he is cast out as fraudulent. So the holy apostles, coming together into one, made a summary of the faith, in which we may briefly comprehend the whole sequence of the faith. Brevity is necessary, so that it may always be held in memory and recollection. I know that in the regions of the East especially, as to the things first handed down by our forebears: while some intend fraud and others carefulness, the heretics fraud, the Catholics carefulness, while, then, the former try fraudulently to creep in, they have added what is not needed; while the latter strive to guard against fraud, they seem, as it were out of a certain piety and want of care, to have gone beyond the bounds of our forebears.

3. So the holy apostles, coming together, made the creed briefly. [Sign yourselves!] [When they had done so:] In this creed the divinity of the eternal Trinity, of one operation, is most evidently comprised: the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit, that is, the venerable Trinity. And that this is our faith: that we believe in like manner in the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit. For where there is no distinction of majesty, neither ought there to be a distinction of faith. Further, I have often admonished you that our Lord Jesus Christ, the Son of God, alone took flesh in such a way, with a rational and perfect human soul; and he took the form of a body, the reality of this body; he was made as man; but he has a singular privilege of his generation. For he was not born of a man's seed, but begotten, it says, of the Holy Spirit from the Virgin Mary, acknowledging the prerogative of the heavenly Author; made indeed as man, so as to take up our infirmities in his own flesh, but he came with the privilege of eternal majesty. So let us say the creed. [And when he had said it, he concluded, saying:] This is what divine Scripture has. Ought we with a bold mind to go beyond the bounds of the apostles? Are we more cautious than the apostles?

4. But you say to me: Afterwards heresies emerged. For the apostles too say: There must be heresies, so that the good may be proved. What then? See the simplicity, see the purity. When the Patripassians had emerged, even the Catholics thought that at this point "invisible, impassible" should be added, as though the Son of God was visible and passible. If he was visible in the flesh, it was that flesh that was visible, not the divinity; it was passible, not the divinity. In fact, hear what he says: O God, my God, look upon me: why have you forsaken me? Our Lord Jesus Christ says this in his passion; he spoke this as man, he spoke according to the flesh, as though the flesh were saying to the divinity: Why have you forsaken me? So, be it that our forebears were physicians, that they wished to add health to the sickness; medicine is not sought. So if medicine was necessary at the time when there was a grave sickness in the souls of certain heretics, and so it had to be sought at that time, now it need not be. For what reason? The faith is entire against the Sabellians. The Sabellians have been shut out, especially from the regions of the West. From that remedy the Arians found for themselves a kind of calumny: and because we hold the creed of the Roman Church, therefore they would reckon the almighty Father invisible and impassible, and would say: You see that they have the creed so; they would designate the Son as visible and passible. What then? Where the faith is entire, the precepts of the apostles suffice: are the precautions of priests, then, plainly not required? Why? Because tares have been mixed in with the wheat.

5. Hence the creed: I believe … our only Lord. Say it thus: His only Son. Is he not the only Lord? There is one God, and one Lord too. But lest they slander us and say that we speak of one person, let us call the Son, too, "our only Lord". Since I have spoken of the divinity of the Father and of the Son, we come to his incarnation, by which he was born and suffered and was buried. You have both his passion and his burial. On the third day he rose again from the dead. You have his resurrection too. He ascended into the heavens, sits at the right hand of the Father. You see, then, that the flesh could diminish nothing of the divinity; rather, from the incarnation a great triumph was won for Christ. For for what reason, when he ascended from death, does he sit at the right hand of the Father? As one who has brought back to the Father the fruit of a good will. You have two things: He rose from death, he sits at the right hand of the Father. So the flesh could bring no loss to the glory of the divinity. You have two things: either our Lord Jesus Christ sits at the right hand of the Father, as it were according to the prerogative of the divinity and of the substance of his generation, when he rose from death; or surely, as the eternal triumpher who won a good kingdom for God the Father, he has claimed for himself the prerogative of his victory. He sits at the right hand of the Father, thence he will come to judge the living and the dead. ⟨Hear, O man: you ought indeed to believe quickly. Let faith itself be brought forth from love. He who loves detracts nothing; a friend who loves a friend detracts nothing; he who loves the Lord ought much more not to detract. Wherefore I say: sits. He who loves has something to fear. Hence and the dead.⟩ He himself is to judge us. Beware, then, of detracting from him whom you are to have as judge. Why this mystery? Is there not one judgment of the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit? Is there not one will? Is there not one majesty? Why are you told that the Son is to judge, unless that you may understand that nothing must be detracted from the Son? So see: You believe in the Father, you believe in the Son; and what third? And in the Holy Spirit. Whatever sacraments you will receive, you will receive in this Trinity. Let no one deceive you! You see, then, that the venerable Trinity is of one operation, of one sanctification, of one majesty.

6. Indeed, take the reason how we believe in the Author, lest perhaps you say: But it has the Church too, but it has the remission of sins too, but it has the resurrection too. What then? The case is equal. Thus we believe in Christ, thus we believe also in the Father, just as we also believe the Church and the remission of sins and the resurrection of the flesh. What is the reason? Because he who believes in the Author believes also the Author's work. In fact, lest you think this comes from our own ingenuity, take a testimony: If you do not believe me, at least believe the works. So you have this. Now your faith will shine out more fully if you think true and entire faith must be given to the work of your Author. The holy Church and the remission of sins. Believe, then, out of faith, that all your sins are remitted to you. That is why you have read in the Gospel, where the Lord says: Your faith has made you whole. The remission of sins, the resurrection of the flesh. Believe that the flesh too will rise again. For what need was there for Christ to take flesh? what need was there for Christ to mount the cross? what need was there for Christ to taste death, and undergo burial, and rise again, except for your resurrection? This whole sacrament is that of your resurrection. If Christ has not risen, our faith is vain; but because he has risen, therefore our faith is firm.

7. So I have said that the apostles composed the creed. If, then, the merchants of these wares and those who contribute money have this law, that if anyone violates his symbola, he is to be held dishonest and infamous, much more must we beware lest anything be taken away from the creed of our forebears; since you have in the book of the Apocalypse of John, which little book is canonical and serves above all for the foundation of the faith (for there he evidently made mention of our almighty Lord Jesus Christ, though in other places too, yet then in that little book itself): If anyone, he says, adds or takes away, he takes judgment and punishment upon himself. If nothing is to be taken away from the writings of a single apostle, how shall we defile the creed which we have received as handed down and composed by the apostles? We ought to take away nothing, to add nothing. And this is the creed which the Roman Church holds, where Peter, the first of the apostles, sat and to which he brought the common statement.

8. So, just as there are twelve apostles, there are also twelve articles. [Sign yourselves!] I believe in God the Father almighty, and in Jesus Christ his only Son, the Lord, conceived of the Holy Spirit, born of the Virgin Mary, suffered under Pontius Pilate, crucified, dead and buried. You have the divinity of the Father, the divinity of the Son, the incarnation of the Son, the passion, death and burial. See, these are four articles. Let us look at others. That he rose again on the third day, ascended into the heavens, sat at the right hand of God the Father; thence he will come, to judge the living and the dead. See, another four articles, that is, eight articles. Let us look at yet another four. I believe also in the Holy Spirit, the holy Church, the communion of saints, the remission of sins, the resurrection of the flesh, eternal life. Amen. See, according to the twelve apostles twelve ⟨articles⟩ too are comprised.

9. I want you indeed to be warned of this, that the creed ought not ⟨to be written, because you have to give it back. But let no one write it. For what reason? We received it so, that it ought not⟩ to be written, but what? To be held. But what is it you say to me: How can it be held if it is not written? It can be held better if it is not written. For what reason? Take this. For what you write, feeling secure, as though you would read it again, you will not begin to recite in daily meditation; but what you do not write, you fear you may lose, and you begin to recite it every day. And it is a great safeguard: there arise stupors of mind and body, the temptation of the adversary, who never rests, some trembling of the body, an infirmity of the stomach; recite the creed and be healed within yourself; above all, recite it within yourself. Why? Lest you form a habit, and, when you recite it aloud alone or where the faithful are, you begin to recite it among catechumens or heretics.
