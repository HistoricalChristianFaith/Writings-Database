Book One. On the Interrogation of Job and on the Weakness of Man

Chapter I

1. That we must undergo many troubles in this life, divine Scripture shows in frequent passages, and also that many consolations are at hand, by which a mind capable of vigour and conscious of what is right ought to swallow up whatever belongs to present hardships and to look upon those things that hold unending delight. For consolations outweigh troubles, since they bring both the calming of present things and the hope of things to come. Hence the apostle Paul too: Unworthy are he says the sufferings of this time in comparison with the glory that is to come: unworthy, certainly, as regards the consolation of comparison, not as regards the fruit of redemption.

2. For whose life on earth is so illustrious that it could equal that heavenly glory? What is more sublime than Paul, who underwent so many dangers and swallowed so much pain and so many weaknesses? In the sufferings which he underwent for the name of Christ he was dying daily, as he himself says, and he reckoned that he suffered nothing unworthy in this time, for the hope and expectation of so great a glory. Elijah endured hunger, plots and the terrors of death, the bitterness of toils: and yet he alone, brought down from heaven to earth by fiery chariots and fiery horses and carried back from earth to heaven, hid all the merit of this warfare and lifted the grace of the devout rider beyond all things human. For what shall I say of Peter? Judging his cross unworthy of the future reward, he asked to be hung with his feet turned upward, so as to add something to his passion, he who did not fear to heap up its torments upon himself.

3. Hence not without reason holy David, both in the rest of his work and most of all in Psalm 41 (42), testified that he was hastening to that glory, saying: When shall I come and appear before the face of God? In that psalm he has plainly expressed both the troubles of human frailty and the consolations from the Lord. In it he also appeals to God on our behalf, because, forgetful of his work, forgetful of the liberality and grace conferred on man, he has forsaken the one whom he had undertaken to protect and adorn, and has cast him off, weak and shipwrecked, to be worn down by various weaknesses. Holy Job had done the same before him, but David more gently, Job more vehemently. It is my heart's purpose, therefore, to consider the interrogations of both, because in them the pattern of human life is expressed, its cause is pleaded, its prerogative is formed. In their order, then, we must look at them.

Chapter II

4. So Job, having lost his children and all that was his except his wife, who alone had been kept for him for his temptation, and drenched moreover with a grievous sore, when he saw that his friends had come not to console him but to aggravate and heap up his pain, perceived that power to tempt him had been given by the Lord to the adversary against him. And although he felt that the arrows of the Lord were in his body, by which he said he was pierced, yet like a good athlete who would not yield to pain nor refuse the hardships of the contest, he added: Let the Lord begin to wound me, but in the end let him not destroy me. For what is my strength, that I hold out, or what is my time, that my soul should bear it? Is my strength the strength of stones, or is my flesh of bronze? Or did I not trust in him? But help has departed from me, and his visitation has despised me.

5. Is not the life of man on earth a trial, and his life like that of a daily hireling, or like a servant fearing his master, who covers himself under a shade, or like a hireling awaiting his wages? So I too have waited for empty months, and nights of pains have been given to me. If I rest, I say: When is it day? If I rise, again: When is it evening? I am full of pains from evening until morning. My body ferments in the rot of worms, and I melt the clods of earth, scraping the matter of my sores. But my life is lighter than a tale; it perishes in empty hope. I shall say: My bed will console me. You terrify me in dreams and strike me down in visions.

Chapter III

6. How wretched is the condition of man, which, like a hireling, labours for others, is in want for itself, and cannot sustain itself except by another's mercy! Daily under dread, under fear, it endures a heavy servitude, and, so as not to be caught by its master, wandering and fugitive, it thinks it can hide under some shade of this world. Consider that man of whom Sirach says in Ecclesiasticus: Every man who transgresses on his bed, despising and saying in his soul: Who sees me? Darkness surrounds me, and walls. Whom do I fear? Does he not seem to you truly to be a hireling, who ..... has squandered, like that young man in the Gospel who, it is read, ..... the portion of the substance received from his father, and, needy and destitute, to relieve his hunger, began to feed another's flocks, so as to earn his own keep by a wage? But that one was nevertheless at length converted, because he returned to his father and did not suppress his sins but disclosed them; whereas this man, who thinks he is not seen by him who sees all things and supposes that his misdeeds can be hidden by darkness, holds a shade before himself, but in vain believes that he lies hidden, since the eye of the Lord, brighter than the sun, catches out all hidden things, illumines what is dark, penetrates the conscience of the inmost heart and descends into the heights and the depths. Vain, then, is he who thinks he is safe in darkness, since he cannot avoid the light which shines in the darkness, and the darkness does not comprehend it. Like a fugitive, therefore, and a bad hireling, he is caught, and he is recognized before he hides himself, because all things are known to the Lord before he seeks them, not only what has been done but also what is to come.

7. He perishes, then, in empty hope who thinks that he can hide his crime: that is a tale, not the truth. Indeed, the tale of sinners is hateful, having no fruit but groaning. For the narration of a fool is a burden on the way. For what else is sin but a burden, which weighs down the traveller of this world, pressed down by the heavy load of offence? If he did not wish to lie under the burden, he ought to have listened to the one who says: Come to me, all you who labour and are burdened, and I will refresh you.

8. But what is more full of tribulation than when the very bed, given for common rest, inflicts a grievous wound? For then we are wont to recall what we have done, and the conscience within is pierced by the goads of its own deeds. Hence Scripture too says to such men: What you say in your hearts, be pierced for it also on your beds? It is indeed the remedy of a debt, but still it has wounded the conscience. But granted that sleep sometimes creeps upon the weary, we are terrified by dreams, we are harassed by visions, so that this is no longer rest but punishment. For thus the riches of the world pass away like the dream of one rising up. Someone has risen from the sleep of this body, and he has possessed nothing, and has lost the very thing he believed he had.

9. Set before me now that rich man who used to amass daily gains and various profits and was harassed by his own desires, suddenly coming to his senses and considering within himself that when he dies he will take nothing at all, nor will the glory of his house go down with him, and that the things he possesses in this life have some pleasure, but not for the future as well, and so opening his eyes to heavenly things. Does he not seem to you, like one who was drinking in dreams, to have drunk, and like one who was feasting in dreams, to have feasted, but then to have opened his eyes and learned that his soul hoped for something empty and is still hungry and still thirsty, because avarice has no limit and is not filled by plundering, but is spurred on, the more needy the more it has acquired? So this man too has risen, and the dream is vain.

Chapter IV

10. But let us hear him speaking once more. Those friends who had come to console him were pressing upon him and, like enemies, were urging him hard with bitter words. For there is one kind of solace for those placed in tribulation and bitterness: to be free of guilt, so that they may not seem to bear the adversities they suffer as the price of an offence. This too they were eager to take from the holy man, so that he himself should seem the author of his own tribulation, as one who had incurred the Lord's displeasure by grievous sins and was enduring these things for his ungodly deeds. They described the punishments of the ungodly, who sow vices and reap pains for themselves: that they perish by the command of God and are destroyed by the spirit of his anger, of him who breathes upon those who inhabit houses of clay and they wither, who brings to nothing the thoughts of the crafty, who stops the mouth of the unjust man. These things they asserted, true indeed of the Lord's power, but not befitting the merits of so great a man. To them, therefore, he answered: In truth I know that it is so.

11. For how shall a mortal be just before the Lord? For if he should wish to contend with him in judgment, he will not hear him, so that he may not contradict one word of his with a thousand speeches. For he is wise in understanding, and strong and great. Who is so hard that he can stand in his sight? He who makes the mountains grow old, and they do not know, and overturns them in his wrath; who shakes the whole earth from its foundations, and its pillars are moved; who speaks to the sun, and it does not rise, and sets his seal against the stars; who alone stretches out the heaven and walks as on a pavement upon the sea; who makes the Pleiades and the evening star and the north and the ministry of the south wind; who does great and unsearchable things, glorious and immense, of which there is no number. If he passes by me, I shall not see, and if he has gone past me, not even so shall I know. With how much more vehement a trumpet has this man sounded forth about the Lord's power? But in it there is help for the just, not ruin. In fact, it is power that seems to be expressed, but rather the mysteries of our redemption are declared.

Chapter V

12. For who are the mountains which he made grow old but Moses, Aaron, Joshua son of Nun, Gideon, the prophets, all the books of the Old Testament? The Lord Jesus came: he brought a New Testament, and what had been before was made old. The Christian has been made new, the Jew has grown old: grace has been renewed, the letter has grown old. He has overturned the mountains and turned them about. For he overturned and undermined the understanding according to the letter and established the spiritual understanding. That carnal understanding of the Law, then, has vanished: it has become spiritual. Hence the Apostle says: But we know that the Law is spiritual, but I am carnal. But he too who was carnal has himself become spiritual, as he himself declared, saying: For I think that I too have the Spirit of God. These mountains, then, Jesus has made grow old, and the Jews do not know. For if they had known, they would never have crucified the Lord of majesty, they would never still be following Jewish ravings. They, then, are the ones who do not know. Hence in the Gospel too the Lord Jesus says: Forgive them, Father, because they do not know what they do.

13. But they are not excused because they do not know, since they are unwilling to know what they ought to have recognized. Certainly we do not begrudge them, if they follow the Lord's sentence; for a sentence usually absolves what is past, not what is to come. But neither is he free from crime who crucified the author of his own salvation and afterwards did not ask for pardon. Granted that before he did not know whom he was persecuting: yet he ought to have recognized the one placed on the cross as the Lord of all the elements, under whom all the elements trembled, the heaven was darkened, the sun fled back, the earth burst apart, the tombs of the dead lay open, the dead received again the company of the living. Hence the centurion too said: Truly this was the Son of God. The centurion acknowledges one not his own, the Levite does not recognize his own: the Gentile worships, the Hebrew abjures. Not without reason, then, were the pillars of the whole earth moved when the chief priests did not believe; but the old ones were moved so that the new might be established, as he himself deigned to say: I have established its pillars. Hear which pillars he has established: Peter and James and John, who seemed to be pillars, gave me and Barnabas the right hands of fellowship.

14. How then do they excuse themselves as not having known, when some of them saw, and others learned, that the sun, before the course of the day was complete, withdrew itself and again, before the space of the night was finished, came forth, and made night in the day, and day in the night? Surely they ought to have understood that the sun withdrew at a command and came forth at a command. For the Lord had foretold that he would be in the heart of the earth three days and three nights: the sun learned this, it kept the precept. So it was in doubt, saying: What am I to do? I rise, and it is day; I set, and it is night. If I keep my course, I shall delay the salvation of the world. Let us hasten to our own redemption too: I too must hasten to the new life; for by the benefit of the cross, by which all things are renewed, there will be both a new sun and a new heaven. I hasten, therefore, that I may see that sun of justice who enlightens the souls of all. But what am I to do? He himself wills that the resurrection take place after three days. I have found what to do, so that I both make no delay and keep the number of the days. I shall not make a whole day and a whole night. I shall shorten the hours, so that the Lord Jesus may indeed be among the dead for three days and nights, yet rise from the dead sooner than the intervals of three days and nights allow. I shall shorten the hours, therefore, once he has mounted the cross. From the sixth hour let night fall at once, that I may not see the Lord's passion, but may flee the spectacle of a parricidal persecution. I shall set, and there will be a night of three hours; I shall come forth and renew the day, so that it is of three hours: the first day has run its course; the second night will follow in its own span; the day will likewise follow; the third night will begin; the Lord will rise in the night, and it will be day in the light of the one who rises, so that that saying may be fulfilled: And the night shall be lit up as the day. This is that great day which Abraham saw and was glad, of which David too said: This is the day which the Lord has made; let us exult and rejoice in it, a day at which I shall be present not in the ministry of labour but in the fruit of exultation.

15. The Lord himself, then, will lead forth the day, and before the consummation of the world the light of the world will be seen, which enlightens every man coming into this world. He is the Lord who seals and numbers the multitude of the stars: he it is who alone stretches out the heaven, who walked as on a pavement upon the sea, when Peter saw him walking and said: Lord, bid me come to you upon the waters. And the Lord bade him, but he faltered, and had the Lord not stretched out his right hand to him, he would have sunk beneath the waves and perished. The flesh faltered, the right hand saved. And he said to him: O you of little faith, why did you doubt? It was faith, then, that walked in the apostle, not the flesh. In fact, faith faltered, and the flesh had begun to feel shipwreck. This is not improperly said, because the flesh is the ship of the soul, as it is written: Those who go down to the sea in ships, and elsewhere: There were pains as of a woman in labour. With a vehement spirit you will crush the ships of Tarshish. For our souls, when they are in labour with the Word, bring forth pains; but she who has given birth no longer remembers the sorrow, for joy that a man has been born to her who redeemed the world. The ships of Tarshish, then, that is, intelligible ships, which carried gold and silver to Solomon, are our bodies, which have a treasure in earthen vessels, as the Apostle said: either because here too they are crushed by the hazard of childbirth, according to what was said: Woe to those who are with child and to those who give suck! — for when the soul is shaken, the flesh is tossed — or because they will be stirred up by a vehement spirit poured into them, when, the time being fulfilled, the resurrection must come, according to what is written: Come, spirit, and breathe upon these dead, and they will live. Hence Job himself too says in later passages: For I know that he is eternal and mighty who is to release me, to raise up on the earth my skin, which has borne this. They are crushed, then, because they are raised up for judgment. And crushing is good; for a heart that is crushed and humbled God does not despise, and elsewhere: Heal its crushings. But to Jehoshaphat too it was said: They were crushed ..... to go to Tarshish, because he had joined himself to a sacrilegious man. Crushing, then, signifies both, because both are in the day of judgment, when all who are in the tombs will hear the voice of the Son of God and will come forth, those who have done good to the resurrection of life, those who have done evil to the resurrection of judgment. This David the prophet also signifies, saying: There were pains of a woman in labour. As we have heard, so also have we seen in the city of the Lord of hosts, in the city of our God. For he takes in both the pain to come and the joy: pain from the judgment, joy from the absolution.

Chapter VI

16. But let us return to the sequence of the interrogation set before us. Many he says are the crushings he has wrought upon me. He does not let me breathe, he has filled me with bitterness; and because he is mighty in power, no one can resist his judgment. If I am just in heart, my tongue errs. Wrath destroys the great and mighty man. The wicked are in grievous death, but the just are mocked; for they have been given into the hands of the ungodly. Look at each point. To the powerful their own wrath is grievous, to the wicked their wickedness, to the just the weakness of their condition. So nothing is free of danger. The strength and greatness of a man is deceived by its own power, wickedness is afflicted, virtue is mocked. The one, because he can do more, slips; the other, because he can do nothing, is afflicted. It is a defect of our condition, because our life is swifter than a runner; it has passed by and has seen nothing. Like the track of a ship or of an eagle flying, seeking food, so too the life of man passes by. What we speak we forget, and no mark of our passing is found except what is full of grief and groaning. I am shaken he says in all my limbs. Would that there were a mediator for us, both reproving and judging between the two of us.

17. I will say to the Lord: Why do you judge me thus? Or is it good to you that I should be unjust, because you have cast off the works of your hands and given heed to the counsel of the ungodly? Do you see as a mortal sees? Or is your life as a man's, or your years as a man's, that you have searched out my iniquities and tracked down my sins? For you know that I have not acted ungodly; but who is there to rescue from your hands? Great faith, great authority of conscience, to summon God as witness of one's own mind! What belongs to the condition he does not deny, what belongs to ungodliness he repels, what belongs to weakness he confesses. To have sinned belongs to the condition, because no one is immune from falling; to act ungodly belongs not to the condition, but is the poison of faithlessness and of a most wicked mind. This the just man does not acknowledge; but the absolution of man lies in God's compassion, not in man's power.

18. Your hands he says have fashioned me: afterwards you turned and struck me. Remember that you made me of clay and will dissolve me into the earth. Did you not milk me like milk, and curdle me like cheese? With skin and flesh you clothed me, with bones and sinews you knit me together; and life and mercy you set in me, and your visitation has protected my spirit. How great is the holy man's lament for our common weakness, how great the authority of his plea, that God made man with his own hands? Guilt is excused under the plea of weakness; grace is commended by the privilege of an eternal working and by the condescension of a heavenly protection. On this passage David too spoke most beautifully, saying: What is man that you are mindful of him, or the son of man, unless that you visit him?

19. These things he says having in you, I know that you can do all things, and nothing is impossible to you. For if I sin, you will guard me, but you have not made me spotless from iniquity. If I am ungodly, woe is me! If I am just, I cannot lift myself up; for I am full of confusion. I am hunted like a lion for the slaughter. Look at three things: If I sin, he says, you will guard me: and therefore, O man, confess your sin, that you may obtain pardon. Tell your iniquities, that you may be justified. Why do you blush to confess those in which you were born? To deny what you were born in is the crime of one who denies, not of one who confesses. Would that you kept what you have received! Why should you think you have what you have not received? Let the sinner, then, confess, let the ungodly man groan, let the just man not lift himself up and exalt himself, lest through arrogance he lose the fruit of justice.

20. And beautifully he says: If I am just, I cannot lift myself up; for I am full of confusion. For the just man notices his own frailty more than in ... ; the wise man acknowledges it, the fool does not. In fact, the wise man is pierced by his own falls, the fool delights in them: the just man is the accuser of himself, the unjust man his defender; the just man wishes to forestall his accuser by confession of sin, the unjust man desires to hide his sin. The former comes forward at the very beginning of his speech to disclose his error; the latter, by the wordiness of his speech, wraps the accusation in sleep, so as not to disclose his error.

Chapter VII

21. Again, adding further, he says: Why have you written evil things against me and laid upon me the sins of my youth? Finely has he seized upon that age for his complaint which is usually the most slippery toward vice. For childhood has innocence, old age prudence, and young manhood itself, the neighbour of youth, has regard for a good name and shame at doing wrong: youth alone is powerless in strength, weak in counsel, hot with vice, scornful of those who admonish it, easily enticed by pleasures. In so great, then, and so rough and stormy a whirlwind of this world, why are such frequent shipwrecks ascribed to an improvident age? Hence David excellently asked the Lord for pardon for the whole of that time, saying: Remember not the offence of my youth and of my ignorance, because then above all the heat of the body boils and is set on fire by the surge of steaming blood.

22. But let us hear him again. A mortal he says born of woman is of short life and full of wrath; like a flower he has blossomed and fallen, and he departs like a shadow and does not stay. Is not an account required of this one too, and have you made him enter into judgment in your sight? But who is clean from filth? No one, even if his life upon the earth be of one day. Truly a wretched condition, that he should be forced to render an account of his sin, which he cannot avoid, that he is compelled to enter into judgment, to come into the sight of the almighty Lord, to set forth the causes of his deeds, which he has run through in so many ages of his life, when no one can be clean from sin, so that from the very cradle guilt creeps upon infancy before there is any sense of error. And how wretched this too, that his life is short, its allurement sweet, its tribulation manifold, its wrath daily. Thus in a slight delight there is perpetual bitterness.

23. There is he says hope for a tree; for if it is cut down, it will grow green again, and if its branch has died on the rock, at the scent of water it will flower, and it will make a harvest like a young plant. But a man who has died has gone away, and mortal man, when he falls, is no more. But in its season the sea surges. We have seen the sequence of the prophetic discourse drawn from two lower elements, earth and sea, which are subject to every injury and to frequent storms. How strong an argument of our tribulation has he expressed! Earthly shrubs, he says, and the woods of trees, even when they have died, rise again to living uses. The sea itself, too, is wont to surge by the changes of the seasons. But our flesh is ever seething and is a storm to itself, and never has respite from the motions of tempests and from pitiable shipwrecks.

24. But when he has fallen asleep, he will not rise again until the heaven is sewn up. This seems to declare: until the heaven is renewed; for there will be a new heaven and a new earth, as it is written. For what is sewn on is old; what is old will be changed. Indeed, hear him saying: In the beginning you founded the earth, O Lord, and the heavens are the works of your hands. They will perish, but you remain, and all will grow old like a garment, and like a covering you will change them, and they will be changed. We can also weave in this point: that what is old is sewn on, what is new is forced. But from the days of John the Baptist the kingdom of heaven is taken by force, and those who use force seize it. So the synagogue was sewing it on in a few, the Church takes it by force in thousands; or else it is because the heaven now seems to be sewn together, often woven of a varied and many-coloured appearance from clouds and gloom and the darkness of night and the saffron blush of the rising day — but then there will be night no more, and they will not need the light of a lamp and the light of the sun, because the Lord will shine upon them, as John said — or because woe to those who sew pillows to overturn the souls of the people.

25. To the prophet lamenting the misery of our frailty, which both had no rest in this life and would lose everything at the sudden onset of death, the Holy Spirit imparted that men would not rise again until he should come who would not sew old things onto new nor join a new garment to an old garment, but would make all things new, as he himself said: Behold, I will make new things. For he himself is the resurrection, the firstborn from the dead, in whom we have all indeed received the prerogative of the future resurrection; yet he alone, as yet, rose with a perpetual resurrection.

Chapter VIII

26. Having heard, then, what God had spoken in him, and having learned through the Holy Spirit that the Son of God would not only come to earth but would also descend to the dead to raise the dead to life — which was indeed done then as a testimony for those present and an example for those to come — he turned to the Lord and said: Would that you kept me in the underworld, and hid me until your wrath ceases, and set me a time in which you would make remembrance of me. For if a man has died, he will live, completing the days of his life. I will wait until I come to be again. Then you will call me, and I will hearken to you; but do not despise the works of your hands. You have numbered my devices, and nothing of my sins will pass you by. And you have sealed up my iniquities in a bag and have marked whatever I have passed over unknowingly. How sweet a passage, which strengthens us concerning the resurrection, and how well it seems to agree with the Lord's saying that is read in the Gospel, where he says: Then they will begin to say to the mountains: Fall upon us, and to the hills: Cover us. For at the consummation of the world there will be the wrath of the Lord. Rightly, then, does the holy man prefer to rise again into the judgment rather than into the time of the divine wrath, which is terrible even to the innocent.

27. At the same time he is understood also to prophesy this, by saying: You will set me a time in which you will make remembrance of me: that he was to be raised up at the Lord's passion, as is shown at the end of this book. And yet he does not cease to lament, and the more he understands that resurrection is set before him, the more he longs to flee this life, seeing himself delivered into the hands of his adversaries, cast forth into the power of the ungodly, he whose very friends had turned into enemies, who, when they ought to have consoled him, heaped ruin upon ruin. Yet mindful of his pure conscience and of his clean prayer, he says: Earth, do not cover over in the blood of my flesh, so that his prayer may be directed like incense to the Lord and not be turned aside into the earth. For the prayer of a holy man pierces the clouds; the prayer of the sinner the earth, opening its mouth, hides in the blood of the flesh, as was said by God to Cain the parricide: Cursed he says are you from the earth, which opened its mouth to receive your brother's blood from your hand, since you will till the earth.

Chapter IX

28. And so the holy man tearfully laments the seasons of this life. I perish he says and am carried about by the wind; I ask for burial and do not obtain it. I pray in my toil. And what shall I do? My days have passed in horror; the joints of my heart are torn apart. And yet nowhere does he disparage the judgment of God; for he knows that deep is the height of the wisdom and knowledge of God, and that his judgments are unsearchable and his ways past finding out.

29. The sons of the boastful he says have not trodden them, nor has the lion passed through them. For who has been able to comprehend his ways, who has penetrated his secrets in their hiding? Whence he says has wisdom been found, or what is the place of discipline? Mortal man does not know its way, nor has it been found among men. The abyss said: It is not in me; and the sea said: It is not with me. It is not permitted to you, O man, to know the heights of wisdom; therefore it is written for you: Do not be high-minded, but fear. Why do you curiously desire to search out what it is not expedient for you to know, nor given you to learn? Paul heard certain secrets of wisdom which he was forbidden to make known to others, and therefore he was caught up into paradise, caught up even to the third heaven, that he might hear the things which, placed on earth, he could not hear. If the things that a man heard he was not permitted to speak, how does he inquire into what he has not heard? You are not permitted to know the counsels of this emperor on earth, and you want to know the divine counsels? You are not permitted to search too curiously into what is done on earth, and do you inquire too curiously what is done above the heaven? Why do you dispute whence wisdom was born? Man does not know its way, nor has perfect wisdom been found among men. It was not in Moses, not in Aaron, not in Joshua son of Nun, not in David himself, who says: You have made manifest to me the uncertain and hidden things of your wisdom, since he himself said in later verses: I became like a beast of burden before you. To know the height of wisdom is above you, O man; it is enough for you to believe. For if you do not believe, he says, neither will you understand. You cannot know the abyss, you cannot comprehend the abyss; how will you comprehend the height of wisdom? The abyss said: It is not in me, and can you say that wisdom is in you?

30. The abyss, then, said: It is not in me, because the Lord himself said: You will not leave my soul in the underworld. And the Apostle said: Who has descended into the abyss? That is, to bring Christ up from the dead. So if the abyss is asked: Where is wisdom? it answers: It is not in me, because he has risen. If the sea is asked: Where is wisdom? it says: It is not with me, because he trod upon me, and my waves could not trouble it. You too, then, set in this strait of the present world, do not seek that perfect wisdom of God in this world, because the world did not know it. But if you wish to find it, tread upon the waves of this world, as Peter trod upon them, and walk upon the waters of this age. Wisdom will stretch out her right hand to you, as she stretched it out to Peter too, for there has been no one whom the wave of this world has not troubled. It troubled Abraham too, it troubled Moses too, it troubled Peter too. Moses crossed through the sea and led an army on foot through the sea, but first he himself was troubled. Peter trod upon the waters, but he had sunk in body, because he limped with the footstep of a weaker faith. Do not, therefore, seek wisdom in the sea, because the Lord Jesus did not say that he would be with the sea, but with his apostles, that they might know him in some part; to them he says: Behold, I am with you until the consummation of the world. Blessed are they with whom he is: would that he were with us too, but the sea is with us. Peter was with Christ, because he too trod upon the sea: to us gold and silver are dear to the heart. But wisdom is above gold, not in gold. Therefore he who desired to have wisdom: Silver he says and gold I have not, but what I have I give you. In the name of Jesus Christ the Nazarene, rise and walk. Because he did not have gold, he had in the name of Christ the grace of devout working. Therefore to you too it is said: Draw wisdom into the inner places. It has lain hidden he says from every man, and was concealed from the birds of heaven. Neither men nor angels knew where it was, for they are the birds of heaven, of whom it is said: And I saw an angel flying through heaven.

31. No one could know wisdom, because no one knows the Son except the Father, and no one knows the Father except the Son and he to whom the Son wills to reveal him. He himself, then, revealed it to John, with whom wisdom was, and therefore John spoke not what was his own, but what wisdom poured into him: In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God. Destruction does not know wisdom, wickedness does not know it. For destruction could not hold her who said: Where, O death, is your victory? Where, O death, is your sting? Wickedness does not know her, because she said: The wicked will seek me and will not find me. They can say: we have heard its glory. God alone is the one who knows it. For God he says has well established its way; he knows its place. What the dwelling of wisdom is, hear his disciple saying: The only-begotten Son, who is in the bosom of the Father, he has declared him. For the Son knew the Father, since he himself says: As the Father knows me, I also know the Father. There is an equal measure of knowledge where there is a unity of power. The Father, then, who knows all things, the weight of the winds, the measure of the waters, himself saw wisdom and declared it through his prophets, since the Father declared wisdom and searched it out, as the Son too declared the Father, whom nothing escapes, and he said: O man, why do you wish to know the depths of wisdom, which are above you? To fear God is wisdom, and to abstain from evils is discipline.

Book Two. On the Interrogation of Holy Job

Chapter I

1. Our previous discussion was on the interrogation of the saints: that the human condition is frail and feeble, having firmness of its own nowhere except in the protection of heaven. Today we must take up this point: that the common run of men, and many even of the prudent, are greatly moved when they see the unjust abounding in prosperity, while the just are frequently afflicted in this world. And truly this is a slippery place, in which even the saints could scarcely keep the footing of true opinion. Indeed, David himself was troubled, who had said earlier: You have made manifest to me the hidden things of your wisdom. But afterwards he strengthened himself and searched out the way of perfect reason. Holy Job too wrestled in discourse over that opinion with those three old friends who had come to console him. Let us, then, bring into the open the discussions of both of them. For they are worthy to bring us a teaching for living, since, placed in adversity, they merited to please God the more. Let us, then, hear each in his own order.

2. Eliphaz, king of the Temanites, Bildad, tyrant of the Sauchaeans, and Zophar, king of the Minaeans, had vehemently rebuked holy Job, on the ground that he was enduring so much punishment because of his sins. For with their weak wit they did not notice that the Lord had given him over to be tempted, so that the athlete of Christ, trained by temptations, might come to the crown with greater glory. So, not seeing so great a mystery of wisdom, from the fear of a narrow heart, lest they seem to accuse of injustice God, who allowed an innocent man to be afflicted with punishments, they turned the desert of the punishments back upon holy Job, saying that all the life of the ungodly is in anxiety, and riches unjustly gathered will be vomited up; that whatever grievous things a man suffers on earth he suffers because of his sins; that even if he is in prosperity, his good fortune cannot be made lasting for him, and it quickly vanishes like a dream, so that his place is not found; that the joy of the ungodly is a heavier ruin, and that therefore holy Job too had been changed from prosperity into adversity and, at the price of his offences, had fallen from the highest to the lowest; that he who asserted that he was innocent was slipping, since the portion of the ungodly man is such that the indignation coming upon him from the Lord snows sorrows upon him and destruction enwraps his house.

Chapter II

3. Holy Job heard these things and, like a strong athlete, sitting on the dunghill, amid such great weals and the pains of a savage wound, his whole body covered with dreadful sores, he spoke mysteries, and he gave himself not to seeking remedies for his own sickness, but to sacred discourses. And so the words of the sick man were stronger than those of the men who were not sick. For they spoke unjust things, but not according to knowledge: they proclaimed the divine judgments, the punishments of the guilty, the rewards of the saints, but they did not know how to distinguish the guilty from the just. Indeed, the man whom the Lord God pronounced just, they condemned for injustice and accused of iniquity. They did not know, then, what befitted each man. But the holy man Job discerned in the spirit how he ought to speak to each; he was stronger, then, than those who seemed sound and unharmed. And why do I say that he was found stronger than the rest? He was found stronger than himself; for Job sick was stronger than he had been when well, according to what is written: power is made perfect in weaknesses. So Job too, when he was weak, was then the stronger. For he was not sick in mind, though he suffered pain in body, because his soul was not in the flesh, to whose sufferings it did not cling, but in the spirit, with whose strength he had covered himself.

4. Therefore he spoke, not the groans of the flesh and the weaknesses of the body, but the voices of the spirit, with which to press on, not to give way. And at first indeed more gently, to strike them with shame, because they pressed the just man unjustly, saying that he was enduring punishments smaller than his sins, and, sinners themselves, they did not blush to accuse an innocent man with falsehoods. 'Grant' he says 'that I have erred, and that the one who strays from the way dwells with me, he who pours errors over the minds of men, so that I speak words that I ought not, as you say, and my words go astray and my speech is not brought forth fittingly: why do you leap upon me and revile me, not considering that this temptation has come to me from the Lord, who judged that I should be hedged about with a kind of rampart of troubles? I am exercised by adversities, walled about on every side with toils and dangers: and still you insult me, wishing to crush the one whom you ought to help. Behold, I laugh at reproaches, and I will not speak nor answer your revilings. For it is not you who judge, but he who judges me is the Lord, and yet the time of his judgment has not yet come. What need is there to cry out before the judgment? It is good to be silent while he who judges is awaited, it is good not to return reviling for reviling, lest we too be numbered among the detractors.'

5. Let us imitate, then, this man, who by his silence refuted those who reviled him. For he showed the virtue of his mind, which insults did not move, and he made manifest the innocence of his conscience, since he did not recognize the charges but laughed at them as foreign to him. But we, as though something were being charged against us, make it more bitter while we wish to clear ourselves: while we desire to avenge ourselves, we confess it, although Scripture says that you should turn away the word of disgrace and take away your garment; for the injurious man passes by. Let us be silent, then, so that he may pass by, lest, provoked, he burn our garment; for it is written: Do not kindle the coals of the sinner, lest perhaps you be burned in the fire of his flame. For this reason, then, the holy man is silent, even if a slave is insolent; even if a poor man reviles, the just man is silent; even if a sinner hurls reproaches, the just man laughs; even if a weak man curses, the just man blesses.

6. David was silent when Gera the son of Shimei cursed him; Job laughed; Paul blessed, as he himself says: We are cursed, and we bless. For under divine teaching the progress of human virtue increased, since he had already come who would make men stronger out of the weak, and Paul had heard him saying: Bless those who curse you, and pray for those who slander you. What he said in word he also proved by example. Indeed, when he was set on the cross, he said of his persecutors who were reviling him: Father, forgive them, for they do not know what they do, so that he prayed for those who slandered him, whom he himself could forgive. Job, then, laughed, because Christ had not yet come, for whom alone the prerogative of the great virtues was reserved. For he himself is the beginning of virtues, as he said: The Lord created me the beginning of his ways.

Chapter III

7. And laughing, he was silent. He teaches for what reason one must be silent: I will cry out, and there is not yet judgment. 'He himself' he says 'willed that I should suffer these things; shut in as by walls of temptations, I cannot flee until it pleases God to destroy the heights of my temptations. For now, if I cry out, there is not yet judgment. I am still in the contest, still I wrestle, still a contest remains for me; for the crown has not yet come forth; and no one is crowned unless he has contended lawfully'.

8. A third contest was owed him. He had lost all that was his, that is, his patrimony together with his sons; his flesh was suffering wounds: it remained for him to overcome the temptations of words. No mean contest. Adam was deceived by speech, Samson conquered by a word; for nothing so penetrates the soul as painted speech, and again nothing bites so as harsher speech. Many, when they had overcome the torments applied to them, have not endured the harshness of words. Job toiled, but he endured, and bore the burdens of words alongside those of his wounds. His president of the games saw him; from the cloud and the whirlwind he gave a hand to him in his toil, and declared that his opponents had fallen with a heavy fall, proclaimed him victor, bestowed the crown.

9. But what is finer than to laugh when we are cursed? For we ought to rejoice if things that are not ours are said of us. First, because the enemy, wishing to say something against us by which to fasten a charge on us, did not find what is true, but composed falsehoods in place of truths. Next, because the Lord himself said in the Gospel, of such an accusation, brought falsely against the innocent for justice's sake: Rejoice and exult, because your reward is great in heaven. He who recognizes the charge, then, ought to be silent, lest he aggravate the wound and the scar be torn open; and he who does not recognize it ought to be silent too; for he hears another's crime, not his own. If he answers back, he makes it his own; if he is silent, he turns it back and wounds the reviler. He too ought to be silent who relies on the reward just mentioned; for he suffers no prejudgment who is subject to no judgment. And if there has been a prejudgment in the world, there will be none in the judgment of God. Indeed, that you may know that insult does not prejudge a good conscience, hear holy Job speaking, a richer witness surely than if he had gained the empire of the Roman world: Now I will be dumb and give up: if now there is prejudgment against me, then I shall not be hidden from your face.

10. These things, then, he answered more sparingly at the beginning, to remind them of the judgment of God and, recalling them from insolence and fury, to bring them to their senses like a good physician. But after he noticed that they persisted in their insults, he went on to more and stronger things, and struck them as with a more powerful fist, since they were stoning an innocent man with the stones of their words. Hear he says, hear my words; let it not be mine to seek consolation from you: bear with me, for I shall speak stronger things. The weight of my words will be heavy. I too will speak according to your opinion, because many abound in this world in the successes of prosperity, and others are weighed down and set in tribulation: and this seems to be dealt out according to the desert of sins. Even if I say this, do not laugh as though I had agreed with you. Even if I am a sinner, I am not guilty before man, because he too who judges, and claims for himself power over me, is under sin. Or if I am judged as a man, that is common to all. I ought not to be rebuked: it is the weakness of our condition, not a particular wickedness.

Chapter IV

11. But tell me: if I suffer this because of my sin, as you charge, why do the ungodly live, and not only live, but are also filled with riches and multiply their fruits, and moreover enjoy sons, and their houses abound? These things are evidently good in appearance, but by a deeper mystery you will find that the things thought good are not good, and the things thought evil are esteemed better than they.

12. They have grown old, he says, in riches. He said πεπαλαίωνται, so that what is signified seems to be not so much a long possession of riches as the grown-old trouble of plenty, just as Ecclesiastes saw that riches are kept to the harm of the one who possesses them, riches which perish amid the utmost strain and anxiety. For the things that are left here perish, and they cannot profit the dead man. So the deceased had anxiety from them; he could not find rest, he who left behind what he would blush at and did not take with him what he might hold, far unlike the one of whom it is written: Blessed is the man who has filled his desire from these; he will not be put to shame when he speaks to his enemies in the gate. He whose inheritance is the Lord, and whose reward is from the childbearing of the Virgin Mary, is proclaimed at his departure with the praises of wisdom, since he had nothing to blush at, he who coveted nothing of the things that belong to the world, but, having put off the cast-off skin of the old man, wounded the adversary with the dart of continence, so that at the end of this life the adversary could not clamour against him, lamed by the wound and confounded by admiration of his virtues. You have it, then, that he is not praiseworthy who has grown old in the desire for money and has not been made new in the receiving of grace.

13. Let us look at another point: Their seed, he says, is according to the soul, that is, they are not counted among the just. For the just sow in the spirit and from the spirit will reap eternal life, but those who sow according to the soul cannot reap spiritual things, because the natural (soul-ish) man does not perceive the things that are of the Spirit of God; for it is foolishness to him, and he cannot know the things that are of the Spirit, and therefore he is not filled with spiritual things, but is judged.

14. Their sons are in their eyes, that is, what they do they do in order to be seen by men, not because, being good, they take counsel so as to choose what will be approved at the judgment to come. So Scripture frequently speaks of sons in place of works, because our posterity is richer in good deeds than in sons. Hence Hezekiah too, freed from a grave sickness, From this day, he says, I will make sons, who will proclaim your justice, O Lord of my salvation, and I will not cease blessing you with the psaltery all the days of my life; for good is the posterity of devotion and of faith, which did not know how to succumb to the captivity that the sons of Hezekiah endured.

15. And he added, saying that they have no fear, there is no scourge from the Lord. But the just man says: For I have been scourged all the day, and he desires to be scourged, that he may be received by the Lord, and he wishes to fear God, because the fear of the Lord is the beginning of wisdom. Nor does he think it blessedness if his heifer does not miscarry, as fools think. For what is signified by the ox but the toil of rural husbandry, which always comes round again in a circle and never ceases, but, when it seems to be completed, is called back to its beginning? These are they who till Sodom and Gomorrah. So those who till Egypt, and turn over the clods of the earth with a kind of plough of a solid mind, beget toil for themselves and reap sorrows. Therefore their heifer does not miscarry, but gives birth, that their toil may be increased, and all that they have conceived they bring forth without the fear of God. But the just glory far otherwise; for they glory not in an abundance of riches nor in the births of cattle, but in the Lord, saying: From your fear we have conceived in the womb, and have travailed with the spirit of salvation. So it is said of the just that they have brought forth the spirit of salvation, which they received from the fear of God, not from the malice of this world, of which we read: Behold, he has travailed with injustice, and conceived toil, and brought forth iniquity. So the miscarriage is better than the childbearing of worldly men. Indeed, concerning the man who came into this world and endured the vanity and the darkness of this world through a long old age, Ecclesiastes declared that a miscarriage is better than he. For this one has rest rather than that one, because he did not experience the changes of the world, in which, even if someone has lived a thousand years, he could not see what is good. So to have escaped these things is more a grace than to have undergone them.

16. But perhaps this may trouble you, that he added that they remain like eternal sheep, and their children play, taking up the psaltery and the lyre, and they delight in the voice of the psalm. And they have ended their life in good things, and in the rest of the underworld they have fallen asleep. Distinguish these things, and since you are spiritual, judge between them. The ungodly are like the eternal, not eternal, because they cannot receive eternity from him who is not eternal. For he cannot give what he does not have, nor can he enlighten who does not possess light; but he transforms himself into an angel of light, to deceive the unbelieving. And he transforms himself by the pretence of a false light, not by the splendour of everlasting brightness. Hence the Saviour too says: I saw Satan like lightning falling from heaven. He is not lightning, but like lightning. Consider some heretic intent on abstinence of the body and on the knowledge of the heavenly mysteries: he is thought to be like one eternal, but he does not have the wage of eternal life, because he who does not have the truth of faith has a false imitation. His little ones play, like that woman who, when she has grown wanton, wishes to marry. She has grown wanton in the psaltery and the lyre, that is, in the sound of the voice, not in the depth of the mysteries, so that it resounded on the lips but did not reach into the hearts.

Chapter V

17. Men of this kind, then, have ended their life in the good things of the world, this life, that is, which they were living, not that life whose reward they hoped for; and therefore they have fallen asleep in the rest of the underworld, not in the heavenly rest. But let us pray rather to undergo toil here, that in the kingdom of heaven we may deserve to obtain the consolation of eternal quiet. For the abundance of prosperity is a great enticement to offend: it lifts one up into pride, it pours in forgetfulness of the Author. Consider that rich man in the Gospel reclining on crimson and purple, from whose table that just poor man Lazarus gathered the crumbs: does not that rich man seem to you to say to God: Depart from me; I do not wish to know your ways? And truly men of this kind do not wish to know the ways of the Lord; for if they wished, they would know them, but because they are full of toil, they are shunned and avoided by the lost. So, like a drunken man, he does not recognize the Author of salvation. Then, turning to his drinking companions, Let us eat, he says, and drink. What does it profit if we serve him? Or what advantage is there if we observe him? These things, then, says the man drunk with the abundance of worldly things, because the deserts of crimes are not repaid at once in the world; for he lay back at his ease, because everything was at hand for his pleasure, and, conscious of his ungodliness, whose punishment was being deferred, he thought that God did not see the crimes of the ungodly.

18. To this opinion of his, therefore, holy Job answered: do not be secure and careless in supposing that the scourges of the Lord do not come to you in this very world. Even the lamp of the ungodly is put out. It shines for a time; it does not have eternal light. And although the world favours them, because they do the will of him who holds sovereignty in the world, a reversal of fortune is wont to come, and sorrows from the heavenly wrath and indignation, so that they are winnowed like chaff by the wind. The unjust are winnowed like chaff, the just like wheat. Indeed, hear the Lord saying to Peter: Behold, Satan has asked to winnow you like wheat; but I have prayed for you, that your faith may not fail. Those fail who are winnowed like chaff; he does not fail who is after the likeness of that grain which fell and rose again, heaped up with the increase of many fruits. Therefore the prophet says: Woe is me, for I have become like one who gathers stubble in the harvest! So ungodliness is compared to stubble, which is quickly burnt up, and to dust. Therefore, after he had said: They will be like chaff by the wind, he at once added a little verse, saying: or like dust, which the wind has carried away. Indeed, that you may know that the ungodly man quickly crumbles and vanishes like dust, you have it said in the first psalm: Not so the ungodly, not so; that is, not like the just, but like dust, which the wind drives from the face of the earth.

19. And he makes a distinction between the just man and the ungodly. This one, he says, dies in the strength of his simplicity, wholly in abundance and grace, and his inward parts are full of fat, their marrow overflows. But that one finishes the course of this life in bitterness of soul, and, having feasted on nothing good, receives his end. What can be rendered to him worthy of his deserts? He is brought into the sepulchres and keeps watch in his own tomb. Not even this is a moderate punishment, not to have the rest of death, to be carried not to the land of the living, but to the tombs of the dead. For he who lives is not sought among the dead, but in the bosom of Abraham enjoys eternal life. And therefore those two men in shining garments said to the women: Why do you seek the living with the dead?

20. Then he enumerates the crimes of the ungodly: that they have passed over the boundaries, and plundered the flock together with the shepherd, led away the orphan's yoke-beast, taken the widow's ox in pledge, reaped a field not their own, while the weak have worked in their vineyards without wage and food; that, stripping off their clothes, they have made many sleep naked, whose soul's covering they have taken away. These were wet with the drippings of the mountains, and, because a covering was lacking, they covered themselves with the rock. Orphans were snatched from their mothers' breasts; those who had fallen, who ought rather to have been raised up, were crushed. The hungry were cheated of food; the soul of the little children groaned heavily.

21. Could he be ignorant of these things, whom nothing escapes? The underworld is naked in his sight, and there is no covering for the most wicked, because they cannot lie hidden. He stretches out the north over nothing, he hangs the earth upon nothing, binding up the water in his clouds, and the cloud is not rent beneath his feet. The pillars of heaven have leapt apart and have trembled at his rebuke. By his power he has calmed the sea, and by discipline he has laid low the great fish of the sea. The bars of heaven dread him: by his command he put to death the apostate dragon. Who understands the power of his thunder? Amid such great outrages, then, what hope is there for the ungodly man? If he trusts in the Lord, will he be saved? I will tell you, he says, what is in the hand of the Lord, and cease to add vain things to vain.

22. And he describes how wretched is the portion of the ungodly: that even if they have many sons, they are without posterity, since they lack a succession of good deserts; for that is the true posterity which is not on earth but in heaven. For men of this kind, then, want is their inheritance and death their succession. When they have heaped up riches, they will beg, because when they are dead they will be in need, since they cannot find rest. No one will pity their widows, but they will remain forsaken and bereft of all the comfort of wedlock. Even if money is gathered like earth, and gold is made ready like clay, their substance will be empty like a spider's web, and all the patrimony of their name will be consumed as by moths. The rich man, sleeping, will add nothing; he has opened his eyes, and he is no more. He remains in sorrows. All the things, then, that are in this world are nothing. Gold is in the mines, silver is in the mines: it is dug out of the mine and returns into the mine. For what else is the mind of the miser but a mine? It keeps whatever it has received as if buried, and hides it in the veins and hiding places of the earth, because it does not know how to use it. Every day gold is brought out of the mines: who has ever been able to bring anything out of a miser?

23. Since, then, the vain hunger for gold profits nothing, because whatever it has gathered slips away, surely those are to be pitied who have forsaken the just way and have forgotten it, the way to which precious stones are by no means to be compared. It is hard to search out, impassable to the proud, shut to the boastful, level for the humbler, open to the wise. And therefore we ought to seek wisdom, that we may walk in the just way, through which that adversary, who like a ravening and roaring lion ranges through this world, could not pass. But let him who wishes to search out wisdom not seek it in the abyss, like the philosophers, who think that they themselves, of their own accord and by their own ingenuity, can know its depths; let him not look for it in the sea — for where there is a tempest, where there is a storm of wind, wisdom cannot be there — but let him seek it there where there is tranquillity of mind and the peace which is above all understanding.

Book Three. On the Interrogation of David

Chapter I

1. The interrogation of holy Job has been run through: now let us take up that interrogation which we have found in the Psalms. David himself, indeed, has in many places not kept silent about the vanity of the world, and has frequently asserted that the things thought to be the good things of this world are empty, and above all in Psalm 38 (39), in which he says: Nevertheless all things are vanity, every man living. Although in the image of God a man walks, yet he is troubled in vain; he lays up treasure and does not know for whom he gathers it, and elsewhere: How long shall sinners, O Lord, how long shall sinners glory? For here they have a shadowy glory; when they have departed from the world, they cannot find the fruit of consolation. Yet the same man inserted Psalm 72 (73), in which he himself, under the name of Asaph, bears witness that at first he was on the point of slipping, so that he was gripped by no moderate sorrow when he saw sinners in this world overflowing with riches and abounding in prosperity, while he himself, who had justified his heart, was in afflictions and tribulations; and that he had incurred no slight stumbling at the outset, but afterwards, corrected by the scourges of the Lord and enlightened by the grace of divine knowledge, had learned the sequence of the true teaching.

2. But nowhere do I find that holy Asaph was harassed by any adversities, whereas holy David endured very many grievous things, full of dangers; for he speaks of his own toils. Hence too the psalm is inscribed, as the title shows, not as being of holy Asaph, but as being for holy Asaph, which is shown more plainly from the Greek psalter, so that David seems to have given to this Asaph too, as to others, the psalm which he himself had written, for him to sing. But since it is written in the title itself that the psalms of David have come to an end — and how have they come to an end, when, after these ten psalms have been run through, the inscription of the titles afterwards includes psalms of David right up to the very end? — therefore, setting aside a definition of this kind, let us consider what the sequence of the psalm is, and let us take our beginning from the first little verse of the prophetic interrogation.

Chapter II

3. How good is God to Israel, to the upright of heart! At the very beginning the progress of his amendment shines out. For no one can truly confess that God is good except him who acknowledges his goodness not from the successes of his own fortunes, but from the depth of the heavenly mysteries and the height of the divine ordering; and that goodness must be weighed not by the appearance of present things but by the advantage of things to come. To the just man, therefore, God is always good, and when he is racked by pains of the body and when he is afflicted by the bitterness of punishments, he always says: If we have received good things from the hand of the Lord, why do we not bear the things that are evil? He rejoices that he is crushed here, so that in the future he may find consolation, knowing that he who has received good things in this life has his reward. He will not be able to hope for future rewards who has not wrestled, who has not been exercised in the contest of various battles. But he who, whether justly or unjustly, is afflicted in this world rejoices, either because he pays here the price of his offences, or because he knows that there is a richer grace with the Lord if he suffers bitter things unjustly for His name or for some good work, according as it is written that it is no glory if, when you sin, you are punished and endure it; but if, doing good, you also suffer, this is grace with God. For to this you were called, because Christ too died for you, leaving you an example, that you should follow his footsteps, who did no sin, nor was deceit found in his mouth: who, when he was reviled, did not revile in return; when he suffered, did not threaten. So the just man, even if he is on the rack, is always just, because he justifies God and says that he bears less than his sins deserve; he is always wise; for true and perfect wisdom is not taken away by the tortures of the rack; he does not lose what he is, because he shuts fear out by the zeal and purpose of love: as a wise man he knows how to say that the things we bear in this body are unworthy of the recompense of future glory, and that all the sufferings of this time cannot equal the reward that is to come. So God is always good to this man, who knows at what time he will reap. And therefore, like a good husbandman, here he ploughs his field with a kind of ploughshare of stricter abstinence; here he roots out with a kind of pruning hook of the virtues, which cuts away vices; here he manures, humbling himself even to the ground, knowing that God raises up the needy from the earth and lifts up the poor from the dung; indeed, the apostle Paul, had he not been reckoned as dung, could never have gained Christ for himself; here he guards his fruits, that there he may store them away in safety. So God is always good to him, because he always hopes for the things that are good from God.

4. Take another point. How good, he says, is God to Israel, to the upright of heart! Is God, then, not good to all? He is indeed good to all, because he is the Saviour of all men, especially of the faithful. And therefore the Lord Jesus came to save what had perished; for he came to take away the sin of the world, to heal our wounds. But because not all seek the remedy, but many shrink from it, lest the force of the ulcer be stung by the medicines, therefore he heals the willing and does not constrain the unwilling. So those who seek the remedy receive health, but those who reject the physician and do not seek him cannot feel the goodness of the physician, which they do not experience. But he who is treated is also healed, and therefore the physician is good to those whom he has healed. So God is good to those whose sins he has forgiven; but he who has an incurable sin in the ulcer of his mind, how can he reckon the physician good whom he shuns? Beautifully, therefore, did the Apostle explain, as we said before, that God is both good to all, who wills all men to be saved, and that the prerogative of the divine goodness is kept especially for the faithful, whom both the will of God and his grace assist. But the psalmist too, in saying: How good is God to Israel, to the upright of heart! referred to the judgment of those who know no other way to think of God than that he is good towards all things and in all.

Chapter III

5. Then in what follows he himself sets out what he felt, saying: But my feet were almost moved, my steps had well-nigh been poured out, because I was jealous of sinners, seeing the peace of sinners. He certainly does not mean the feet and steps of the body, but the direction of the mind and its step, of which he says elsewhere: Let not the foot of pride come to me, and let not the hand of sinners move me. Therefore we must always ask that the Lord direct the footsteps of our minds, lest they slip and, poured out on a kind of slipperiness of error, be unable to keep their steadiness. And the cause of his slipping is this, that he emulated the peace of sinners. But we ought to emulate the things that are good, not those that are full of disgrace, as the apostle Paul too expressed it, saying: But it is good to emulate in what is good always.

6. And do not let it trouble you that he has placed peace in evil. Indeed, in the Gospel too you have it that there is a peace which Christ rejects, as he himself says: My peace I leave you, my peace I give you: not as the world gives do I give to you. For there is a peace that has no stumbling block, and there is one that has. That which comes from love has no stumbling block; that which comes from pretence has. Therefore the prophet too says: Peace, peace; and where is peace? Let us flee, then, the peace of sinners; for they conspire against the innocent, they band together to oppress the just man, to destroy the widow or to storm her chastity.

7. And therefore there is no reclining in their death; not declining (declinatio), as most Latin manuscripts have it, but reclining (reclinatio). For when we toil and are bent over some work and stoop, we are accustomed to recline; but sinners, those namely of grave offences, and above all the ungodly, cannot recline, of whom it is said: and bow down their back always. For those who have not cleaved to Christ do not lift themselves up to heavenly things. And therefore they do not rise again with him, those whose death is very evil, as it is written: The death of sinners is very evil. But he who dies with Christ and is buried together with him not only reclines but is also raised up again. To him that saying aptly applies: You have turned all his bed in his weakness, especially if he is a martyr, whose weakness is dissolved by his passion, and his death by resurrection.

8. We have seen that rich man who, clothed in purple and fine linen, lay at table in this world and feasted splendidly every day, from whose table the poor man Lazarus gathered what fell: how, placed in torments in the underworld, he could not recline, but scarcely lifted up towards Abraham, not his whole self, but only his eyes, asking him to send Lazarus to dip the tip of his finger in water and cool his tongue. So for his death there was no reclining, nor support in his stroke.

9. For scourges profit nothing after death. And therefore David, while he was in the life of this body, made himself ready for scourges, that the Lord might receive him chastened. Consider again for me holy Job, who was covered with ulcers and shaken in all his limbs and full of pains in his whole body, dissolving the clods of earth with the matter and moisture of his wounds: how, since he could not recline while placed in this body, he found the rest of death. And therefore, conscious of himself, he said: Death is rest to a man. So he was not moved in his stroke, nor did he waver through any slip of his speech, he who in all those things did not sin with his lips, as Scripture testifies; but rather he found the support of his stroke, through which he was strengthened in Christ. So both Job and David, because they were scourged here, had support in their stroke, because the father scourges the son whom he receives; but those who are not scourged here are not received there as sons. And therefore they are not in the toils of men, and they will not be scourged with men, so that they may be scourged for ever with the devil.

Chapter IV

10. Therefore, he says, their pride has held them fast; they are covered with their iniquity and ungodliness. Iniquity is an evil garment; if anyone wishes to hold it on us, we must let it go, lest he begin to go to judgment with us. And if anyone tries to take away our spiritual tunic, which we have received, let go the cloak of iniquity, take up the covering of faith and patience with which David covered himself in fasting, so as not to lose the coverings of virtue. Fasting itself is a covering. Indeed, had not fasting sobriety covered holy Joseph, the wantonness of the adulteress would have stripped him. If Adam had been willing to cover himself with this fasting, he would not have been made naked. But because he tasted of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil against the heavenly prohibition, and transgressed the commanded fast with the food of incontinence, he knew that he was naked. But if he had fasted, he would also have kept the garments of faith and would not have seen himself uncovered. Let us not, then, clothe ourselves with iniquity and ungodliness, lest it be said of any of us: and he clothed himself with cursing. Adam too clothed himself badly, who, while he looked for coverings of leaves, received the sentence of the curse.

11. The Jews clothed themselves with cursing, of whom it is written: Their iniquity has come forth as if from fat; they have passed into the disposition of the heart. For adipale (fatty) is so called from adeps (fat), that is, rich. For just as the soul that is fed on good things and filled with virtues is filled as if with fat and fatness, as it is written, so iniquity, which proceeds as if from fat, is signified as not thin and slight, but full of vices. Indeed, they did not fall into error by some chance slip, but passed into sacrilege by counsel and disposition.

Chapter V

12. They have set their mouth against heaven, and their tongue has passed over the earth. What it is to set one's mouth in heaven, that younger one of the brothers teaches us, who returned to his father and said: Father, I have sinned against heaven and before you. And they set their mouth against heaven who think that the authorship of their crimes is assigned to them by a certain necessity of their nativity. These are wont to spare neither heaven nor earth, so that they judge that the life of man is governed by a certain course of the stars. They leave nothing to providence, nothing to good morals. And would that these too had returned like that one of the two young men: the good Lord would not have denied the remedy. And yet, even if they themselves do not wish to be healed, the Lord reserves the grace of return, so that those in Israel who were driven out through the blindness of their own heart may return through the fullness of the Church, and may not pass empty days of this life, but may have them full of good work and of faith, when the Lord has filled them with spiritual grace. And hear how they return. For blindness he says has befallen Israel in part, until the fullness of the Gentiles should come in, and so all Israel should be saved. But because the mystery had to be fulfilled, that God should shut up all things in unbelief, that is, should refute and convict them — for when two contend, if one is superior, it is said: he has shut up that other — and therefore through his mercy, that the world might be made subject to God, the people did indeed return in its heirs, but it was led astray by the error of a late crime, so as not to believe that God has foreknowledge of hidden things. But the Lord reserved for them the grace of future salvation, that they might be redeemed at some time, saying: Therefore my people will return hither. What is hither? That is, to me, to my equity and my justice, to my worship.

13. And it will fill up the days of its life. This you will certainly take in this way, that the people is indeed redeemed, the people that has believed. And in this, even if those who have not believed are not redeemed, nevertheless the prerogative of redemption is conferred on the people of God.

14. These men, then, set in error, said: How did God know? And is there all knowledge in the Most High? For they think that there is no knowledge in God, because sinners abound in worldly prosperity. And he still brings them in speaking: Behold, these are sinners, and abounding for ever they have obtained riches. You have this expressed more plainly in the Gospel, where Simon the Pharisee, seeing that that sinful woman had come into his house and poured out ointment upon the feet of Christ, said within himself: This man, if he were a prophet, would surely know who and what sort of woman this is who touches him, that she is a sinner. But the patience of God does not prejudge the truth, and his foreknowledge and providence are proved all the more by this very fact, that one who is set in sin abounds in the success of worldly prosperity. The stronger man, seeing this, laughs; the unwary man is led astray and shaken.

Chapter VI

15. In short, the psalmist says that he said: Then in vain have I justified my heart and washed my hands among the innocent, that is: I see them abounding, I see all advantages going favourably for them, but myself crushed and harried by very many temptations. In vain, then, did I give myself to innocence and apply myself to the pursuit of a sober way of life. And he says finely: I have washed my hands among the innocent, so that he might seem not to claim for himself the height of innocence, but to offer diligent pursuit of it.

16. Meanwhile he testifies that such a word did not go out from him with impunity. For he recalls that he was scourged all the day, because he had said that he had justified his heart to the Lord in vain; but after the scourges there followed at once the correction of his perverse opinion. For at once he says, my informer is in the mornings, that is, in what is open and clear; for the light of truth, laying hold of him, did not allow him to think the things that he had said. And so the light of truth poured over my mind refuted and convicted me, because I had not spoken rightly: In vain have I justified my heart. For I spoke those things as one set in darkness, and as I recalled them my heart was pricked, and when my heart was pricked my affection was enlightened, so that there came to be in my heart a flaming fire, which made in me the spiritual beginning of day. And so, when the day had grown light and risen upon me, and as though I were set in many mornings, I understood that I had come to be outside the ordering of the generation of the sons of God, I who, though at first I had believed that the maker of the world, providing for the human race, had made all things for our benefit, whether sad things or those that give little delight, afterwards, troubled by perverse opinions, lost so good a judgment.

17. And so I conferred with my heart and said to myself: If I shall tell it thus, that In vain have I justified my heart, the voice of God met me, saying: Behold, the generation of your sons, for which I have ordained, that is: behold, you find in the Scriptures that I have ordained, O you, Adam, for the generation of your sons, that riches come to the ungodly by chance, not by any merit. Nor are the gains of treasure the rewards of virtue, just as, conversely, poverty is not the punishment of sin; but these things come indiscriminately, which are rolled along like a river by a certain flood of the world.

18. And I judged, and seemed to myself to know, that this was true, that this agreed with and befitted divine providence, and that I had been troubled in vain over things on which I ought not to have dwelt.

Chapter VII

19. And so, because I seemed to myself to have grasped the true judgment and to have attained knowledge of the matter itself, I said to myself: This is toil before me, until I enter into the sanctuary of God and understand at the last, that is: only one toil remains for me, to go into the sanctuary of God, where the Cherubim are, that is, the depth of knowledge, and not to toil among uncertain and empty opinions; for the narration of a fool is like a burden on the way. Therefore let us go into the inner shrine of sacred knowledge and the inner recesses of truth, so that there may be no toil in us; for wisdom leads us away from the sense of toil. In short, there is no toil in Jacob, but the cause of toil is ignorance, since he who does not know that rewards are laid up for the just is not refreshed from his toils, but rather is bowed down and broken by the toil of his own imprudence. Let us go, then, into the sanctuary of God, where the Cherubim are, in whom is the remembrance of sacred knowledge and of that true and eternal light.

20. In that lampstand an image shines forth, by which we may understand at the last. For the holy man comes to know at the end, and attains perfect wisdom with the advance of age, saying: Make known to me, O Lord, my end, and what is the number of my days, that I may know what is lacking to me. What is the end, if not that when the kingdom is delivered up to God the Father, when the hidden things of wisdom are revealed? This end of our contest the prophet was seeking, desiring to know what was lacking to his perfection; for the end of our discipline and of our studies is perfection.

21. This, then, is the first ground of true knowledge, that the things that are in the world happen by chance; the second is this, that because of their evasions you have set for them flowing successes of worldly advantages and an abundance of riches, so that they might not plead that, because of poverty and the bitterness of some grief and mourning, they had been less devoted, and had been driven by the necessity of want into the guilt of robbery and into the pursuit of plunder. For they have not been enriched with riches or raised up with honours for tranquillity of life and the enjoyment of gladness, but so that, their complaint being shut out, their tribulation might be heaped up.

22. And so men of this kind are cast down while they are raised up. For it is not a grace but a ruin, where neither is the enjoyment of the gift kept for long, and the excuse for offending is taken away. For what complaint is of heavier weight than that divine one which you have in the book of the prophet Micah: O my people, what have I done to you, or in what have I grieved you, or how have I been troublesome to you? Answer me. Did I not lead you out of the land of Egypt and free you from the house of bondage? See how the ungodly are cast down while they are lifted up: their complaint is shut out, their punishment is heaped up. For, invited by heavenly benefits, they ought not to have deserted the giver of security and prosperity of life, whom they ought rather to have obeyed. But as the justice of God is great, so also is his vengeance severe. For when has the ungodly man ever been wont to last, of whom you have it written elsewhere too: I saw the ungodly man exalted above measure and lifted up above the cedars of Lebanon, and I passed by, and behold, he was not; and I sought him, and his place was not found? Incredible is the swiftness of his extinction. You see the ungodly man suddenly powerful in this world: while you pass by, he already is not. For how great is a shadow on the earth, how short-lived! Move your footstep, and the shadow has passed. Or if anything here moves you, lift up the footstep of your mind to the things that are to come, and you will find that there the ungodly man will not be, whom here you believed to be; for he who is nothing is not. In short, the Lord has known those who are his; but those who are not, he does not acknowledge, because they themselves have not acknowledged him who is.

Chapter VIII

23. Of these same men, then, he says here too: They have failed and perished because of their iniquity, like the dream of one rising up, that is: so the ungodly fail and vanish, like the dream of one first rising up from sleep, because they are in darkness and have walked in darkness, and no trace of any good work of theirs remains, but they are like those who see a dream. Now he who dreams dreams in the night, and the night is in darkness. The sons of darkness are deprived of the sun of justice and of the splendour of virtue, always sleeping and never waking, of whom it is well said: They have slept their sleep and have found nothing. For when their souls have been separated from the body, as though released from the sleep of the body, they will find nothing, they will hold nothing, and what they thought they held they will lose, since, when the stupid man and the fool has overflowed with riches, he will leave his riches to strangers, nor will the glory of his house go down together with him to the lower world.

24. What follows also shows how his image is not found, but perishes, since his image is not found in the city of the Lord, that Jerusalem which is above. For the Lord painted us after his image and likeness, as he himself teaches, saying: Behold, Jerusalem, I have painted your walls. If we have acted well, this heavenly image remains in us; if anyone acts badly, this image is blotted out in him, the image, that is, of him who came down from heaven, and the image of the earthly one is in him. Hence the Apostle too says: As we have borne the image of that earthly one, let us bear also the image of this heavenly one. The images of the good, then, endure and shine forth in that city of God. But if anyone has turned aside to graver sins and has not done penance, his image is abolished or cast out, as Adam was cast out and shut out of paradise. But whoever has conducted himself devoutly and honourably enters into the city of God and brings in his image, so that it may shine in that city of God. O Lord, in your city you will bring their images to nothing, because those who have clothed themselves with works of darkness cannot shine in the light. Let us fetch an example from the world. See how in cities the images of good princes endure, while the images of tyrants are blotted out.

Chapter IX

25. Considering these things, then, and applying his mind to them, the holy prophet, who had before been troubled, was delighted. Hence he himself says: For my heart was delighted and my reins were relaxed. And I was brought to nothing and did not know, and I became like a beast of burden before you. And I am always with you. 'When I had come to know,' he says, 'that God cares for human affairs and looks upon them, my reins rested,' that is: from the very great weariness of my former imprudence I have rested through the knowledge of the heavenly good and of grace. There are also certain reins of the soul, which are vexed in us through the toil of ignorance: these are relaxed so as to rest by the knowledge of heavenly doctrine, and, supported as it were by a kind of prop of heavenly precepts, they are cherished. 'Then,' he says, 'I understood that I was wearying myself in vain, since I did not know what is true.'

26. And I became like a beast of burden. He finely added before you; for in comparison with heavenly things, what is man but a beast of burden without reason? For the stars too, though they are bright, vanish at the rising of the sun. And Moses says: I have not been fit since yesterday, since you began to speak with your servant. And I am thin he says of voice and slow of speech. So then man too, in comparison, I will not say with Christ, but with the angels, seems to be a dumb beast of burden. But still let no one despair, because the Lord saves men and beasts of burden. And therefore, because I have learned not from myself but from you, I will cleave to you always, that I may cease to be a beast of burden, and that you may say to me: But you, stand here with me. For, surrounded by the grace of God, he begins to be a man who through imprudence displayed a beastly insensibility and ignorance; for man is proved to be man by this, if he is capable of reason and of grace, and so he rejoices that he has been separated from the dumb animals and admitted into the fellowship of men, which God visits and protects. For what is man, except that the Lord is mindful of him, or that he is visited by the Lord?

Chapter X

27. Hence, as one visited by him, he says: You have held my right hand, and in your will you have led me, and with glory you have taken me up. So we have received it, and according to the Greek it agrees so. For the Greek said: ἐκράτησας τῆς χειρός, that is, you have held the hand, τῆς δεξιᾶς μου, my right hand. He is well directed whose right hand God holds with his own hand. He can say: The Lord is at my right hand, that I may not be moved. If Adam had been willing to have the Lord at his right hand, he would not have been deceived by the serpent. But because he forgot the commandment of God and fulfilled the will of the serpent, the devil held his hand and made it stretch out to the tree of the knowledge of evil and good, so that he plucked what was forbidden. In him judgment was passed beforehand on all, and the adversary began to stand at the right hand of all. Hence also that form of curse went forth against Judas: And let the devil stand at his right hand. If that curse is heavy, this blessing is very great, by which the bonds of the harsh curse are loosed. Therefore the Lord Jesus set the devil at his own right hand, as we read in the book of Zechariah; for he had taken up the cause and the place of man. He stood, then, there where the inheritance of Adam stood. Like a good athlete he allowed him to stand at his right hand, so that he might drive him back, saying: Get behind, Satan. And so the adversary, cast down from his place, withdrew; and so that the devil might not stand at your right hand, he says: Come, follow me. David, therefore, foreseeing the coming of the Lord, who came down from heaven to free us from the power of the adversary, says: The Lord is at my right hand, that I may not be moved. But he at whose right hand the devil was, was moved. Rightly, then, does he say here too: You have held my right hand, that is: so that I can no longer sin, so that I, who before was wavering with slippery step, may be able to stand firm in a sure station. How well the apostle said this, when the Lord saw him troubled and, stretching out his own right hand, did not suffer him to stagger, and made him firm with fearless step! And so Peter, set free, what else did he speak but these prophetic verses: You have held my right hand, and in your will you have led me, and with glory you have taken me up? What is the right hand but the active virtue of the soul? If it is directed by the will of the Lord, it desires nothing else, seeks nothing, demands no riches of this world, no aids.

Chapter XI

28. Therefore the holy man says: For what remains for me in heaven? And what have I wished from you upon the earth? That is: you are my portion, you suffice me for all things; I have sought nothing else but to have you as my share; I have subjected myself to no heavenly creature, as the Gentiles do; I have coveted no riches of this world and no allurements of pleasures. I need nothing, I who have been taken up by you, nor does anything remain in the heavenly places that I should seek further. Having nothing, I have all things, because I have Christ, whom the most high Father did not spare, but delivered him up for us all; how then has he not with him given us all things? as the Apostle said; for all things are in Christ, through whom are all things and in whom all things hold together. Having all things in him, therefore, I seek no other reward, because he himself is the reward of all. And therefore he said to the perfect man: Take up your cross and follow me. He who follows him is not led to perfection by a prize, but is brought by perfection to its consummation in the prize. For the imitators of Christ are good not because of hope, but out of love of virtue; for Christ is good by nature, not out of desire for a prize. He suffered for this reason, that it delighted him to do good, not because he sought an increase of glory from his passion. Therefore he who desires to imitate him works not what profits himself, but what profits others. Hence not undeservedly he fails himself, but grows strong for others by the increase of virtue.

29. And fittingly he says: My heart and my flesh have failed, O God of my heart. For things everlasting cannot succeed unless earthly things have failed. The flesh therefore fails when the things that are carnal are put to death; those also fail who carry about the dying of Jesus Christ in their flesh; for the death of Christ works in them, so that every allurement of errors may die. Hence it is gathered that the heart of man also fails when the evil thoughts that proceed from the heart are put to death, so that forgetfulness may hide all earthly things, and God may become the God of the heart of those who, blessed and pure in heart, deserve to see God, so that they may draw near to you and not separate themselves. For God is one who draws near, not one who repels those who draw near; for he wishes to be to all a cause of salvation, not of death. In short, he repels no one except him who has thought that he must be kept apart from his sight.

30. For behold, he says, those who remove themselves far from you will perish. For each one by his works either joins himself to your loving-kindness or separates himself from it; for he flees God who does those things in the knowledge of which he fears to be caught, like the one who, covered by walls and surrounded by darkness, thinks that he is not seen by the Lord God; yet he is seen, when it is said: You have destroyed all who commit fornication away from you. For just as a woman who commits fornication does not cleave to her husband, and is neither one flesh with her husband nor one spirit, but divides and separates herself by fornicating, so whatever soul does not cleave to God, but, serving the vain worship of idols, commits fornication, divides itself by the perversity of sacrilege and becomes far from the Lord, though it ought to be near; and he perishes who is separated from the Lord.

31. Hence the holy man, who dreads the judgment of God, wishes always to cleave to Christ and to place his hope in him, so as to praise the Lord, to whom is honour, glory, everlastingness from the ages, and now and always and unto all the ages of ages. Amen.

Book Four. On the Interrogation of David

Chapter I

1. Many indeed have lamented the weakness of human frailty, yet holy Job and holy David more excellently than the rest. The former, the earlier of the two, is direct, vehement, sharp, and, as if exasperated by grievous pains, in a grander buskin; the latter is gentle and placid and mild, of a milder feeling, so that he truly imitated the feeling of the hart, which he set before himself to imitate. Nor let it disturb you if I seem to proclaim so great a prophet by the likeness of a wild beast, since you read that it was said to the apostles: Be shrewd as serpents, simple as doves.

2. But nevertheless, although likenesses of this kind are supported by devout examples, and the nature of harts is innocent and gentle, I think that the hart set forth in this place for the prophet's imitation is the one of whom Solomon, the champion of his father's mind, said in Proverbs: Let the hart of friendship and the foal of graces converse with you. For the true Son of God expressed in himself the nature that he himself gave to living creatures; he came into this world like a hart, and with wonderful simplicity joined himself to those by whom snares were being prepared for him. For the simplicity of harts of this kind is said to be such that, when they see themselves being hunted, they attach themselves to those horsemen who, set there in the service of deceit, lead them, drawn in by a show of flight and a pretence of companionship, right into the nets. So therefore the Lord, as if unaware of the danger and unforeseeing, mingled with the Jews who were contriving a trick against him, and took to himself the companionship of Judas the traitor, by whose deadly pretence he came right up to the snares of the cross and the nets of the passion. Hence, turning to him, he said: Judas, do you betray the Son of Man with a kiss? And in this way indeed he came to the nets of the synagogue and willingly put them on, but he was neither entangled nor bound, he who set all free.

3. In short, he stood out above the nets. And because his own had not received him, he called the Church and conferred his grace upon her, as the most holy Church herself testifies in the Song of Songs, saying: I have adjured you, daughters of Jerusalem, by the powers and strengths of the field, if you will stir up and stir up again love until it pleases. She asks, therefore, in the odour of the field of which holy Jacob smelled, that is, by that faith, by that devotion, that her bridegroom be stirred up by the daughters of Jerusalem, so that he may hasten to the bride, and that his love be stirred up in her, or even that he himself be stirred up, because love is the bridegroom. For God, as John said, is love. But he did not allow himself to be stirred up by others, since he was hastening of his own accord; going forth from his bridal chamber, he exulted like a giant to run the way. The bride saw him and heard the voice of him coming, and suddenly turning she said: Behold, he comes here, leaping upon the mountains, leaping over the hills; for he leaps upon the greater, leaps over the lesser, so as not to suffer hindrances to his devout haste. My kinsman is like she says a roe or a young hart upon the mountains of Bethel. A good hart, whose mountain is the house of God, to which he ran with such speed that he anticipated the vows and desires of the bride. In short, him whom she had seen coming from afar she suddenly knew to be present with her, and hence she says: Behold, he is here behind our wall, looking out through the windows, standing out through the nets. My kinsman answered and said to me: Rise, come, my near one, my beautiful one, my dove, for behold, the winter has passed, the rain has gone: it has departed of itself; flowers have been seen on the earth. The winter is the synagogue, the rain the people of the Jews, who could not see the sun; the flowers are the apostles. And he added: The harvest of the cutting has come, the voice of the turtledove has been heard in our land. That harvest is the faith of the Church; the voice of the turtledove is chastity.

4. Not with these alone .... but Christ also took on the likeness of the hart, because, coming to earth, he trod down that serpent, the devil, without any harm to himself; he offered him his heel, but did not feel his venom. Hence it was said to him: You will walk upon the asp and the basilisk. Let us therefore also be harts, so that we may be able to walk upon serpents. We shall be harts if we follow the voice of Christ, which prepares harts and makes them not fear the bites of serpents, and, if any happen to have been wounded, takes away their pain by loosing the offence. Of these harts the Lord says to Job: Have you guarded the births of the harts? And have you numbered their months full of bearing, and have you loosed their births or reared their sons, so that they do not fear? Hear how the sons of such harts do not fear. Let Isaiah teach you, saying: And a little child will put his hand into the den of asps, and they will not harm him. And so that you may recognize that it seems to signify the sons of the Church, he added: And will you send forth their births? Their sons will break forth and will be multiplied in generation. They will go out and will not return back. For no one putting his hand to the plough and looking back is fit for the kingdom of God.

5. Rightly therefore the Lord became a hart, so that the voice of the Lord might prepare for himself such harts, of whom he says: In my name they will cast out demons, they will speak with new tongues, they will take up serpents, and if they drink anything deadly, it will not harm them. For they took up serpents when the holy apostles, by the spirit of their mouth, drew the spiritual wickednesses out of the lurking places of bodies, and did not feel the deadly venoms. In short, when a viper springing out of the brushwood had bitten Paul, the barbarians, seeing the viper hanging from his hand, thought that he would suddenly die. But he stood undaunted, and was neither moved by the wound nor filled with the venom. Hence, seeing him as one not begotten by the condition of man but brought forth, as it were, by the grace of God, they thought him to be above men. See the hart drawing vipers out of their lurking places by the divine spirit which was in his nostrils, as Job said. Paul, turning it says in the spirit and looking back with grief at the python, said: I command you in the name of the Lord Jesus Christ to come out of her at once. And it came out that same hour. See the hart when he came to baptism and, washed in the flowing water of the sacred font, cast off all the venoms of persecution. See the hart, the Lord Jesus, when he came to John the Baptist, and, when John said to him: I ought to be baptized by you, and do you come to me? he answered: Let it be so now. And with this word, thirsting for the salvation of all, he went down into the waters with all eagerness. But now the hart has played enough for us in the opening of the treatise, as at the beginning of the year, after the custom of the common people. Let us go on to the rest.

Chapter II

6. David interrogates, as I said, saying to the Lord: As the hart longs for the springs of waters, so my soul longs for you, O God. My soul has thirsted for the living God. When shall I come and appear before the face of God? The holy man burns and cannot contain himself — for the magnificence of the mind is greater than the magnitude of any body — and, secure of his merit, he longs to fly away from earth to heavenly things, as he himself also says elsewhere: Who will give me wings like a dove: and I will fly and be at rest. For here are snares, by which the just man, even if he is not entangled, is nevertheless hindered; here are pains and cares, there joy, where grace is; here, finally, are the chains of the body, which Paul longed to loose, so that, stripped of all hindrances, he might stand free before the Lord. This, then, was what the soul of David thirsted for: that he might see God no longer through faith but face to face, and not only be absent from the body but be released from the body; for to be dissolved and to be with Christ is much better, because to the just man to die is gain. And it is indeed great gain to be without sin, not to be moved by the allurements of offences. For who is clean from filth, when not even the life of one day of man on earth is free from the contagion of offences? By living, therefore, we incur losses of innocence; by death we obtain an end of error. Gain, therefore, is acquired by death, but by the use of life, as for wretched debtors on an interest-bearing bond, the interest is increased unto guilt. And well does the soul thirst which hastens to the spring, not of this water, but of eternal life, of which he said above: For with you is the spring of life, and in your light we shall see light. Rightly, therefore, David hastened to arrive and to appear before the face of God, whose countenance is light, because the Lord enlightens all on whom he looks.

7. My tears have been to me he says bread day and night, while it is said to me daily: Where is your God? There tears are rightly bread where there is hunger for justice. For blessed are those who hunger and thirst for justice, for they shall be filled. There are, therefore, tears which are bread and strengthen the heart of man. With this discussion that saying of Ecclesiastes also agrees: Send your bread before the face of the water, because there is heavenly bread where there is the water of grace, since those rightly receive the substance of the word and the nourishment of the mystical meaning from whose belly flow rivers of living water. Likewise also, this living bread is there where there is the water of tears and the weeping of penitence. For thus it is written: In weeping they went out, and in consolation I will call them back. Blessed, therefore, are those whose tears are bread, who have deserved to laugh, because blessed are you who weep.

8. Mindful of these things he says I poured out my soul upon myself. The holy man gathers himself in from the things that are outside and pours out his soul over himself, so that the soul, poured out over the body, may hide the weakness of the flesh, cover the appetite of the body, and the virtue of the soul and the mind may stretch forth everywhere. Hence also in later verses he says: I will pour out my prayer in his sight. Where prayer is poured out, there sins are covered. But of what things does he say he is mindful? Of those, surely, which he desired: that he might come and appear in the sight of God, that he might see that eternal court of his, in which he walked about in spirit and took delight in an entrance anticipated.

9. For I shall go in he says into the place of the wonderful tabernacle, even to the house of God, with the voice of exultation and praise, the sound of one feasting. Not undeservedly did he weep, because he was dwelling on earth, he to whom heavenly tabernacles were owed and whom entrance into the court of the Mighty One awaited. In short, he preferred that court alone to all the riches of his kingdom, as he himself testified elsewhere, saying: One thing I have asked of the Lord, this will I seek: that I may dwell in the house of the Lord all the days of my life, and that I may see the delight of the Lord. The delight of the Lord is in the Church. The Church is the image of heavenly things; for after the shadow passed, the image succeeded. The shadow is the synagogue: in the shadow is the Law, in the Gospel the truth. Therefore in the light of the Gospel the image of the truth shines out. The prophet therefore wept because good things full of grace and filled with joy were being delayed.

Chapter III

10. In short, in later verses too he says: Woe is me, that my sojourning is prolonged! And therefore he interrogated the Lord, because he was hastening to better things. Yet in the very afflictions of the world the hope of things to come was a great consolation for things present. For who would not lift up his mind, who could hope that those blessed fellowships are reserved for him in the heavenly tabernacle? But because to our weak condition things to come are often a weariness, and things present a vexation, therefore the soul of the holy prophet too was troubled when the waves of the body rose up.

11. For I would not have you wonder if the prophet says that his soul was shaken, since the Lord Jesus himself said: Now my soul is troubled. For he who took on our weaknesses took on our feeling also, in which he was also sorrowful even unto death, not on account of death; for a voluntary death could not have held sadness, a death in which there was to be the joy of all, the refreshment of all. Of it he also said elsewhere: And I rose and saw, and sleep became sweet to me. Good is the sleep that made the hungry not hunger and the thirsty not thirst, for whom it prepared the sweetness of the sacraments. How then was his soul troubled with fear, who made the souls of others not fear? He was sorrowful, therefore, even unto death, until grace should be consummated. This is proved by his own testimony, when he says of his death: I have a baptism to be baptized with, and how I am distressed until it is accomplished?

12. Troubled therefore by the slippery windings of this world, David says: Why are you sad, O soul, why do you trouble me? Hope in God, for I will praise him: the salvation of my countenance and my God. Therefore, when we are anxious and full of care, let hope strengthen us by the expectation of things to come. Look at each point. Hope, for I will praise, he says, not 'I praise,' but 'I will praise'; that is: I will praise better then, when, having beheld the glory of God with unveiled face, I shall be refashioned into the same image.

13. Suddenly, while he was consoling himself, coming back to himself he says: My soul is troubled within myself, that is: I who ought to strengthen others am myself troubled, and since I have no support from myself, let us take it from the Author.

Chapter IV

14. Therefore he says I will remember you, O Lord, from the land of the Jordan and of Hermonin. He remembers from the land of the Jordan, in which grace heaps up the memory of devotion. Into the Jordan that Naaman the Syrian went down and was made clean of leprosy. In the Jordan Christ was baptized, when he instituted the form of the saving washing. The name Jordan signifies descent, the descent by which the Lord Jesus came down, who cleansed the neighbours of the river Jordan from the contagion of offences. This river goes out of Egypt and divides the land of promise. Therefore whoever is troubled, if he takes good counsel, goes out of Egypt and follows the way of light; for they have interpreted Hermonin as the way of the lamp. Go out of Egypt first, then, if you wish to see the light of Christ. The Canaanite woman went out from the borders of the Gentiles and found Christ, to whom she said: Have mercy on me, Son of David! Moses went out of Egypt and was made a prophet and was sent back to the people, to free their souls from the land of affliction. And the lamp of Christ is in the body. This lamp shows you the way. Hence also the holy man says: Your word is a lamp to my feet, a lamp because it has enlightened the souls of all and shown the way in darkness. The Gospel is the way of the lamp; it shines in shadow, that is, in the world. Hence you also have elsewhere: They will be made white as snow in Selmon, that is, in overshadowing.

15. Christ is also the Jordan, who divides the land; hear how he divides: And a sword will pass through your own soul, to reveal the thoughts of many hearts, because he is the divider of our souls, who descends into the inmost secrets of the heart and detects the thoughts of minds. This sword is the living word of God. In short, in the Letter to the Hebrews you read thus: For the word of God is living and powerful and sharper than any sharpest sword, piercing even to the division of soul and spirit and of joints and marrows. This is the spring of Siloam, which is called Sent, since Christ said that he was sent by the Father. There is also that division which is gathered from the fact that the tribes of the Jews inhabited both banks of the Jordan, since the Son of Man, who came down from heaven in later times, that true Jordan, that true divider of earthly and heavenly things, gave the fathers a divided possession: one to be possessed on earth, the other to be kept for the merits of the future life. Both of these befit Christ alone, either to divide heavenly things or to detect hidden things; for he divides inward things who detects hidden things, which is surely the mark of divinity. In short, you have it written thus, that God said: I will rejoice and divide Sicima. This is that magnificent portion which Jacob assigned to his son Joseph as more excellent than all. Hence he says: But I give you Sicima, magnificent above your brothers, which I took from the hands of the Amorites with my sword and arrow, because the division was owed to the Lord alone, a division which is grasped by the word, that is, by that spiritual sword of the true Solomon. What is meant by "alone"? The Father without Christ, or Christ without the Father? Not at all. When I say "the Father alone", I do not separate the Son, because the Son is in the bosom and secret of the Father. When I say "the Son alone", I join the Father too, as the Son also joined him, saying: Behold, the hour comes that you leave me alone; but I am not alone, because the Father is with me. So, therefore, the Father too is called blessed and alone powerful in such a way that the Son is not set apart from him, who is always in the Father. In short, John said excellently: In the beginning was the Word, but he was not without the Father. And God the Father was, but he was not without the Word, because the Word was with God.

16. This Sicima is the Church; for Solomon chose her, whose hidden love he distinguished. This Sicima is Mary, whose soul the sword of God passes through and divides. This Sicima is "ascending", as the interpretation has it. Who she is that ascends, hear him saying of the Church: Who is she that ascends, made white, leaning upon her brother? She is the active one, who in Greek is called ἐναργής, because she shines forth with faith and works. To her sons it is said: Let your works shine before my Father, who is in heaven.

17. David therefore remembers God from the land of the Jordan and of Hermonin, from the little mountain. Who is this little mountain? Let us consider whether perhaps the divinity of Christ is the great mountain. In short, I fill heaven and earth says the Lord. If, therefore, the divinity of Christ is the great mountain, surely his incarnation is the small mountain. Christ, therefore, is both, both the great mountain and the lesser: great truly, because great is the Lord and great is his power; lesser, because it is written: You have lessened him a little below the angels. Hence Isaiah says: We saw him, and he had neither form nor comeliness. Yet the same one both became lesser from great and great from lesser: lesser from great, because though he was in the form of God, he emptied himself and took the form of a servant; and great from lesser, because Daniel says: And the stone which struck the image became a great mountain and filled the whole earth. If you ask who this stone is, recognize it: The stone which the builders rejected, this has become the head of the corner. Yet the same one, even when he seemed small, was great. To this Isaiah assents, saying: A child is born to us, a son is given to us, whose beginning is upon his shoulders, and he is called the angel of great counsel. Christ is all things for your sake: a stone for your sake, that you may be built up; a mountain for your sake, that you may ascend. Ascend, therefore, above the mountain, you who seek heavenly things. For this he bowed the heaven, that you might be nearer; for this he rose up to the summit of the mountain, that he might lift you up.

18. Not undeservedly, therefore, did abyss call upon abyss, that this mountain might become small, of which the prophet says: Abyss calls upon abyss at the voice of your cataracts. The Old Testament did not avail for the redemption of this world: it called upon the New Testament and, as it were, summoned it to its aid. The Law cried out, announcing the Gospel. For it was half-full, and therefore it was necessary that he should come who would fulfil the Law. For Christ is the end of the Law unto justice for everyone who believes, he who came to fulfil the Law, not to destroy it. How the Law is an abyss, hear him saying: Your judgments are like a great abyss. At the same time understand from this that both are the testament of one wisdom, which he came to fulfil as his own. And by the cataracts we understand the depths of the words and the force of the heavenly utterance, which have flowed down to us like rain from heaven. Christ, therefore, and the divine Scripture are the remedy of all wearinesses and the one refuge in temptations.

Chapter V

19. In short, when David noticed that the surges of the waves of the world were coming over him, which we must undergo in great number on the sea of this life, mindful of the mercies of the Lord, which he has promised in countless oracles, turning to prayer he appeals to God, knowing that his mercy is commanded in the light, that is, in the Law, because your precepts are light, but in temptations it is made manifest as in the darkness of night. Hence, like a traveller who longs to return to his homeland and to reach the place he is making for, but is wearied by the rough road of this life, he summons a guide and begs for relief.

20. With me he says is prayer to the God of my life; I will say to God: you are my upholder. Well does he seek help already known to him, and he approaches the author of the promise and the provider of the accustomed gift, so that in giving aid, if the merit of man falls short, the divine precedent may not fall short. Someone says: 'When did God uphold him'? To establish this, come with me to the beginning of sacred Scripture; see how the Lord with his own hands formed man out of clay, whence this same man also says in later verses: Your hands have made me and fashioned me. Like a potter, God wrought the fabric of human flesh. And to Jeremiah it is said: Go down into the potter's house, and there you will hear my words. It often happens to a potter, surely, that while a vessel is being shaped it falls from his hands, and he gathers up the clay again to remake the vessel. In short, Jeremiah too says: I went down and saw how the vessel fell which he was making in his hands. And again he says: He made another vessel as it pleased him. Rightly, therefore, he is called upholder, because he himself upheld us with his hands, he himself formed us. Those are a human potter's vessels, some for honour, others for dishonour. We are all earthen vessels: even if someone is a king, he is an earthen vessel; even if an apostle, he is a vessel. Hence Paul too: We have, he says, this treasure in earthen vessels; and the prophet says of the king: Jeconiah has been made worthless like a vessel for which there is no need. And he added: Earth, hear the word of the Lord: write this man down as disowned. As if by a father's right, our God is wont to disown degenerate sons; therefore the earth too writes down those who are sons of the earth. Hence, when the Jews were accusing the adulteress, the Lord Jesus was writing with his finger on the earth. But the just are not written on the earth, as we read. To them it is said: Rejoice, because your names are written in heaven.

21. The Lord, therefore, upheld us when he fashioned us; he upholds us also when he commands us to be born. Hence the just man says: You upheld me from my mother's womb. Of which mother? Before I formed you in the womb, I knew you. Those whom he forms he also upholds; he upholds them also as they come forth: And before you came forth from your mother's womb, I sanctified you. He is an upholder who has upheld with his hands, as the maker of the human race is called upholder, and one who has upheld by visitation, in order to protect. Hence the prophet himself says elsewhere: He who dwells in the help of the Most High will say to the Lord: You are my upholder and my refuge. The first upholding is of workmanship, the second of defence. In short, hear Moses saying: Spreading out his wings he took them up and upheld them upon his shoulders. He upheld them as an eagle does, which is accustomed to test its young, so as to keep and nourish those in whom it has observed that the native quality of a true birth and the grace of an uncorrupted nature bear witness, or to cast off those in whom it has detected, at a still tender age, the weakness of a degenerate origin.

Chapter VI

22. Why have you forgotten me? and why have you cast me off? God does not forget — for it is impossible that he should forget, to whom all things that are, that have been made and that are to come, are present — but our sins pour into him grounds for forgetting, so that he blots out those whom he has known to be unworthy of his visitation; for the Lord knows those who are his. Yet where some work iniquity, to them he says: I do not know you. Who, then, is there who could say to God: Why have you forgotten me? But yet this is common to the saints and to us who are weak. The holy man says it as one conscious, as it were, of his own merit, and yet the holier he is, the humbler. But if the holy man scarcely says it, what shall I, a sinner, say, unless I refer it to this: why have you forgotten your work, why have you forgotten your visitation, finally, why have you forgotten my weakness? For what is man, except that you visit him? Do not, then, forget the weak. Remember, O Lord, that you made me weak; remember that you fashioned me of dust. How shall I be able to stand, unless you always fix your gaze on this clay to make it firm, so that my firmness may come forth from your face? When you turn away your face, all things will be troubled: if you fix your gaze, woe is me. You have nothing to look upon in me except the contagions of offences; it is profitable neither to be forsaken nor to be seen, because while we are seen, we offend. Yet we may judge that he does not cast off those whom he sees, because he cleanses those whom he looks upon. A fire burns before him, to burn away the crime.

23. It is good for us, then, not to be cast off. Therefore David complains, because he believed that he, who had before been upheld, was cast off. Indeed, in what comes later he said: Upon you I have been strengthened from the womb. But above too we have it written: Upon you I was cast forth from the womb, from my mother's belly, you are my God. It is good even to be cast forth, but upon God. Indeed, this is said in Psalm 21 (22) in the person of Christ, who truly was cast forth from the virgin's womb upon the Father; for earthly things did not receive him as he died. Hence, when he was placed on the cross, as he sent forth his spirit, he said to the Father: Into your hands I commend my spirit.

24. Let none, then, add Lord, as the psalm-singers do, for I have found it neither in my Latin manuscript nor in the Greek nor in the Gospel, which is clearer still. Indeed, he had said above: Father, forgive them this sin, and therefore he commends his spirit into his hands as to the Father, in whose bosom the Son always is. And yet, even if they add that he said Lord, let them consider that he speaks this as a man placed in death.

25. Therefore he was cast forth from the womb upon the Father, from his mother's belly, that is: he defined that the womb which cast him forth is his mother's belly. But the Father says: From the womb before the morning star I begot you. Surely the Father did not cast forth the Son, from whom the Son never went out, as he himself says: I was delivered up and did not go out. He did not cast forth him to whom he is joined by the unity of the same substance. It can therefore be read thus too: Upon you I was cast forth from the womb, from my mother's belly, so that there follows: You are my God, do not depart from me. It can also be read thus: From my mother's belly you are my God, since even when placed in the womb I never withdrew from you; I was with you, I who, like Jonah placed in the womb of the whale, was beseeching you for the people. And truly from his mother's belly he was with God, according to what is written: For before the child knew good or evil, he chose what is good and: Before he could call father or mother, he plundered the strength of Damascus and the spoils of Samaria, so that, having called forth the Gentiles, he might win a kingdom for the Father by the worship of loving devotion.

Chapter VII

26. Let us look at the rest. Why he says have you cast me off, and why do I go about sorrowful, while the enemy afflicts me, breaks my bones? Those who trouble me have reproached me, while it is said to me daily: Where is your God? Why are you sorrowful, O soul? and the rest. The first interrogation contained the complaint that the good things were being put off, whose fruit was already longed for. The second interrogation was that the coming of Christ, awaited by the prudent, was being delayed, the coming which the Law had announced, which the prophets promised; and the hearts of the just burned the more impatiently, since they had known that he would come for the redemption of all ...... also of all, for whom he would open the way of virtue by the path of the Gospel and show the paths of good works, as he himself said in Proverbs: The Lord created me the beginning of his ways. This, then, is why it was said to him: Where is your God? because Christ had not yet come, but was hoped for. The devil, therefore, was raging, in order to crush those whom he knew would believe in the coming of the Lord, and he afflicted them with various deaths. David, therefore, interrogates, so as to rouse him who delays by a prophetic complaint, to urge him to hasten, to admonish him to come to our aid. We have a likeness of this interrogation also in what comes later, where the same prophet says: Why, O God, have you cast us off to the end? And there he openly and tearfully lamented that he had forgotten his congregation and cast away the rod of his inheritance, and that his enemies had risen up against the people of God, of whom he says: And those who hate you have boasted in the midst of your feast. Perhaps this little verse would seem to point to the Assyrians, who triumphed over the people of the Jews, were it not followed by: They set up their signs as signs, and I did not know them. There are always signs, that is, standards, in war, which are wont to go before those about to fight and to lead the army's column. Every company or legion follows its own signs. And if they have been scattered by the turmoil of battle, they return to where they have seen their own signs to be, however far away they are. Every commander sets up these signs and prescribes that they be followed. But there are also other signs, which the victorious enemy imposes and decrees are to be observed as by captives; but he who is a faithful soldier follows his own signs and does not know foreign ones.

27. Let us consider more attentively and more earnestly what the foreign signs are. Christ has set his sign on the foreheads of each one; there too the Antichrist sets his signs, so that he may recognize his own. But to him who is a Jew in secret the true confessor says: They set up their signs as signs, and I did not know them. The devil and his ministers set them up, but I did not know them, because I did not consent to his arts, I did not yield to his commands. The Assyrian Nebuchadnezzar set signs upon the youths of the Hebrews and changed their names, and ordered that they should worship his image and withdraw from the solemn rites of their fathers and, setting the law of God aside, follow the rites of the Chaldeans. The king decreed this, but Daniel resolved in his heart to shun the contagion of the royal table. Rightly, then, it befits him to say that he did not know the foreign signs. It had been ordered that the Hebrew youths should worship the king's image; they answered him: We do not worship your image. Fittingly, then, each of them said: They set up their signs as signs, and I did not know them, that is, I did not experience them, I did not receive them by any consent, I did not take them over into myself by any fellowship. Hence also we read of the Son of God that he did not know sin. And elsewhere you have: For he who keeps the commandment does not know a wicked word, since it is perfectly clear that what is meant is not the knowledge of wickedness but a criminal fellowship. David himself also says later: But the malicious who turned away from me I did not know, and he declares where the adversaries wished to set these signs. As on a way he says above the summit. As in a forest of trees they have cut down its gates with axes all together; with the two-edged axe and the adze they have thrown it down. What does this mean, except to show that our faith ought not to be as if on the way, lest the birds of the sky come and take it away, just as that word which you read of in the Gospel ought not to be sown along the ways and paths?

28. Therefore the adversaries, wishing to uproot the faith of this holy man, since they do not see their signs in his heart, tried to place them as if on the way, that is, in an open thoroughfare; but the heart is at the summit, because the eyes of the wise man are in his head. And they set up signs as in a forest of trees, which are quickly burned up by fire or cut down with axes; for fire goes out from the forest and burns up even the cedars of Lebanon. And they thought this should be done in order to defile the tabernacle of the divine name, which is in us. For just as we are the temple of God, so too are we the tabernacle of God, in which the feasts of the Lord are celebrated. Therefore you, O man, guard your summit, so that you may shatter the heads of the enemies, the crown of the hair of those who walk about — for they walk about in superfluous things, not in holy ones: in the crown of the hair, not in the crown of devotion and faith — and if the spirit of one who has power rises up against you, as you have in Ecclesiastes, do not leave your place. For Christ, who made you after the image of God, has set you higher. Hold, therefore, the higher place of faith and devotion which you received from Christ, so that, having been made higher, you may easily repel the wicked spirit rising from the lower things, that is, from earthly and worldly things, and not receive his signs in your breast. Let him not occupy the porches of your soul nor the entrances of your mind, and, as in a forest of trees, lay waste with his fires what is frail and perishable, or cut down the doors of your heart with his axes. Let there be in us, then, not a forest but a vineyard; let the gate of our mouth and of our heart be shut most carefully, lest the enemy go in. He quickly splits the door if he finds it open; but Christ knocks, he does not throw it down, he who has strengthened, O Jerusalem, the bars of your gates. Christ knocks with his hand, that you may open; the adversary cuts down with axes; and therefore it is written that neither axe nor hammer should enter the house of God. Pride and deceit ought to be outside, not inside; for battles are outside, peace is inside, the peace which is above all understanding. Let your soul not be cut by the iron, but as the soul of Joseph did, so let your soul pass through the iron, lest your ruling part, like a kind of tabernacle of the Word, be torn down at the very beginning of faith and at the entrance of spiritual teaching. For one who is founded by practice and exercise remains immovable and gives no place to him who, transforming himself as it were into an angel of light, tries to climb up to the summit. If he does not see his signs in us, he will not be able to have the authority to resist. Therefore, so that the enemy may not afflict us, may not break our bones, let us not fail to persevere in Christ, so that he may say of us: It is three days now that they persevere with me, and I am not willing to send them away fasting, lest they fail on the way. Blessed is he to whom he himself has given the support of the heart, so that, set on the path of this life, he cannot fail. He does not fail who hopes in the Lord and praises him with inmost affection, since even that horseman whose horse's heel the serpent bit, although he fell backwards, was nevertheless not deceived, because he awaited salvation from the Lord.

Chapter VIII

29. The third interrogation, too, is that, placed in the midst of men who practise wickedness, he desires his cause to be separated from their contagion. Many think this should be referred to the Lord Jesus, because it belongs to him alone not to fear judgment, who conquers when he is judged. For he has a judgment from a man of iniquity, a judgment which Christ enters willingly, as you have it written: My people, what have I done to you, or how have I grieved you? Yet since the Father has given all judgment to him, certainly not as to one who is weak, but as to the Son, what judgment can he himself undergo? If they think that the Son must undergo the Father's judgment, the Father surely judges no one, but has given all judgment to the Son, so that all may honour the Son as they honour the Father. The Father honours the Son, and do you pass judgment on him? We have set this down so that no one should think that we, as if out of fear of the question, put the person of the prophet in the place of the Lord, since holy David, foreseeing in the spirit that the Jews would rise up against the Lord's passion, does not dread the judgment upon his own faith, and also demands that his cause be distinguished from the nation of the persecutors, lest the lineage of the whole Jewish race be entangled with the wicked heirs of his family and posterity.

30. Not without reason, then, is he troubled who sees that he has a wrestling against flesh and blood, and within himself a grievous shipwreck, in his own body a storm which he has no strength to withstand unless heavenly help comes to his support; for no enemy is more grievous to a man than those of his own household. And what is so much of one's own household as a man is to himself, and the weakness of his own flesh? And therefore the prophet hastens and prays with all his affection that Christ may come, the strength of all, who would take upon himself all weaknesses and make both one, and, by removing the wall that kept the inner affection divided so that it could not come together in concord, would dissolve the enmities of mind and flesh fighting against each other. Therefore, because there was a battle for him within himself, and there was one from his neighbours, who, unmindful of law and equity, were preparing snares of fraud and ambushes, and the remedy he had hoped for was being put off, he thought himself cast off, as though he who had promised that he would be the remedy were refusing to come; and, as if called back from the hope of the dawning day into the depths of darkness, he prayed that the light might shine forth to drive back the squalor of this world, that the eternal truth might be present to abolish the deceitful image of this world.

31. God was at hand to his prayers, he who is wont both to stand by unlooked for and to show himself to those who do not ask for him, as he himself says: I was made manifest to those who did not seek me; and, assenting to his devout prayers, he anticipated the whole sequence of his petitions with swift fulfilment, and suddenly led the holy prophet in the spirit into the Church and into his tabernacles, and set before his eyes the sacred altar, on which the redemption of the whole world was to take place, and the remission of sins for all throughout the whole earth.

Chapter IX

32. Seeing, then, in the spirit that sweetness of the heavenly sacraments, that table which repels the plots of those who wear him down, as he himself said earlier: You have prepared a table in my sight against those who crush me, he says: And I will go in to the altar of my God, to God who gives joy to my youth. Beautifully did he say, as if he were Adam: And I will go in. For we had been cast out of the paradise of the Lord, from the time when Adam, conscious of his sin, turned aside from the face of the Lord. Fittingly also he added: And I will go in to the altar of God, as though he were returning into the sight of God. For God had turned away from our gifts when he did not approve the gifts of that parricide Cain. Cain lay hidden, estranged from the sight of the Lord: the savage force of his raging mind, because the Lord had looked upon his brother's gifts, left his own heirs under offence. Abel had perished not to himself, he whose blood also cried out to the Lord, but he had perished to all of us. Almost no one's sacrifice was any longer approved, because there was none who did good, there was not even one, since neither faith toward God nor devotion toward a brother's kinship was preserved. The Lord Jesus came to raise up Adam; Abel too was raised up, whose gifts pleased God. The Lord Jesus offered himself, that is, the first-fruits of his body, in the sprinkling of blood that speaks better than the blood of Abel spoke on the earth. God looked upon his gifts, and from that time he himself left the grace of divine reconciliation to good heirs. Rightly, therefore, does holy David say, as if in the person of man reconciled: And I will go in to the altar of my God, to God who gives joy to my youth.

33. Let us support this passage, if we can, by the example of another prophet as well, showing how the Lord first turned away from man's sacrifices and was afterwards reconciled to him. We have it written in the book of Isaiah, where God says: What to me is the multitude of your sacrifices? says the Lord. I am full, that is: I abound in what is mine, I do not seek what is yours; I do not want holocausts of rams and the fat of lambs and the blood of bulls and of goats, and do not come thus into my sight. And yet Abel had offered a sacrifice from the young of the flock, by which he had pleased God; but he who was waiting for the truth of the sacrifice did not require the type; for the saving passion of the Lord was awaited. For who he says has required these things from your hands? You shall not go on treading my palace. And below: When you stretch out your hands to me, I will turn away my face from you. But wash yourselves, be clean, take away malice from your souls. Judge for the orphan and justify the widow, and come, let us reason together, says the Lord. It is clear, therefore, that the Lord both turned away from man's sacrifices before and was reconciled afterwards, so that he deigned to look upon our sacrifices.

34. Therefore he goes in without fear who enters into the mercy of the Lord. Indeed, it is said to the good servant: Go in into the joy of your lord, but of the wicked servant it is said: Take him into the outer darkness. Therefore Adam too, cast out of the heavenly homeland and of that seat of paradise, was banished to the island of sin. Rightly, then, does Scripture say: Be renewed, O islands, because we are surrounded by the waves of sins like islands in the strait of this world. These islands, then, have been renewed through the coming of the Lord by the remission of sins; that is, men, set in the washing in the midst of the waters as if they were islands, were being pounded by masses of waves, like islands by the resounding, dashing billows of sins, like islands on which before, as on reefs of fraud, the simple suffered frequent shipwrecks, because there was guile in the heart and flatteries in the mouth. But after the Lord Jesus, in whom there is no guile, coming into this world, calmed the depths of human minds by setting forth heavenly teaching, and poured tranquillity back into the affections of each, taking away the hedge of discord, there began to be, as it were, harbours of refuge for those who approached, so that each may moor the ship of his own repose in the affection of his neighbour or his brother, and hold fast in a kind of shoreline retreat of a devout mind.

35. Not without reason, then, does David cry out as one renewed: And I will go in to the altar of my God, to God who gives joy to my youth. So it is that he who had said above that he had grown old among his enemies, as we read in the sixth psalm, here says that his youth has been renewed out of the old age, grown old indeed, of man's fall. For we are renewed through the washing of regeneration, we are renewed through the outpouring of the Holy Spirit, and we shall be renewed also through the resurrection, as he says later: Your youth will be renewed like the eagle's. Hear how we are renewed: You will sprinkle me with hyssop and I shall be cleansed; you will wash me and I shall be made white above snow. And in Isaiah he says: If your sins are like scarlet, I will make them white as snow. Rightly is he renewed who is changed from the darkness of sins into the light of virtues and into grace, so that he who before was filthy with foul defilement may shine above snow with an exceedingly white brightness.

Chapter X

36. I will praise you on the lyre, my God. Our soul has its own lyre; for Paul would not say: I will pray with the spirit, I will pray also with the mind; I will sing psalms with the spirit, I will sing psalms also with the mind, unless he had a lyre that would resound under the plectrum of the Holy Spirit. Our flesh is a lyre when it dies to sin, that it may live to God; it is a lyre when it receives the sevenfold Spirit in the sacrament of baptism. For the tortoise, while it lives, is sunk in mud; once it has died, its shell is fitted for the use of singing and for the grace of a holy discipline, so that it may sound forth the distinctions of seven notes in modulating measures. Likewise our flesh, if it lives for bodily allurements, lives in a kind of mire and whirlpool of pleasures; if it dies to luxury and incontinence, then it takes up the true life again, then it begins to give forth the sweet melody of good works. Sweet is the sound of chastity, sweet the sound of those who fear God. And so their sound has gone out into all the earth. Sweet is the sound of faith, which is proclaimed, as it is written, in the whole world. Let this sound go out from us to God, as it went out also from the Thessalonians, so that even when we are not singing we may sing, and proclaim the Lord by the harmony of good works, to whom is honour, glory, everlastingness from the ages, and now and always and unto all the ages of ages. Amen.
