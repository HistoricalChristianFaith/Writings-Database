On Isaac and the Soul

Latin source: Ambrose's treatise on Isaac and the soul, in one book of eight chapters, an allegory of the soul's union with Christ the Word drawn chiefly from the Song of Songs, written at Milan about 386–391 as a companion to On Death as a Good, from the critical text of C. Schenkl, Corpus Scriptorum Ecclesiasticorum Latinorum 32/1 (Vienna, 1896–97), pp. 641–700, verified against the page images and collated with the Benedictine edition as reprinted in Migne, PL 14, with the edition's misprints corrected and the deletion in Schenkl's Addenda applied. Scripture is translated as Ambrose quotes it, from his own Old Latin text; italics mark the Scripture he quotes. The chapter and section numbers are the Maurist ones shared by all editions; the Maurist chapter summaries are not translated. The one psalm Ambrose names (Chapter IV §17) keeps its Latin (LXX) number, with the Hebrew number in parentheses. Schenkl's supplements are read into the text, the word he brackets as spurious is omitted, the word he marks as corrupt (Chapter VIII §66, † Tharsis) is kept as printed, and his critical apparatus is not translated. This English translation is released into the public domain.

Chapter I

1. In treating of his father we have already set out sufficiently both the origin and the grace of holy Isaac. It redounds to his full glory that, born to so great and so inimitable a man, he was a reward to his father Abraham. Nor indeed is this to be wondered at, since in him a figure of the Lord's birth and passion went before: for a barren old woman bore him by the promise of God, that we might believe that God is able to bring it about that even a virgin can give birth; and he was offered, an only son, to be sacrificed, one who would both not be lost to his father and would fulfil the sacrifice. And so by his very name he signifies the figure and the grace; for Isaac means in Latin laughter, and laughter is the sign of joy. And who does not know that he is the joy of all, who, with the dread of fearful death either crushed or its grief removed, has been made for all the forgiveness of sins? So the one was named and the other was signified; the one was portrayed and the other was announced. He it is whom even then the bondwoman persecuted; he it is on whose account it was even then said: Cast out the bondwoman; for the son of the bondwoman shall not be heir with my son Isaac; he it is for whom his father acquired a foreign bride; he it is, meek, humble and gentle, who, as Rebecca, that is, patience, was coming, went out into the field to withdraw apart. For it is the mark of a wise man to separate himself from the pleasures of the flesh, to lift up his soul and draw it away from the body; for this is to know oneself as man, who in the speech of the Chaldaeans is called Enos, in Latin man. Now Enos is he who undertook and hoped to call upon God, and so he is believed to have been taken up. So no one seems to be a man except him who hopes in God. And he who hopes in God is shown, by a clear interpretation of the truth, not to dwell on earth but, as one taken up, to cleave to God.

2. Good, then, is the true Isaac, as being full of grace and a fountain of joy. To this fountain Rebecca came, to fill her water jar. For Scripture says that going down to the fountain she filled her water jar and came up. And so either the Church or the soul went down to the fountain of wisdom, to fill her whole vessel and to draw the teachings of pure wisdom, which the Jews would not draw from the flowing fountain. Hear him who says what this fountain is: They have forsaken me, the fountain of living water. To this fountain the soul of the prophets ran thirsting, as David also says: My soul has thirsted for the living God, that it might satisfy its thirst with the abundance of divine knowledge and wash away the blood of folly with the watering of spiritual streams. For this is the flow of blood which, as the Law indicates, is uncovered when a man has union with a woman sitting in the days of her blood. The woman is delight, the allurements of the body. So take care that the vigour of your mind is not bent by a kind of union with bodily pleasure and softened, and dissolved into all its embraces, and that it does not open its fountain, which ought to be closed and fenced in by the zeal of attention and the reflection of reason; for a garden indeed enclosed, a fountain sealed. For when the vigour of the mind is relaxed, the senses of bodily delight pour themselves out, exceedingly destructive and rushing headlong into an appetite full of grave danger; these, had the careful guard of a lively mind remained, it would have reined in.

Chapter II

3. Consider, then, O man, who you are, by what you may guard your salvation and your life. What, then, is man? Is he soul, or flesh, or the union of both? For what we are is one thing, what is ours another; he who is clothed is one, the garment another. We read in the Old Testament: all the souls that went down into Egypt, said of men. And elsewhere it is said: my spirit shall not remain in these men, because they are flesh. So it is read of each of the two that it is called man, both of the soul and of the flesh. But there is this distinction: where soul is said for man, the Hebrew is meant, cleaving to God, not to the body, as in that saying: every simple soul is blessed; but where flesh is named for man, the sinner is expressed, as in that saying: but I am carnal, sold under sin. For what I work, I do not know; for I do not do what I will, but what I hate, that I do. This latter saying now concerns both as well; for he who wills is one, he who hates another, he who does yet another. Then he added: if, then, I do what I hate, I consent to the law, that it is good. But now it is no longer I who work it, but the sin that dwells in me. This too, more expressly: I see the law of my flesh fighting against the law of my mind and making me captive in the law of sin. And yet, when he had declared that man is at strife with himself in both, that is, the inner and the outer man, he chose to set himself on the side of the soul rather than on that of the body, because his soul was being dragged captive to sin, the soul in which he would rather be, and in which he affirms that he is, saying: unhappy man that I am! who will free me from the body of this death? He longs to be freed from it as from a foreign enemy.

4. So the soul is not blood, because blood belongs to the flesh; nor is the soul a harmony, because a harmony of this kind too belongs to the flesh; nor is the soul air, because the breathed spirit is one thing, the soul another; nor is the soul fire, nor is the soul an entelechy; but the soul is living, because Adam was made into a living soul, in that the soul gives life to the body, which is without sense and lifeless, and governs it. There is also a more excellent man, of whom it is said: but the spiritual man judges all things, and he himself is judged by no one. This man is more excellent than the rest. Hence David too says: what is man, that you are mindful of him, or the son of man, except that you visit him? Man has been made like to vanity. It is not that man according to the image of God who is a man of vanity; but he who lost it and fell into sin and slipped down into these material things, he himself is the man of vanity.

5. The soul, then, is by its own nature most excellent, but often, through its irrational part, it becomes liable to corruption, so that it is inclined to the pleasures of the body and to wantonness, so long as it does not keep the measure of things; or else it is deceived by opinion and, inclined toward matter, is glued fast to the body. So its power of sight is hindered and it is filled with evil, because while it attends to evil it fills itself with its vices and, through the lack of goodness, becomes more intemperate.

Chapter III

6. But the perfect soul turns away from matter; it flees and spurns everything immoderate, unstable and malign, and neither looks upon nor draws near to the corruption of that earthly stain: it strains toward divine things, but flees earthly matter. And flight is not to leave the earth, but, while being on earth, to hold fast to justice and sobriety, to renounce vices, not the uses of the elements. Holy David fled from the face of Saul, surely not so as to leave the earth, but to avoid the contagion of a man harsh, heedless and faithless. Yet he fled cleaving to God, as he himself says: my soul has cleaved behind you. He drew himself away and lifted himself up from the vices of this world; he lifted up his soul, as Isaac in the field either withdrew himself or, as others have it, walked about. For this too shows what it is to have a familiar fellowship with the virtues: that each one walk about in the innocence of his heart, be mingled by no practice with earthly vices, pursue a blameless way with an unstumbling step of the mind, and open no place in himself to corruption.

7. Such was Isaac when he was awaiting Rebecca's coming, preparing himself for a spiritual union. For she was coming already endowed with heavenly mysteries; she was coming bearing with her great ornaments for the ears and the hands, because the beauty of the Church stands out in hearing and in works, to whom, we observe, it was rightly said: be unto thousands of thousands, and let your seed possess the cities of the adversaries. Comely, then, is the Church, which has acquired sons from hostile nations. But this can also be applied to the soul, which subdues the passions of the body and turns them to the duties of the virtues, and makes the motions that resist her obedient to her. So either the soul of the patriarch, seeing the mystery of Christ, seeing Rebecca coming with vessels of gold and silver like the Church with the people of the nations, and marvelling at the beauty of the Word and of his sacraments, says: let him kiss me with the kisses of his mouth; or Rebecca, seeing the true Isaac, that true joy, the true gladness, desires to kiss him.

8. What, then, is: let him kiss me with the kisses of his mouth? Consider either the Church, kept in suspense through many ages, since the Lord's coming had long ago been promised to her through the prophets, or the soul which, raising herself from the body, having renounced luxury and delights and fleshly pleasures, and stripped also of care for worldly vanities, has long been yearning for the infusion of the divine presence into herself and for the grace of the saving Word: consider how she is wasted with grief because he comes late, and is afflicted, and so, as one wounded by love, since she cannot bear his delays, turns to the Father and asks him to send her God the Word, and declares the cause of her impatience, saying: let him kiss me with the kisses of his mouth. She seeks not one kiss but many kisses, so that she may satisfy her desire; for she who loves is not content with the stinginess of a single kiss, but demands many, claims many, and in this way is accustomed to commend herself the more to the beloved. Indeed, that woman in the Gospel was approved for this, because she has not ceased he says to kiss my feet. And therefore her many sins are forgiven her, because she loved much. So this soul too desires many kisses of the Word, that she may be enlightened with the light of divine knowledge. For this is the kiss of the Word, namely the light of sacred knowledge; for God the Word kisses us when he enlightens our heart and the very ruling part of man with the spirit of divine knowledge. Endowed with this nuptial pledge of love, the soul, glad and exulting, says: I opened my mouth and drew in the spirit. For a kiss is that by which lovers cleave to one another and, as it were, enjoy the sweetness of an inner grace. Through this kiss the soul cleaves to God the Word, for through it the spirit of the one who kisses is poured over into her, just as those also who kiss each other are not content with a touching of the lips, but seem to pour their spirit into one another.

9. And so, showing that she loves not only the outward form of the Word and a certain countenance, but all that is within him, she adds to the grace of the kisses: for better she says are your breasts than wine, and the fragrance of your ointments than all spices. She asked for a kiss; God the Word poured himself wholly into her and laid bare to her his breasts, that is, his doctrines and the teachings of inner wisdom, and was fragrant with the sweet fragrance of his ointments. Captivated by these, she says that the delight of divine knowledge is richer than the gladness of all bodily pleasure. For in the Word there breathes the fragrance of grace and the remission of sins, which, poured out over the whole world, has filled all things as with an ointment emptied out, because the heavy filth of vices has been wiped away from all.

10. Therefore she says the maidens have loved you. Draw us: let us run after the fragrance of your ointments. Good indeed is prudence, but sweet is mercy; for few attain the one, while the other reaches all. Because of this indulgence of yours, she says, souls renewed in spirit love you. Hence it is said also to the soul: your youth will be renewed like the eagle's; for he was speaking to the soul when he said: bless the Lord, O my soul. And therefore this soul too hastens to the Word and asks to be drawn, lest perhaps she be left behind, because the Word of God runs and is not bound. Indeed, he exults like a giant to run the way, and, because his going forth is from the highest heaven and his running back is to its highest point, seeing herself unequal to such great speed she says: draw us. Good is the soul which asks not for herself alone, but for all. Draw she says us. For we have the desire of following, which the grace of your ointments breathes into us; but because we cannot match your courses, draw us, so that, supported by your help, we may be able to tread in your footsteps. For if you draw us, we too shall run and shall catch the spiritual breezes of swiftness. For the burden is laid down by those to whom your hand is a support, and your oil is poured in, by which he was healed who had been wounded by robbers. And lest what she says, draw us, seem shameless to you, hear him saying: come to me, all you who labour and are burdened, and I will refresh you. You see that he draws us willingly, so that we may not be left behind as we follow. But let him who wishes to be drawn run, that he may lay hold; and let him run forgetting the things that are behind and reaching out to those that are before; for so he will be able to lay hold of Christ. Hence the Apostle too says: so run that you may all lay hold. She too who desires to lay hold wishes to reach the prize. Prudently, then, she asks to be drawn, because not all are able to follow. Indeed, to Peter when he said: where are you going? the Word of God answered: you cannot follow me now, but you will follow afterwards. He had entrusted to him the keys of the kingdom of heaven, and yet he judged him unequal to following him. This soul, however, he did not put off, because she was not presuming, but asking.

Chapter IV

11. Indeed she says: the king has brought me into his chamber. Blessed is every soul that enters the inner places. For, rising up from the body, she becomes more remote from all things, and within herself she searches and seeks whether she can in any way follow after that divine being. And when she has been able to grasp it, having passed beyond the things that are intelligible, she is established in it and is fed by it. Such was Paul, who knew that he had been caught up into paradise, but did not know whether he had been caught up outside the body or caught up in the body. For his soul had risen up from the body and had withdrawn and lifted itself away from the entrails and bonds of the flesh, and, having become a stranger to himself, he held within himself the unspeakable words which he heard, and could not make them public, because he perceived that it is not lawful for a man to speak them. The good soul, then, despises things visible and sensible, and neither stands among them nor lingers and stays in despising them, but ascends to those things that are eternal and invisible and full of wonders, raising herself with the pure perception of a devout mind. For, striving after perfection, she strains toward that good of the Godhead alone, and thinks that nothing else is to be sought, because she holds what is highest. And so a man of this kind, in whom is the beauty of the soul, abounds for himself alone, because he is sufficient for himself. Nor is he ever alone who has the Lord present as his protector.

12. Indeed, brought into that secret place of the divine one, let us exult she says and rejoice in you. Let us love your breasts beyond wine. For the just man exults not in riches and treasures of gold and silver, not in the fruits of possessions, not in powers, not in banquets, but in God alone.

13. Yet the same soul, knowing that she has been darkened by her fellowship with the body, says to other souls, or to those heavenly powers appointed to the sacred ministry: do not look at me, for I have been darkened, for the sun has not looked upon me. The sons of my mother have fought against me, that is, the passions of the body have assailed me, the allurements of the flesh have discoloured me; therefore the sun of justice has not shone upon me. Bereft of this protection, I have not been able to keep my devotion and my observance whole. For this is the meaning of: my vineyard I have not kept, because I brought forth thorns and not grapes, that is, producing sins instead of fruits.

14. And when she speaks of the Word, as the splendour of the Word shines upon her, she turns to him and says: where do you pasture? where do you rest at midday? Rightly she says where do you pasture? because the Word of God is royal; where do you rest? because it is moral; at midday, because it is mystical, since it was at midday that Joseph, set at a banquet with his brothers, revealed the mysteries of times to come. But David too says: reveal your way to the Lord and hope in him, and he himself will act, and he will bring forth your justice as the light and your judgement as the midday. And Paul himself declared that a light shone round about him at midday, when he was converted from persecution to grace. She complains, then, that she has been forsaken, that she has been left destitute, poor after being rich; for she abounded in the gift of graces, but she began to be in want when the abundance of the divine presence was denied her, and so she asks to be kept at least as a hired servant, she who before claimed for herself the grace of a more precious union.

15. To her the Word of God replied: unless you know yourself, comely one among women. You who complain that you have been left: unless you know yourself, unless you repent of your fall, unless you give proof of the earnestness of your devotion, unless your faith and sincerity increase, your complaint will profit nothing. Or thus: unless you know yourself, that you are comely, unless you preserve the beauty of your nature, and the allurements of the body do not sink you nor its hindrances hold you back, the nobility of a better creation will avail you nothing.

16. Know yourself, then, and the beauty of your nature, and go out as one whose feet are freed from bonds and laid bare to a naked footstep, so that you do not feel the coverings of the flesh, so that bodily bonds do not entangle the footstep of your mind, so that your foot may appear beautiful. For such are those who are chosen by the Lord to proclaim the kingdom of heaven, of whom it was said: how beautiful are the feet of those who bring good tidings of peace! Such was Moses, to whom it is said: loose the shoes from your feet, so that, as he was about to call the people to the kingdom of God, he might first lay aside the cast-off garments of the flesh and walk with bare spirit and with the footstep of the mind. This, then, is what he says: go out in the heels of the flocks and pasture your kids in the tents of the shepherds, because by the flocks we understand a kingdom, since it belongs to power to preside over flocks. And each one presides over himself with a certain royal power, if he restrains in himself the luxuries of the body and brings his flesh into subjection. And therefore it was said: the kingdom of God is within you. Hence he fittingly says to the soul: go out, that is, go out from slavery, go out from the rule and dominion of the flesh, and go out not in the flesh but in the spirit, go out to the governing of power. And therefore he added: pasture your kids, rule those things that are on your left; for if they are not ruled, they easily slip. Restrain the wantonness, the lasciviousness of your body and its irrational luxury, tame its fickle motions, pasture them not in bodily tents but in the tents of the shepherds, who know how to rule a flock. For lovely are the tents of Israel, like shady groves above a river, in which the soul, placed as though in readiness for battle, wages a good warfare, spies out hostile incursions, and seeks victory by the toil of virtue, so that she can be compared to that mare which is Solomon's, swift for running, apt for bearing, since fruitfulness of soul is desired and sought.

17. This mare, then, is precious, and the chariots of Pharaoh are swift, which some refer to the Church and to the people. But of this mystery we have spoken often elsewhere, and especially in Psalm 118 (119); in this place, however, we have undertaken to speak of the soul. This soul is reckoned like this mare, that is, a soul of prophetic or apostolic virtue, because she is numbered in the flock of those who by the fruitfulness of their preaching filled the spaces of the whole world, and, though set in the body, yet felt no loss in their spiritual course. And therefore she is praised because, with the heavenly precept shining upon her, she is now beautiful, now fair, she who displays in her face the comeliness of chastity and lifts up the ornaments of her neck, on which are the insignia of patience and humility. The true Isaac loved the comeliness, the humility and the patience of such a soul, and therefore eagerly sought her offspring.

18. And Rebecca conceived, and by her patience she loosed the knot of barrenness. But let us consider what the prophetic and apostolic soul brings forth, and how she brings forth. She went it says to inquire of the Lord, because the infants were leaping in her womb. And she received the answer: two nations are in your womb, because she presumes nothing of herself, but in all things asks the most high God to preside over her counsels, and at the same time, full of peace and piety, she joins two peoples by her faith and preaching and, as it were, encloses them in her womb.

19. Not without reason is she called sister rather than wife, because a gentle and peaceable soul receives the name of a common piety rather than of a particular union, and because she considers herself bound to all rather than to one.

20. Isaac dug wells, and very many indeed, which his father had dug and which the foreigners had filled up after the death of Abraham his father. Above the rest, however, he dug these wells: one in the valley of Gerar, and he found there a well of living water, and the shepherds of Gerar quarrelled with the shepherds of Isaac, because they claimed the water of this well for themselves as their own, and he called its name Injustice. And he dug another well, over which a dispute arose, and he gave it the name Enmities. And he dug a third, over which no quarrel broke out among the shepherds, and he called it Breadth. He dug also a well and found no water in it, and he called it the well of the Oath.

21. Who, reading this, would think that these were earthly rather than spiritual works, that either Abraham or Isaac dug wells, patriarchs, that is, so great, or even Jacob, as we find in the Gospel, men who were as it were fountains of the human race, and especially of devotion and faith? For what is a well of living water but the depth of profound doctrine? Hence also Hagar saw an angel at a well, and Jacob found himself a wife, Rachel, at a well; Moses too laid down beside a well the first claims of his future marriage.

22. From the well of vision, then, Isaac began to open the wells, and in good order, so that the water of this well might first wash and refresh the rational part of the soul and its eye, so as to make its sight clearer. He dug also several other wells. Hence it is also written: drink water from your own vessels and from the fountains of your wells. The more of them there are, the richer is the overflowing of graces. Yet he dug the well which his father Abraham had dug, over which the shepherds of Gerar, that is, of the wall, quarrelled — for where there is a wall, there is division between those who oppose one another, there is injustice — and therefore he called it Injustice. He dug another too, and, when dissension arose, he called it Enmities. In these the moral teaching seems to shine forth, because, when the partition of the wall was taken away, the enmities in the flesh of man were dissolved and both were made one, in figure through Isaac, in truth through Christ; and rightly was pure water afterwards found in that well, as being moral teaching, useful for drawing. And the well of Breadth, what else does it signify but the teaching about natural things? For which reason it is also called Breadth, because he who has passed beyond these worldly and sensible things is now at rest and secure, without contention, without quarrel. Hence, when the adverse and foreign thoughts have now been overcome — for what is so foreign as all worldly things, which cannot be everlasting? — the wise man can say: the Lord has made room for us and increased us upon the earth, because he has transcended earthly things. Last is the well of the Oath, at which God appeared to him and said to him: do not fear; for I am with you, and blessed him. This teaching is now mystical.

23. You have this in Solomon, for his Proverbs are moral, Ecclesiastes is natural, in which he as it were despises the vanities of this world, and his Song of Songs is mystical. You have it also in the prophet: sow for yourselves unto justice, gather the vintage unto the fruit of life, light for yourselves the light of knowledge. To have the light of knowledge is to have the perfection of love. And therefore it was said: do not fear; for love casts fear out. And so that we may know that Solomon too interpreted these wells in such a way as to refer them to moral, natural and mystical teaching, he set these wells in each of his books which he wrote on moral, natural or mystical matters.

24. For in Proverbs too, when he was saying that the outward show of worldly allurement must be avoided, he says: drink water from your own vessels and from the fountains of your wells, and let waters overflow for you from your own fountain. And below: let the fountain of your water be your own, and rejoice with your wife, because true wisdom is a remedy for us against the temptations of the world, and moral teaching too, which with its watering washes away, and with the stream of its fountain wipes clean, the image of worldly pleasure smeared, as it were, with harlots' paints.

25. About natural things too you have it said in Ecclesiastes: I made myself pools of water, to water from them a sprouting grove. Nor should it trouble you that he put pools in place of a well, for Moses too spoke of the well of Breadth, because he who has transcended this world with a devout mind is freed from all care and from straits. Not without reason, then, does Ecclesiastes have pools, he who saw that there is no abundance under the sun, but that, if anyone wishes to abound, he should abound in Christ.

26. There remains for us the well of mystical things, which we also find in the Song of Songs, where Scripture says: a fountain of gardens, a well of living water, and a rushing stream coming down from Lebanon. For if you pursue the depth of the mysteries, it seems to you a well, since mystical wisdom lies as it were in the deep; but if you wish to draw the abundance of love, which is greater and richer than faith and hope, then it is a fountain for you. For love overflows, so that you can both draw it close at hand and water with its abundance your garden, overflowing with spiritual fruits. And because he who has love is beyond the very well of Breadth, therefore he said that where love is, there a great rushing stream comes down from Lebanon. And lest it trouble anyone that he called the same thing both a well and a fountain, let the Gospel too instruct you, in which it is written that Jesus came to a city of Samaria which is called Sychar, near the estate which Jacob gave to his son Joseph. Now Jacob's fountain was there. And being wearied it says he was sitting thus upon the well. Hence we recognize there too that this well is to be referred to mystical teaching, because there that Samaritan woman, that is, the guardian, and the guardian of the heavenly precepts, drew divine mysteries from that well, learning that God is spirit and is worshipped not in a place but in spirit, and that the Messiah is Christ, and that therefore he who is still awaited by the Jews has already come. When she had heard these things, that woman, who bears the figure of the Church, came to know the mysteries of the Law and believed.

27. In the very book of the Song of Songs, too, Solomon clearly expressed this threefold wisdom, although in Proverbs he said that whoever wished to hear his wisdom should write it down for himself threefold. So in the Song the bride says of the bridegroom: behold, you are handsome, my kinsman, and indeed beautiful: our resting place is shaded, the beams of our houses are cedars, our ceilings are cypresses. We can take this of moral things. For where do Christ and the Church rest but in the works of his people? Indeed, where there was unchastity, where there was pride, where there was iniquity, there the Lord Jesus says: but the Son of Man has nowhere to lay his head.

28. But what do we take about natural things? In his shadow she says I desired, and I sat down, and his fruit was sweet in my throat. For he who passes beyond earthly things, and to whom worldly things are dead, since the world is crucified to him and he to the world, despises and flees all the things that are under the sun.

29. About mystical things too she says: bring me into the house of wine, set love in order in me. For as a vine embraces its vineyard, so the Lord Jesus, as an eternal vine, embraces his people with certain arms of love.

30. Consider each in turn. In moral things there is the flower, the lily among thorns, as he himself says: I am the flower of the field and the lily of the valleys. In moral things, then, there is the flower; in natural things the sun of justice, which in rising and rising again gives light, and in setting casts shadow — beware lest it set for you, for it is written: let not the sun go down upon your anger — in mystical things there is love, because Christ is the fullness of the Law. And therefore the Church, which loves Christ, is wounded by love.

31. This love, therefore, she rouses and rouses again, until she receives his voice and summons his presence, because when sought he not only comes, but comes leaping, leaping upon the mountains, leaping over the hills. He leaps upon souls of greater grace, he leaps over those of lesser. Or thus: how does he come leaping? With a kind of leap he came into this world. He was with the Father; he came into the Virgin, and from the Virgin he leapt across into the manger. He was in the manger, and he shone from heaven; he went down into the Jordan, went up on the cross, went down into the tomb, rose from the tomb and sat at the right hand of the Father. Thence, like a fawn of the harts, which longs for the fountains of waters, he came down to Paul and shone round about him, and sprang upon the holy Church, which is Bethel, which is called the house of God; for the calling of Paul is the strength of the Church.

32. So he comes, and first he is behind the wall, so as to dissolve the enmities of soul and body by taking away the wall, which seemed to bring a hindrance to concord. Then he looks out through the windows. What the windows are, hear the prophet saying: the windows from heaven have been opened. So he means the prophets, through whom the Lord looked upon the human race before he himself came down to earth.

33. And even today, if any soul seeks him much, she will gain much mercy, since to him who seeks much, very much is owed. If, then, any soul seeks him more zealously, she hears his voice from afar, and although she inquires of others, she hears his voice before those very ones of whom she inquires. She sees him coming to her leaping, that is, hastening and running and leaping over those who with a weak heart cannot grasp his power. Then, by reading the prophets and holding fast to their words, she sees him looking out through the enigmas of the prophets; she sees him looking out, but as through a window, not yet as present. She sees him standing out above the nets. What is this, unless perhaps that those nets are for us, not for him? Nets, because that soul is still among sensible and worldly things, which are accustomed to capture the mind of man and wrap it in a kind of fold of their own. To her, then, while she is still placed among worldly things but yet seeks him, he shows himself through the nets.

34. Indeed he says to a soul of this kind: arise, come, my nearest one, that is, arise from the delights of the world, arise from earthly things and come to me, you who still labour and are burdened, because you are anxious about the things of the world. Come above the world, come to me, because I have overcome the world. Come near, already beautiful with the beauty of eternal life, already a dove, that is, gentle and meek, already wholly of spiritual grace. Rightly, then, she ought no longer to fear the nets, when it is he who calls the soul to himself who could not be caught by the temptations and nets of the world. For while we men walk in the midst of snares, exposed through our appetite for food to nets and snares alike, he, though placed in the body, did not fear the nets, but stood out above the nets, that is, above the temptations of the world and the passions of the body; indeed, he made others too rise above them. And therefore, wishing to establish this soul as well, he said: arise, come, my nearest one, do not fear the nets.

35. Now winter has passed, that is, the Pasch has come, pardon has come, the remission of sins has arrived, temptation has ceased, the rain has gone, the storm has gone, the shaking. Before the coming of Christ it is winter; after his coming there are flowers. Hence he says: flowers have appeared on the earth. Where before there were thorns, there now are flowers. The time he says of cutting has come. Where before there was desert, there is harvest. The voice of the turtle-dove has been heard in our land. Well did the prophet add our, as though marvelling that where before there was unchastity, there is chastity.

36. The fig tree has put forth its unripe figs. The tree which before, as though fruitless, was ordered to be cut down, has now begun to bear fruit. But why do you hesitate because he said unripe figs? It shakes off the earlier ones so as to bear better ones later, just as the fruit of the synagogue is cast off, while that of the Church is renewed.

37. And although there is full calm and the mysteries have grown to maturity, yet he says again: arise, secure in the shelter of the rock, that is, safe in the protection of my passion and in the defence of faith; they sucked indeed honey from the rock and oil from the firmest rock. Clothed with this shelter, the souls of the devout are no longer naked, and this is their outer wall. And therefore he says to this soul too: and come, you, my dove, in the shelter of the rock, close to the outer wall, show me your face and let me hear your voice. He urges her to confidence, that she may not be ashamed of the cross of Christ nor of his seal; he urges her to confession; he wishes all snares to be removed, so that the good fragrance of faith may breathe, so that the day may shine, so that the shadow of the hostile night may do no harm, since he who is close to Christ says: the night is far spent, the day has drawn near. There is both the shadow of worldly things, which passes away, and the day of heavenly things, Christ, who shines for his saints. This soul receives good pledges of love.

Chapter V

38. But because we ought always to be watchful, always intent, and because the Word of God leaps forth like a roe or like a fawn of the harts, let the soul always keep watch and reach forward, the soul that seeks him again and desires to hold him. Therefore, as though he had slipped away from her, On my bed she says in the nights I sought him whom my soul loved. Let him who seeks well seek on the bed, let him seek in the nights. Let neither the day be a holiday nor the nights; let no time be free from the duty of piety, and, if she has not found him at first, let her persevere in seeking. And so she says: I will rise therefore and seek in the city, in the marketplace, in the streets. And perhaps she has not yet found him for this reason, that she sought in the marketplace, where there are lawsuits, and in the streets, where there are fairs of goods for sale; for Christ is not bought with any money.

39. We can also understand it thus: she seeks Christ on the bed who seeks him with tranquillity, with peace; she seeks him in the nights, because he spoke in parables — he made, you see, darkness his hiding place, and night to night shows knowledge —, and then because the things we say in our heart ought also to prick us with compunction in our beds. But not even so did she find him, and so she says: I will rise, that is, I will raise up and lift my attention, so as to seek tirelessly, to seek anxiously; I will enter into the city. There is also a soul which says: I am a fortified city, I am a besieged city. There is a city fortified through Christ; there is that city, Jerusalem in heaven, in which interpreters of the divine Law and men skilled in its teaching abound: through them the Word of God is sought. I will seek she says in the marketplace of that city, in that marketplace where those learned in the law treat of rights, where the oil is sold which the virgins of the Gospel buy, so that their torches may always shine and the smoke of iniquity may not put them out. I will seek she says in the streets, in which the waters overflow that burst forth from those fountains from which Solomon says we must drink.

40. While she seeks Christ, then, she finds the watchmen, who are in ministry: of them she inquired. But the soul that seeks God passes even the watchmen; for there are mysteries which even the angels long to see. Hence Peter says: They have been announced he says to you through those who preached the gospel to you by the Holy Spirit sent from heaven, into whom the angels long to look. Therefore whoever has passed the watchmen finds the Word. John passed them, who found the Word with the Father.

41. There are also many who seek Christ in leisure and do not find him, and they find him in persecutions, and find him quickly. And so, as if after temptations, because he is present in the perils of his faithful, How little a while it was she says, when I passed from them, and I found him. I held him and did not let him go. For everyone who seeks finds, and whoever has found ought to cling to him, so that he may not lose him.

42. And since through the Gospel we see the heavenly mysteries figured on earth, let us come to that Mary, let us come to Magdalene. Let us consider how they sought Christ on the bed of his body, in which he lay dead, in the nights, when the angel said to them: You seek Jesus, who was crucified: he is not here; for he has risen. Why then do you seek the living with the dead? Why do you seek in the tomb him who is already in heaven? Why do you seek in the bonds of burial him who looses the bonds of all? Not a tomb but heaven is his seat. And so one of them says: I sought him and did not find him.

43. Yet while they are going to tell the apostles, he had pity on them as they sought, and Jesus met them, saying: Hail. And they came up and held his feet and worshipped him. Jesus is held, then, but he is delighted to be held so, because he is held by faith. In fact that woman too delighted him who touched him and was healed of her flow of blood, of whom he said: Someone touched me; for I feel that power has gone out from me. Touch him, then, and hold him by faith, and clasp his feet faithfully, so that power may go out from him and heal your soul. Even if he says: Do not touch me, hold him; for I have not yet ascended to my Father he said. Once he said: Do not touch me, when he had risen, or perhaps he said it to her who thought he had been taken away by theft and not raised by his own power. In fact in another book you have it that to those who were holding his feet and worshipping he said: Do not fear. Hold him, then, you too, O soul, as Mary also held him, and say: I have held him and will not let him go, as both of them were saying: we hold you. Go to the Father, but do not leave Eve behind, lest she fall again. Lead her with you, no longer wandering but holding the tree of life. Snatch her up as she clings to your feet, so that she may ascend with you. Do not let me go, lest the serpent again pour out his venom, lest he again seek to bite the woman's heel, so as to supplant Adam. Let your soul say, then: I hold you and will bring you into the house of my mother and into the secret chamber of her who conceived me, that I may know your mysteries, that I may drink in your sacraments. Receive, then, Eve, no longer covered with fig leaves but clothed with the Holy Spirit and glorious with new grace, because she no longer hides as one stripped naked, but comes to meet you as one encompassed by the splendour of a shining garment, because grace clothes her. But neither was Adam naked at first, when innocence clothed him.

44. So the daughters of Jerusalem, seeing her clinging to Christ and still ascending with him — for he deigns often to come to meet those who seek him and to come down to them, so as to lift them up — say: Who is this who comes up from the desert? — this place on earth seems a desert and uncultivated, overgrown with the briars and thorns of our sins —, marvelling, that is, how the soul, which before was left in the underworld, clings to the Word of God and has ascended like a shoot of the vine raising itself to the heights, like smoke born of fire and seeking the heights, and besides how she is fragrant with good fragrances. Now that fragrance breathes the sweetness of devout prayer, which is directed like incense in the sight of God. And in the Apocalypse we read that the smoke of the incense went up with the prayers of the saints, which incense, namely the prayers of the saints, is borne by an angel upon that golden altar which is before the throne, and it is fragrant like a sweet ointment of devout supplication, because it is composed of petition for eternal and invisible things, not for bodily ones; above all, however, she is redolent of myrrh and frankincense, because she is dead to sin and lives to God.

45. Seeing her, then, ascending and not resisting, and delighted by the fragrances of her merits, recognizing too that she is the bride of that Solomon, the peaceful, they follow her with a zealous escort up to the couch of Solomon, because true rest in Christ is owed to her. For Christ is the couch of the saints, on which the hearts of all, weary with the battles of the world, find rest. On this couch Isaac rested and blessed his younger son, saying: The elder shall serve the younger; reclining on this couch Jacob blessed the twelve patriarchs; reclining on this couch the daughter of the ruler of the synagogue rose from death; lying on this couch the widow's dead son, called by the voice of Christ, broke the bonds of death.

46. When the bride, then, has been led to the bridegroom's place of rest, they sing a nuptial song, telling of love from the daughters of Jerusalem: Go forth and see in King Solomon, in the crown with which his mother crowned him on the day of his espousals. They sing an epithalamium and call the other powers of the heavens, or souls, that they may see the love which Christ has toward the daughters of Jerusalem. Hence he also merited to be crowned by his mother as the son of love, as Paul shows when he says that God has rescued us from the power of darkness and transferred us into the kingdom of the Son of his love. So the Son of love is himself also love, having love not as an accident but always having it in his substance, like the kingdom, of which he says: In this I was born. And so they say: Go forth, that is, go out from the cares and thoughts of the world, go out from bodily straits, go out from the vanities of the world, and see what love the peaceful King has on the day of his espousals, how glorious he is, because he gave resurrection to bodies and joined souls to himself. This is the crown of a great contest, this the splendid gift of Christ's espousals: his blood and passion. For what more could he give who did not spare even himself, but offered his death for us?

47. The Lord Jesus himself too, delighted by the faith of this soul, by her confession and grace, and having praised her merits, calls her nearer, saying: Come here from Lebanon, bride, come here from Lebanon: you will pass and pass through, from the beginning of faith, from the head of Senir and Hermon, from the dens of lions, from the mountains of leopards, that is: go out from the body and strip yourself wholly; for you cannot be present with me unless you first go abroad from the body, since those who are in the flesh are abroad from the kingdom of God. Come here he says, come here. He did well to repeat it, because whether present or absent you ought to be present with the Lord your God and to please him. Be present when present, be present when absent, even if you are still in the body. For all are present to me whose faith is with me. He is present to me who goes out from the world; he is present to me who thinks of me, looks upon me, hopes in me, to whom I am the portion; he is present to me who has been absent from himself, he is present to me who has denied himself. He is with me who is not within himself, since he who is in the flesh is not in the spirit. He is with me who goes out from himself, he is near me who has been outside himself, he is whole for me who has lost his soul for my sake. And so: Come here, come here, bride: you will pass and pass through, from the beginning of faith. She passes and passes through from the earth, and she passes through who reaches Christ. She passes by the merit of faith and the brightness of works, which shine like Senir and Hermon, that is, the way of the lamp; she passes, having overcome the temptations of the world and conquered the spiritual wickednesses, seeking the crown of the lawful contest, and so she has deserved to be praised with Christ as judge.

48. An enclosed garden is my sister, my bride, an enclosed garden, a sealed fountain; your shoots are a paradise of pomegranates with the fruit of henna. The bride is praised because she is a garden having in herself the fragrance of that full field of which Isaac says: The fragrance of my son is as the fragrance of a full field. So the good soul is fragrant with the fragrances of righteousness. And perhaps the field is the patriarch, and the garden is the soul of someone lower, a portion, as it were, of the field; and an enclosed garden, lest it be overrun by beasts, and a sealed fountain, she who by the integrity of the seal and by persevering faith has washed away her own sins. For whoever takes it of the Church has something to refer to the grace of virginity, because, placed in the paradise of delight, she gathers spiritual fruits without labour, since the souls of the patriarchs, with a kind of rustic labour, bring her their fruits, whereby she may obtain an everlasting sweetness. She is rightly called a sealed fountain, because the image of the invisible God is impressed in her. They praise also the gifts of the soul, which were sent to the bridegroom, with which she came endowed. Now the dowry of the devout soul is good fragrances, myrrh, aloes, saffron, by which the grace of gardens breathes and the stench of sins is done away.

49. So, secure in such great praise, she asks that the north wind, a heavy wind, be still, lest it shake down the flowers, and that the south wind blow; that is, she wants winter to pass and the mildness of a softer breeze to bring the spring. She invites the bridegroom into her garden. The bridegroom comes down and, delighted by the variety of her fruit, rejoices that he has found stronger food and found sweeter food too. For there is, as it were, a kind of bread of the word, and a honey: one discourse is more forceful, another persuasive. There is also one faith more fervent, like wine, another clearer, as is the juice of milk. On this food Christ feasts in us, this cup he drinks, and by the inebriation of his drink he summons us to make our going out from lower things to the better and the best.

Chapter VI

50. Hearing this, the soul drank in the inebriation of the heavenly mysteries and, as if put to sleep by wine and as though placed in a going out of herself or in a stupor, she says: I sleep, and my heart keeps watch. Then, struck by the light of the Word present, when she had rested with her eyes lowered, she is roused by the Word. Now this is already the fourth stage of progress of the soul. For first, impatient with love and not bearing the delays of the Word, she was asking that she might merit kisses, and she merited to see the one she desired, and she was also brought into the chamber of the King. Second, when she was exchanging words with him, she rested in his shadow, and suddenly the Word departed from her in the middle of their talk; yet he was not long absent from her as she sought him, but came leaping over the mountains and bounding over the hills. And not long after, like a roe or a fawn of the harts, while he was speaking to his beloved, he leapt away and left her. Third, when she had sought him on her bed and in the nights, in the city and the marketplace and the streets, and had not found him, at length she called him back by her prayers and grace, so much so that she was even called nearer by the bridegroom. Fourth, she herself is now roused by him as she sleeps, although she was keeping watch in her heart, so that she heard at once the voice of him who knocked. But she suffered a delay while she rises, because she could not keep pace with the swiftness of the Word; while she opens the door, the Word passed by, and she went out at his word, and him whom she sought through wounds, but wounds of love, she at last, and barely, found and held, so as not to lose him afterwards. In sum, I have touched on these things in a brief discourse; now let us examine them one by one.

51. Even if you sleep, provided only that he knows the devotion of your soul, Christ comes and knocks at its door and says: Open to me, my sister. Well does he say sister, because the nuptials of the Word and the soul are spiritual. For souls do not know the covenants of marriage and the use of bodily union, but are like the angels in heaven. Open he says to me, but close to strangers. Close to this age, close to the world, and do not yourself go out to those material things, nor, leaving your own light, seek another's, because material light pours in a gloomy darkness, so that the light of true glory is not seen. Open, then, to me; do not open to the adversary, nor give place to the devil. Open yourself to me; do not be narrowed, but be enlarged, and I will fill you. And because, having gone over the whole world, I found more of trouble and offence, and did not easily find where to rest, therefore do you open, so that the Son of man may lay his head in you, he who has no rest except upon the humble and meek.

52. The soul, hearing Open to me and a head full of dew, that is, suddenly troubled by worldly temptations, and as if about to rise, she who was bidden to rise, says, while she was fragrant with aloes and myrrh, the tokens of burial: I have put off my tunic; how shall I put it on? I have washed my feet; how shall I soil them? For she fears lest she rise again into temptations, lest she return again into fault and sin, and begin to soil her goings out and the advances of her virtues with earthly footprints. Certainly in this way too she shows the perfection of her virtue, she who merited so great a love of Christ that he comes to her and knocks at her door, and comes with the Father and sups with that same soul, and she with him, as John said in the Apocalypse. For when she had heard above: Come here from Lebanon, bride, come here from Lebanon, and when she observed that she could not be present to Christ in the flesh, but would be present then if she was present in the spirit, conforming herself to his will, so that she too might be conformed to the image of Christ, she no longer feels the cast-off spoils of the flesh; now, like a spirit, she has stripped herself of union with the body; now, as one who has forgotten and who could not remember that union even if she wished, she says: I have put off my tunic; how shall I put it on? For she has put off that tunic of skins which Adam and Eve received after their fault, the tunic of corruption, the tunic of the passions. How shall I put it on? She does not seek to put it on, but signifies that it has been so cast aside that it can no longer be a garment for her. I have washed my feet; how shall I soil them? That is: I washed my footsteps while I went out and raised myself from my cohabitation with the body, from that connection and intimacy of fleshly embrace; how shall I soil them by returning into the enclosure of the body and that dark prison of its passions?

53. While she says this, the Word sent his working as through an opening, not yet face to face; he sent it as a hand. And my womb she says was troubled over him. And I rose to open to my brother. My hands dripped myrrh, my fingers are full of myrrh upon the hands of the bolt. Let us consider what this signifies. First, as I said, God the Word is seen in his works as through an opening, not fully and perfectly. Then love increases and what has been conceived grows, and from his seeds, which the soul has received in a kind of intelligible womb, she desires to see the whole fullness of his divinity dwelling in him bodily, as we read. She rose to see that Word of God more closely. And in this very thing her advance is signified, that she rose through vigour and virtue. For by the presence of the Word the soul drew in virtue, just as the presence of Mary, when she was heavy with child, so instructed John, placed in the womb, that he leapt in the womb and exulted, recognizing the presence of the Lord. She rose to open, and her works and deeds were mortified to the world. For such ought the soul to be which is to receive the Word, that it die to the world and be buried together in Christ; for so is Christ found, and such a lodging he seeks for himself. Then the very ministers of her workings, that is, the hands and fingers by which Christ is grasped, are mortified; these fingers we can take to be the works, the prominent parts as it were, of our deeds. And so, as if from her embrace, when she was already spreading out her intelligible hands and fingers to grasp the Word, the devout soul says that it had passed by her, but not yet passed through her. And this marks an advance, when the Word of God passes by and passes through the soul, because it is written: And a sword shall pass through your own soul, that the thoughts of many hearts may be revealed. Here she is still passed by, not passed through; like Mary, she is perhaps passed through in the later verses, where the Lord Jesus is set like a seal in her midst.

54. Next, at once, there is another advance of the passing Word, because the soul went out at his word, that is, following his word she went out from the body, raising herself from its dwelling and making herself a stranger to it, that she might be present with God and be a fellow citizen of the saints; for we cannot at the same time be of the household of the flesh and of God. Therefore in this place the soul is said, as I said, to go out when she withdraws herself from the pleasures of the body. Indeed it is written: Go out from Babylon, fleeing from the Chaldaeans. The Hebrew is admonished by the prophetic address to flee, not of course the regions of Babylon, but its ways, since there are Hebrews who are in Babylon and yet show by their ways that they have gone out from Babylon. For those too of whom the prophet says that they sat by the rivers of Babylon were indeed sitting in the region of Babylon, but they were not in its vices and confusion. For how were they in that confusion of vices who wept, doing penance because they had fallen from the ark of devotion and faith and from the merits of their fathers' virtue? But the soul that goes out in the Word seeks the Word.

55. Hence she too, while she was seeking, fell in with the watchmen who go about the city. They struck me she says and wounded me; the watchmen of the walls took my mantle from me. Well indeed was she coming, as a bride, with a mantle with which to veil her head when the bridegroom came to meet her. Just as Rebecca, on learning that Isaac was coming to meet her, got down from the camel and covered herself with a mantle, so this soul was putting on beforehand the tokens of the nuptial garment, lest perhaps she be cast out as not having a wedding garment, or in order to veil her head because of the angels; but they struck her, so that she might be proved more fully; for souls are exercised by temptations. They took her mantle from her, inquiring whether she brought the true beauty of naked virtue, or because one ought to enter that heavenly city without a covering, bringing no coverings of disguise with oneself. There are also those who inquire lest any soul carry with it the cast-off spoils of fleshly allurement and bodily concupiscence. She is stripped of her mantle when her conscience is laid bare. But there is also one who is stripped to good effect, to whom it is granted to imitate him who says: For the prince of this world is coming, and in me he will find nothing, surely because in him alone he finds nothing who did not commit sin. Blessed too is that soul in whom he finds nothing grave or much, but finds in her the cloak of faith and the discipline of wisdom.

56. So without loss to herself, because even if someone wishes, he cannot take away true wisdom — even if the adversary makes an uproar, yet there the true integrity of an innocent life shines out — without loss, then, she passed the watchmen and, mingling with the daughters of that heavenly city, she seeks the Word, and by seeking she stirs up his love within herself, and she recognizes where she should seek the Word. She knows that he lingers among the prayers of the saints and clings to them, and she understands that he pastures his Church, or the souls of his righteous, among the lilies. This mystery the Lord showed you in the Gospel, when on the sabbath he led his disciples through the sown fields. Moses led the people of the Jews through the desert; Christ leads through the sown fields, Christ leads through the lilies, because through his passion too the desert blossoms like the lily. Let us follow, then, so that on the sabbath day, that great sabbath in which there is great rest, we may gather fruits. And do not fear lest the Pharisees accuse you as you gather from the sown fields. Even if they accuse, yet Christ excuses, and makes the souls he wishes, which follow him, like that David who, above the law, ate the loaves of the presentation, already then foreseeing in his prophetic mind the sacraments of the new grace.

Chapter VII

57. So she is praised by the bridegroom because she has sought him so well and so steadfastly, and therefore she is now not only called sister but is also named well pleasing, as pleasing to him who was pleasing to the Father, and beautiful as Jerusalem, a wonder like ranks set in order, because she holds all the mysteries of the eternal city and is a wonder to all who see her, since she is full like equity and perfect; and having borrowed her brightness from the light of the Word, while she always strains toward it, she also becomes terrible by a certain order, carried forward to the summit of the virtues. And so, as to one now perfect, he says: Turn your eyes away from me, do not strain against me. Since by her exceeding devotion and faith she has passed beyond the capacity of her nature and of her own condition, and since it is a heavy thing to gaze face to face upon the unapproachable light, Turn he says your eyes away from me, because she cannot bear the fullness of his divinity and the splendor of the true light. Yet we can also take Turn your eyes away from me in this way: though you are perfect, other souls still have to be redeemed by me, others to be propped up; for by looking at me you lift me up, but I came down for this reason, that I might lift up all. Although I have risen and have the Father's seat, yet I will not leave you orphans, as though bereft of a father's protection, but I will strengthen you by my presence. You have this written in the Gospel: Behold, I am with you even to the consummation of the world. Turn your eyes away from me, then, because you lift me up. For the more one strains toward the Lord, the more he lifts up the Lord, and is himself lifted up. Hence that man too says: I will exalt you, O Lord, for you have taken me up; for the holy man exalts the Lord, the sinner humbles him. So he wants her to turn her eyes away, lest, regarding her as one who can already follow to higher things, he should be lifted up and leave the other souls behind. That is why in the Gospel too he showed his glory not to all the disciples, but to the more perfect. Now picture some teacher who wishes to open an obscure matter to his hearers: although he himself is powerful in speech and knowledge, he nevertheless comes down to the ignorance of those who do not understand, and uses simple, plainer and ordinary speech, so that he can be understood. Whoever, then, among the hearers is livelier of mind, one who can follow easily, lifts him up and shakes him off. Seeing him, the teacher calls him back, so that he may rather allow the teacher to linger over humbler and plainer things, in order that the others too may be able to follow.

58. As for Aquila's rendering, sounding as one unveiled, by sounding he pointed to the wonder she deserves, as one who has great and resounding works, and by unveiled to the brightness of her works, or else to the fact that the works of her soul shine before the Father who is in heaven. Hence you understand that it was not idly that her mantle was taken from her, so that, laid open by her merits and bare, she might shine.

59. She is praised besides because she is faithful, because she is powerful in word, because she is fruitful with various fruits, because she is one, like a dove, having the unity of the Spirit, in which there is the peace that has made both one, and because she is not composed of diverse elements of a divided and warring nature. For what is so diverse as fire and water, air and earth, of which the frame of our body consists? But every soul that is simple is blessed, the soul that imitates him who says: that all may be one, as you, Father, in me and I in you, that they too may be one in us; for this is the consummation and the perfection. Hence he also added: that they may be one, as we also are one, I in them and you in me, that they may be consummated into one. This, then, is the soul that is a dove and perfect, which, being simple and spiritual, is not troubled by the passions of this body, in which there are fightings without, fears within. In fact Scripture teaches us that concord and peace are signified by this word of unity, saying: And the multitude of believers had one soul and one heart, and there was no division among them at all.

60. Nor is it idly that the soul is praised for fruitfulness, not only because she is fruitful in virtues, but also because she has nothing evil in her. For that is comely and beautiful in which there is nothing evil; for what is good is comely, and what is uncomely is evil. Fruitfulness in good works is beautiful. The opposite of beauty, therefore, is barrenness, since in one who is deprived of beauty or comeliness there is indeed evil; and what is evil is barren and unfruitful. Of this what clearer proof is there than nature? For land that is good is fertile and fruitful, but land that is bad is hungry and barren; and land that is fertile is comely. For what is more beautiful than a full field, when the crop waves, when the fruits glow red, or when garlands of grapes hang down, or the olive bends laden with berries, or the tops of the mountains and the low places of the valleys are clothed with green grass? And to use the testimony of Scripture, Jacob was comely, and therefore he was the fragrance of a full field; Esau was hairy and uncomely, and therefore he was a rustic, one who could bear no fruits. Of the Lord himself too, after he had made the fruitfulness of the Church rise up, it was beautifully said: The Lord has reigned, he has put on comeliness, and elsewhere: You have put on confession and beauty. It is clear, then, that what is fruitful is comely, what is unfruitful uncomely. The case of the soul is like that of the soil, for that soul is comely which is fruitful in merits, fruitful in counsels, and that one uncomely which is barren. For the infirmities of the soul are barrenness and matter; for barrenness cheats her of her fruit, brings in want, strikes in fear, kindles desires and vain opinions; thus the soul falls. What, then, is evil but the lack of the good? For she is cheated of her own and stands in need of what is another's; she is emptied and filled without any measure or limit. But material vices overshadow the grace of the soul. Ignorance and concupiscence are diseases of the soul, but they are to be referred to form rather than to matter. Matter is the flesh; form is ignorance and concupiscence. Why then is the flesh accused, when such great stains lie in the form? Because form can do nothing without matter. In fact the form of an axe does nothing without matter. For what would concupiscence be, if the flesh did not inflame it? It is cold in old men, and in boys too, because in them the body is weak; it burns in young men, in whom the strength of the body is at the boil. Evils, then, have arisen from goods; for nothing is evil except what is deprived of goods. Yet through evils it came about that goods stood out. So the lack of the good is the root of evil, and evil is detected by the definition of the good, since evil is found out through the knowledge of the good. But the Good needs nothing, is sufficient to itself, and gives measure and perfection, and an end as well, to all things; in it all things hold together, and on it all things hang. This is the nature of the Good, which fills the mind.

61. About this the pure soul is busied; this she looks into within, and she beholds God and abounds in all good things. Hence she also says: His throat is sweetness, and he is wholly desire. For God is the author of all good things, and the things that are his are surely all things. Nowhere is there evil there, and if our mind remains in him, it knows no evil. So the soul that does not remain in God is herself the author of her evils, and therefore she sins; and the soul that sins shall herself die, for, loosed from the golden chains of the virtues, she is carried headlong down the steep and slides to lower things. But that soul is blessed which no hostile battles of the body overcome. This soul, like a sparrow, flies away when the snare is broken; for the pleasures of the body are the baits of evils. Whoever strains after these entangles his soul in a snare,

62. but he who refrains from its baits and goes out from its darkness, his soul shines like the dawn, the soul of which it is said: Who is this that looks forth like the dawn, beautiful as the moon? For she looks forth as from a free house, and does not say: Darkness covers me and walls surround me, and who knows whether the Most High sees? But rather she seeks the light: as though set in the upper rooms of her house, that is, of her body, and above the world, she gazes upon divine things and lifts herself up to the eternal, so as to be present with God, now carrying the light of her works around the whole world, as the moon carries its disk.

63. As for Aquila's rendering, sounding as the sun, it seems that the turning of the heavenly axis and the course of the sun and moon and stars and the concord of the spheres are expressed — and so it seems to some of our own writers too — an idea which, although it has not won belief, does not seem out of place, at least for the charm of its sweetness.

Chapter VIII

64. While she is being praised by the bridegroom, she modestly shrinks from being praised to her face; then, called back by the bridegroom's love, she says: I went down into the garden of nuts to see in the birth of the torrent. For where is the Church but where the rod and the priestly grace flower? There she often is, that she may be tested in bitternesses and temptations. By the nut we understand bitternesses, by the torrent temptations, but such as can be borne, for it is written: Our soul has passed through the torrent. And so she goes down into the place of bitterness, where the vine flowers, and the fruit, diverse and manifold in the form of pomegranates, which, as under one covering for the whole body, is protected by faith and love. In that bitterness, then, the soul did not know herself; for the corruptible body weighs down the soul, and the earthly dwelling is quickly bowed down. Yet she ought always to know herself. But Peter too was tempted, Peter too did not know himself; for if he had known himself, he would not have denied his Author. But Christ knew him; in fact he who also looked back at him knew him — for the Lord knows those who are his — and, as his own, he called him back from his fall with the reins of his mercy, like a good driver. Our driver, then, is Christ.

65. And so the soul says: It made me the chariot of Aminadab. The soul, then, is a chariot, which bears a good driver. If the soul is a chariot, it has horses, either good or bad. The good horses are the virtues of the soul, the bad horses are the passions of the body. So the good driver restrains and checks the bad horses and urges on the good. The good horses are four: prudence, temperance, fortitude, justice; the bad horses are anger, concupiscence, fear, wickedness. Sometimes the horses themselves are at odds with one another, and either anger pulls forward or fear, and they hinder one another and slow the course. But the good horses fly off and raise themselves from the earth to the heights and lift the soul up, above all if they have the sweet yoke and the light burden of him who says: Take my yoke upon you; for my yoke is sweet and my burden light. He is the driver who knows how to guide his own horses, so that the course of all may be even. If prudence is swifter, justice slower, he prods the more sluggish with his own whip. If temperance is milder, fortitude harsher, he knows how to yoke together those that disagree, lest they break up their chariot. And so one may, in a spectacle seen by the mind, see each soul snatched up to heaven in the highest contest, the horses hurrying to be the first to reach the prize of Christ, that the palm may the sooner be set upon their necks. These horses are subject to the yoke of faith, bound by the bond of love, by the reins of justice, by the halters of sobriety. Beautifully, then, she says: It made me the chariot of Aminadab, that is, of the father of the people. And he who is the father of the people is also the father of Nahshon, that is, of the serpent. Now call to mind who hung on the cross like a serpent for the salvation of all, and you will understand that that soul is peaceful which has God the Father as her guardian and Christ as her charioteer, for this name too is written in our books: Father, father, charioteer of Israel.

66. This charioteer, then, says: Turn, O Shulammite, turn. Well does he say, as a charioteer to his chariot: Turn, O Shulammite, that is, the peaceful one. For the soul that is peaceful quickly turns and corrects herself, even if she has sinned before, and Christ all the more mounts her and deigns to drive her, he to whom it is said: Mount your horses, and your riding is salvation, and elsewhere: I sent your horses † Tharsis. These are the horses of Christ. So Christ mounts his horses; the Word of God mounts devout souls.

67. Know from this that he has mounted this soul too and brought her to the place of the palm, when he also says to her: How have you become beautiful and sweet, O love, in your delights? Your stature has become like a palm. And she herself says: I said: I will go up into the palm. But love itself is also the palm; for it is the fullness of victory, for love is the fullness of the Law. Let us run, then, that we may lay hold of it; let us run, that we may conquer. He who has conquered goes up into the palm and eats its fruits. He who has conquered no longer runs, but sits, as it is written: He who has conquered, I will grant him to sit with me on my seat, as I too have conquered and sit with my Father on his seat. Hence the philosophers set forth in their books those chariot contests of souls, and yet they could not reach the palm, since their souls did not know the summit and the loftiness of the Word. This loftiness the soul knew in whom was the turning of the Word.

68. For thus she says: I am my brother's, and his turning is toward me. She repeated this thought three times, in different ways, in the Song of Songs. At the beginning she says: My brother is mine and I am his, who pastures in the lilies, until the day breathes and the shadows are removed. Then she says: I am my brother's and my brother is mine, who pastures among the lilies. At the end she says: I am my brother's and his turning is toward me. The first refers to the beginning of the soul — and that is why she also put first My brother is mine; for when he shows himself, the soul too has taken up the desire of cleaving to God — what follows to her progress, the third to her perfection. In the first, as at the beginning, the soul still sees shadows, not yet stirred by the revelation of the Word drawing near, and therefore the day of the Gospel was not yet shining for her. In the second, without the confusion of shadows, she gathers holy fragrances. In the third, now perfect, she provides rest for the Word within herself, so that he may turn toward her and lay down his head and rest; and now, holding her reward, she invites him whom she had sought before and could not find to her field, saying:

69. Come, my brother, let us go out into the field, let us rest in the villages. Above, she invited him to the garden; here, to the field, which has not only the grace of flowers but also wheat and barley, that is, the supports of more solid virtues, so that he may look upon its fruits. Let us rest she says in the villages, to which Adam had been banished when he was cast out of paradise. In these he rested, but he worked the earth. The reason why she wants him to go out into the field is plain to the understanding: that, like a good shepherd, he may pasture his flock, lift up the weary, call back the straying. For although this soul has kept for him things new and old, yet there are still those who are like lambs, who have to be nourished on the juice of milk. So, as one perfect, she intercedes not for herself but for others: that he may go out from the bosom of the Father, that he may come forth like a bridegroom going out from his chamber and run the way to win the weak; that he may not linger on that secret throne of the Father and in that light, where the weak cannot follow, but that he may be taken up and brought into the house and the secret chamber of the bride; that he may be outside as far as she is concerned, so as to be within for us; that he may be in our midst, though he is not seen by us.

70. Hence she says: Who will give you to me as a brother, brother, sucking the breasts of my mother? Finding you outside, I will kiss you. Good is the soul that is outside, so that the Word may be within. She is outside the body, so that the Word may dwell in us.

71. I will take you up she says and bring you in. Rightly is the Word of God taken up and brought in, because he knocks at the soul that the door may be opened to him, and unless he finds the door open to him, he does not enter; but if anyone opens the door, he enters and sups. So the bride takes up the Word, that by taking him up she may be taught. Hence not undeservedly she goes up still to higher dwellings and always makes progress.

72. This is what the Virtues signify when they say to the soul: Who is this who comes up made white, leaning on her brother? Earlier they had said: Who is this that looks forth like the dawn, beautiful as the moon, chosen as the sun? Here something more was found to add: that she went up leaning on the Word of God. For the more perfect recline upon Christ, as John too reclined on Christ's breast. So, then, she either leaned upon Christ or reclined herself upon him, or surely, since we are speaking of nuptials, she was already being led into the chamber by the bridegroom, as one given over into Christ's right hand.

73. And because there is now the union of love, the bridegroom speaks tenderly to her and says: Under the apple tree I raised you up. There your mother was in labor with you, there she who bore you was in labor with you. Good is the soul that rests under a tree that is fruitful and of a very good fragrance. For if Nathanael was good, in whom there was no guile, and was seen under a fig tree, surely she is good who was raised up under the apple tree by her bridegroom. It is more to be raised up than to be seen, and more still to be raised up by the bridegroom. For although Nathanael was seen under the tree, yet his soul was not a bride, since he came to Christ secretly, because he feared the Jews. It was not beautiful as the moon, chosen as the sun, since it was in the shade; for a bride marries by day, and confesses publicly. That is why she is under the apple tree, he under the fig, because she spread the fragrance of her confession far and wide, while he had the sweetness of purity and innocence but did not have the fragrance of the spirit.

74. There he says your mother was in labor with you, there she who bore you was in labor with you; for we are born where we are reborn. And those are travailed with in whom the image of Christ is being formed. Hence that man too says: My little children, with whom I am in labor until Christ be formed in you; for he is in labor who receives in his womb the spirit of salvation and pours it into others.

75. Hence, since Christ was now formed in her, he says: Set as a seal upon your heart, as a signet upon your arm. Christ is a seal on the forehead, a seal on the heart: on the forehead, that we may always confess; on the heart, that we may always love; a seal on the arm, that we may always work. Let his image shine, then, in our confession, let it shine in our love, let it shine in our works and deeds, so that, if it can be done, his whole form may be expressed in us. Let him be our head, because the head of the man is Christ; let him be our eye, that through him we may see the Father; let him be our voice, through whom we may speak to the Father; let him be our right hand, through whom we may bring our sacrifice to God the Father; he is also our seal, which is the mark of perfection and of love, because the Father in his love sealed the Son, as we read: whom God the Father sealed. So Christ is our love. Good is the love, when he offered himself to death for sins; good is the love that forgave sins.

76. And therefore let our soul put on love, and love of such a kind as is strong as death, because as death is the end of sins, so too is love, since he who loves the Lord ceases to sin. For love indeed thinks no evil, nor rejoices over iniquity, but bears all things. For how can one who does not seek his own things seek what belongs to others? There is also that death which is strong through the laver, by which all sin is buried and guilt is forgiven. Such was the love which that woman of the Gospel brought, of whom the Lord said: Her sins, which are many, are forgiven, because she loved much. Strong too is that death of the holy martyrs which wipes out former guilt, and therefore strong: a death to which love is not unequal, the love that is made equal to the passion of the martyrs, so as to take away what sins deserve.

77. Jealousy too is as the underworld, since he who has jealousy for God does not spare even his own for Christ's sake. And so love has death, and love has jealousy, and love has wings of fire. In fact Christ, loving Moses, appeared to him in fire, and Jeremiah, having within him the gift of divine love, said: And there was a flaming fire in my bones, and I was undone on every side, and I cannot bear it. Good, then, is love, having wings of burning fire, which flies through the breasts and hearts of the saints and burns up whatever is material and earthly, but proves whatever is pure, and makes better by its fire whatever it has touched. This fire the Lord Jesus sent upon the earth, and faith shone forth, devotion was kindled, love was lit up, justice shone out. With this fire he set the heart of his apostles aflame, as Cleopas testifies, saying: Was not our heart burning within us, while he opened the Scriptures? The wings of fire, then, are the flames of divine Scripture. In fact he opened the Scriptures, and fire went out and pierced the hearts of the hearers. And truly they are wings of fire, because the words of the Lord are pure words, silver tried by fire. When Paul too was taken up by Christ, he saw a light shining round above him and above those who were with him; he fell from fear and rose up more approved; in fact he who had come as a persecutor was made an apostle. The Holy Spirit too came down and filled the whole house, in which a great many were sitting, and there appeared tongues divided as of fire. Good are the wings of love, true wings, which flew through the mouths of the apostles; and wings of fire, which spoke a purified speech. With these wings Enoch flew away, snatched up to heaven; with these wings Elijah flew away, carried up to the heights in a fiery chariot and with fiery horses; with these wings the Lord God led the people of the fathers by the pillar of fire; these wings the Seraphim had when he took a coal of fire from the altar and touched the prophet's mouth and took away his iniquities and purged his sins; by the fire of these wings the sons of Levi were purified, and the peoples of the nations are baptized, as John testifies, saying of the Lord Jesus: He will baptize you in the Spirit and fire. Rightly did David wish his reins and his heart to be burned, for he knew that the fiery wings of love were not to be feared; rightly did the Hebrew youths in the burning furnace not feel the blaze of the fire, because the flame of love kept them cool. And that we may know more fully that perfect love has wings, you heard him saying a while ago: How often have I wished to gather your children, as a hen gathers her chicks under her wings.

78. Let us take up, then, these wings, which, like flames, may direct us upward. Let each one strip his soul of its filthier wrappings and, like gold in the fire, prove it cleansed of mud. For thus the soul is purified, like the finest gold. The beauty of the soul is pure virtue, and her comeliness is the truer knowledge of higher things, so that she may see that Good on which all things depend, while it depends on nothing. By it, then, she lives and receives understanding. For that supreme Good is the fountain of life; love and desire for it are kindled in us; to draw near to it and be mingled with it is pleasure; to one who does not see it, it is an object of desire, and in one who sees it, it is present; and therefore he despises all other things and is soothed and delighted by this. This is what supplies substance to all things, while itself, abiding in itself, it gives to others, but receives nothing into itself from others. Of it the prophet says: I said to my Lord: You are my God, since you have no need of my goods. This alone he desired to see, as he himself says elsewhere: One thing I have asked of the Lord, this will I seek: that I may dwell in the house of the Lord all the days of my life, and see the delight of the Lord, and consider his temple. If, then, anyone has been found worthy to see that pure and incorporeal supreme Good, what else would he have to desire? In fact Peter saw the glory of Christ's resurrection and did not want to come down, saying: Lord, it is good for us to be here. And how much more incomparable is that glory of the divinity and that unapproachable light! If anyone has seen it, what else would he covet? Not kingdoms, not wealth, honors, glory, powers; for in the enjoyment of those there is nothing of blessedness, but to enjoy this is blessed, so that, despising those things, one remains turned toward this. Seeing this beautiful image, then, let one go within and leave the face of the body outside. For he who gazes upon bodies ought not to gaze deep into them, lest, like one sinking in a whirlpool, he be swept away and swallowed up and, as though plunged into the depths, appear nowhere. Let us flee, then, to our truest fatherland. There is our fatherland, and there our Father, by whom we were created, where Jerusalem is, the city which is the mother of all.

79. But what is this flight? Not, surely, one of the feet, which belong to the body; for wherever they run, they run on earth, and they pass from soil to soil. Nor let us flee in ships or chariots or on horses, which are hampered and fall, but let us flee with the mind and with eyes or with inner feet. Let us accustom our eyes to see what is bright and clear, to look upon the face of continence and temperance and all the virtues, in which there is nothing rough, nothing dark and crooked. And let each one look upon himself and his own conscience: let him cleanse that eye, so that it may have no filth; for what is seen ought not to be out of harmony with him who sees, since God willed us to be conformed to the image of his Son. That Good, then, is known to us and is not far from each one of us; for in him we live and are and move; for we are also his offspring, as the Apostle stated that the Gentiles had signified. He is the Good we seek, the only Good; for no one is good but God alone. This is the eye which gazes upon that great and true beauty. The eye does not look upon the sun unless it is healthy and vigorous, nor can any but a good soul see the Good. Let him become good, then, who wishes to see the Lord and that which is good. Let us be like this Good, and let us do in accordance with it the things that are good. This is the Good which is above all activity, above all mind and understanding. It is that which always abides, and to it all things turn, in whom dwells the fullness of the divinity, and through him all things are reconciled to him; and, to define more fully what the Good is, life is the Good, because it always abides, giving life and being to all, because Christ is the fountain of the life of all, of whom the prophet says: In his shadow we shall live; for now our life is hidden in Christ; but when Christ, our life, appears, then we too shall appear with him in glory. So let us not fear death, since it is rest for the body, and for the soul either freedom or release. Nor let us fear him who can kill the flesh but cannot kill the soul, because we do not fear him who can take away a garment, we do not fear him who can steal what is ours but cannot steal us. We, then, are souls, if we wish to be Hebrews, of those who are companions of Jacob, that is, his imitators. We are souls, but our members are garments. The garments must indeed be looked after, so that they are not torn, so that they do not grow old; but he who uses them ought rather to look after and guard himself.
