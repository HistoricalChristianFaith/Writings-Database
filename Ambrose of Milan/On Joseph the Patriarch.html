On Joseph the Patriarch

Latin source: Ambrose's treatise on the patriarch Joseph, in fourteen chapters, reworked from sermons he preached at Milan (about 388), from the critical text of C. Schenkl, Corpus Scriptorum Ecclesiasticorum Latinorum 32/2 (Vienna, 1897), pp. 73–122, verified against the page images and collated with the Benedictine edition as reprinted in Migne, PL 14, with the edition's misprints corrected. Scripture is translated as Ambrose quotes it, from his own Old Latin text; italics mark the Scripture he quotes. The chapter and section numbers are the Maurist ones shared by all editions; the Maurist chapter summaries are not translated. The one psalm verse Ambrose quotes in Greek (Chapter III §17) is kept as printed, with an English rendering in square brackets; Psalms keep the Latin (LXX) numbering, with the Hebrew number in parentheses. Schenkl's supplements are read into the text, the words he brackets as spurious are omitted, the one lacuna (Chapter VI §33) is marked . . . ., and his critical apparatus is not translated. This English translation is released into the public domain.

Chapter I

1. The life of the saints is for all others the rule of living, and therefore we receive the sequence of the Scriptures set out more fully, so that, while by reading we come to know Abraham, Isaac and Jacob and the other just men, we may follow with striving steps a path of innocence, as it were, opened up for us by their virtue. Since I have often treated of them, today the history of holy Joseph presents itself. Though there were very many kinds of virtue in him, that of chastity shone forth as especially outstanding. It is right, then, that, since you have learned in Abraham the unwearied devotion of faith, in Isaac the purity of a sincere mind, in Jacob a singular endurance of spirit and of labours, you should turn your mind from that general view of the virtues to the particular kinds of discipline themselves. For although those are broader, these are more distinct, and they penetrate the mind all the more easily the more they are circumscribed and defined.

2. Let holy Joseph, then, be set before us as a mirror of chastity. For in his conduct, in his actions, purity shines, and a certain radiance of grace, the companion of chastity, gleams. Hence too he was loved by his parents more than the other sons. But this was a cause of envy, which ought not to be passed over in silence.

3. From here, then, the argument of the whole history proceeded, so that at the same time we may learn that the perfect man is not moved by an injury to avenge his hurt, nor repays evil in return. Hence David too says: if I have repaid evil to those who repaid me. But what would there have been for which Joseph deserved to be preferred to the others, if he had either injured those who injured him or loved those who loved him? For most people do this. But this is wonderful: if you love your enemy, as the Saviour teaches. Rightly, then, is he to be admired who did this before the Gospel: injured, he spared; attacked, he forgave; sold, he did not repay the wrong, but paid back favour for insult, which after the Gospel we have all learned and cannot keep.

4. Let us learn, then, the envy of the saints too, that we may imitate their patience, and let us know that they were not of a more excellent nature, but of more excellent observance, and that they were not ignorant of vices, but amended them. But if envy scorched even the saints, how much more must we beware lest it set sinners ablaze?

Chapter II

5. We are taught, then, what the affection of parents and the favour of children ought to be. To love one's children is sweet, and to love them more intensely is sweeter still; but often fatherly love itself, unless it keeps to moderation, harms the children, if either it weakens the beloved one by too much indulgence or, by preferring one, turns the rest away from the affection of brotherhood. More is gained for a son for whom the love of his brothers is gained. This is the more splendid munificence of fathers, this the richer inheritance of sons. Let equal favour join the children whom equal nature has joined. Love knows no gain of money in which there is loss of love. Why do you wonder if quarrels arise between brothers over a farm or a house, when envy blazed up among the sons of holy Jacob over a tunic?

6. What then? Is Jacob to be blamed because he preferred one to the rest? But neither can we take from parents the freedom to love more those whom they believe to deserve more, nor ought we to cut off in children the eagerness to please more. And so Jacob too loved him more in whom he foresaw the greater marks of virtues, so that he seems not so much, as a father, to have preferred a son as, as a prophet, a mystery; and rightly he made him a tunic of many colours, to signify that he was to be preferred to his brothers by a clothing of diverse virtues.

7. And so divine grace shone forth in him while he was still a boy, since he dreamed that, when he had bound sheaves with his brothers, as it seemed to him in the vision, his sheaf rose up and stood upright, while his brothers' sheaves turned and worshipped his sheaf. In this, surely, the future resurrection of the Lord Jesus was revealed. When the eleven disciples saw him in Jerusalem, they worshipped him, and all the saints, when they have risen, will worship him, bringing forward the fruits of good works, as it is written: But coming they shall come with exultation, carrying their sheaves. Hence his brothers, although out of envy they denied belief in the dream, yet expressing its interpretation in their own words, answered him: Shall you, reigning, reign over us, or, ruling, be ruler over us? For that vision signified the King who was to come, whom all flesh of the human race would worship with bended knee.

8. And he saw another dream and told it to his father and his brothers, namely that the sun and the moon and eleven stars worshipped him. Hence his father rebuked him, saying: What then will this vision be that you have dreamed? Shall I and your mother and your brothers come and worship you upon the earth? Who is he whom parents and brothers worshipped upon the earth but Christ Jesus, when Joseph and his mother with the disciples worshipped him, confessing the true God in that body, of whom alone it was said: Praise him, sun and moon; praise him, all you stars and light? And what does the father's rebuke signify but the hardness of the people of Israel, from whom is Christ according to the flesh, whom even today they do not believe to be God and are unwilling to worship as Lord, because they know that he was born of them? So they hear his answers, and do not understand: they themselves read that the sun and the moon praise him, but they refuse to believe that it was said of Christ. So Jacob is deceived by a type that belongs to another, but he is not deceived in his own love. His fatherly love does not go astray; it is the feeling of the people that would go astray that is expressed.

Chapter III

9. It was not, then, that the patriarch did not believe so great a dream, he who was prophesying both things alike by a twofold oracle, so as to represent the person both of the just man and of the people: that the Son of God was to come to earth, who would be both loved by the just and denied by the unbelievers. He saw, then, the mysteries of the future incarnation, he who sent his son to his brothers to see whether the sheep were well. What sheep was God already then seeking in that zeal of the patriarch, but those of whom the Lord Jesus himself said in the Gospel: I have not come except to the lost sheep of the house of Israel? And he sent, it says, to Shechem, which by interpretation is called 'shoulder or back', that is, to those who would not turn to the Lord but, fleeing from his face, would turn their backs, which is properly the mark of the sinner; for Cain, indeed, went out from the face of God, and the prophet says: You will make them a back. But the just man does not turn away from the Lord; he runs to meet him, saying: My eyes are ever toward the Lord. And Isaiah, when the Lord said: Whom shall I send?, offered himself of his own accord, saying: Behold, I. And Simeon waited to see Christ the Lord, and after he had seen him, since he had seen the forgiver of sins and the redeemer of the whole world, as though relieved of sin he asked to be released from the use of this flesh, saying: Now dismiss your slave, Lord, for my eyes have seen your salvation. And Zacchaeus first found the privilege of his commendation in this, that he climbed a tree to see Christ. So Joseph was sent by his father to his brothers, or rather by that Father who did not spare his own Son but delivered him up for us all, by that Father of whom it is written: God sending his own Son in the likeness of the flesh of sin.

10. And Joseph was wandering, it says, because he could not find his brothers. Not without reason was he wandering who was seeking those who wandered; for the Lord knows, indeed, those who are his. And so Jesus too, wearied from the journey, sat upon the well. He was wearied, he who did not find the people of God whom he was seeking; for they had gone out from the face of the Lord. For he who follows guilt goes out from Christ. The sinner goes out, the just man goes in. And so Adam, a sinner, hid himself, but the just man says: Let my prayer go in before your sight.

11. And Joseph found his brothers in Dothan, which signifies 'failing'. For where is he who deserts God, if not in failing? Nor is it any wonder if those were failing who did not hear him who says: Come to me, all you who labour and are burdened, and I will refresh you. So Joseph came to Dothan, and they saw him beforehand coming from afar, before he drew near to them, and they raged to kill him. Rightly were they far off who were in failing, and they raged for this reason, that Christ had not drawn near to them. For if the type of Christ had drawn near to them, they would surely have loved their brother; but those could not be near who were plotting fratricide. Behold, it says, that dreamer is coming. Now therefore come, let us kill him. Were not these the words spoken by those who were plotting a fratricidal sacrilege, as Solomon says of them: Let us take away the just man, for he is useless to us?

12. And they added, in Genesis: And we shall see what his dreams will be. This was written of Joseph; it was fulfilled in Christ, when the Jews said at his passion: If he is the King of Israel, let him come down now from the cross, and we believe him. He trusts in God: let him deliver him now, if he wills him. Were the brothers themselves, then, so impious as to kill their brother? And whence the great merits of the patriarchs, that the Law should designate the tribes of the whole people by their names? How do the names of devotion and the marks of crime agree? So these too were labouring under the type of the people, not under a fault of their own mind. Hence all the envy, hence the plotting of fratricide: envy by figure, love by affection.

13. And so Reuben and Judah, keeping the holy rights of brotherhood, wished to free him from their hands, and rightly Judah is preferred in his father's blessing, when it is said to him: The sons of your father shall worship you. A lion's whelp is Judah, and he is the expectation of the Gentiles. This surely befits Christ alone, for whom it was reserved to be worshipped by his brothers and awaited by the Gentiles, to wash his robe in wine by the passion of his own body, because he stained his flesh with no spot of sin. Asher too surely did not believe that this applied to himself: Asher, his bread is rich, and Asher himself is with princes. And what of Joseph himself, to whom it was said: My son to be increased, Joseph, my son to be increased, my son envied, my younger son, return to me, against whom they took counsel and whom they cursed, in whom the blessing prevailed over the blessings of the abiding mountains and the desires of the everlasting hills: whom did he understand to be signified in himself but him who, surpassing the merits of all, held, above the desires of all the saints, the summit of immeasurable power, whom no one could equal even in wish? So in the patriarchs envy is made good by grace; they are both excused from guilt and consecrated by the gift of revelation. For it is not so blameworthy to have said what belongs to the people as it is blessed to have seen what belongs to Christ. They took on the role of the sinful people, so as to receive the grace of the Lord the Redeemer. Surely grace has wiped out the guilt; the guilt has not lessened the grace.

14. And so that we may recognize that all this is a mystery concerning the people and the Lord Jesus, Come, it says, let us sell Joseph to the Ishmaelites. What does the name Joseph mean by interpretation, if not that it signifies divine grace and the expression of the most high God? Who then is sold but he who, though he was in the form of God, did not judge it plunder to be equal to God, but emptied himself, taking the form of a slave? For we would not have bought him, had his own not sold him. They sold badly; they bought worse. They sold to merchants; they bought a good fragrance from traitors; for Judah sold, and the Ishmaelites bought, who by Latin interpretation are signified as 'hating their God'. Therefore in one place we find Joseph bought for twenty, in another for twenty-five gold pieces, in another for thirty, because Christ is not worth one and the same price to all. To some less, to others more. The faith of the buyer is the increase of the merchandise. To the more devout God is more precious; to the sinner the Redeemer is more precious. He is worth more to him too who has more grace. But he is also worth more to him to whom much has been given, because he to whom more has been forgiven loves more, as the Lord himself declared in the Gospel concerning that woman who poured ointment upon his feet and washed them with tears and wiped them with her hair and dried them with kisses, of whom he said to Simon: Therefore I say to you: her many sins are forgiven her, because she loved much. But he to whom less is forgiven loves less. Sometimes the differences of price carry the expression not only of a quantity but also of a number, as you have in the case of the ointment which the Lord said had been poured out for his burial, as it is written, when Judas says: This could have been sold for three hundred denarii; by this number it is not a quantity but the signification of the cross that seems to be expressed. So here too the difference of twenty or thirty gold or silver pieces carries a sign of twofold or threefold perfection. For the twenty-five gold pieces of the jubilee, which is the number of remission, signify the most precious portion. Here too, so that you may notice the figure of the Lord's passion, Judah the patriarch says: Let us hand Joseph over to the Ishmaelites; but let our hands not be laid upon him. And well had he said above: But do not lay hands on him, which is what the Jews said at the Lord's passion: It is not lawful for us to put anyone to death, so that the word of Jesus might be fulfilled, signifying by what death he was to die.

15. Already then, therefore, the sign of the future cross was being prefigured, and at the same time this too: in being stripped of his tunic, that is, of the flesh which he assumed, he was stripped of the comely diversity of virtues. So it was his tunic, that is, his flesh, not his divinity, that was bloodied, and it was the clothing of the flesh, not the immortality of life, that they were able to take from him. This tunic the Jewish beasts bloodied, those of whom he says: Behold, I send you as lambs among wolves.

16. And as for the pit being dry, what wonder if the pit of the Jews has no water, since they have forsaken the fountain of living water and have made for themselves broken pits? And that you may know that this is truly a mystery, the Lord himself says of himself: They placed me in the lower pit, in darkness and in the shadow of death.

17. Those, then, buy Christ who bring good fragrances: incense, with which they kindle the altars of a devout mind, whence David too says in Greek: κατευθυνθήτω ἡ προσευχή μου ὡς θυμίαμα ἐνώπιόν σου [Let my prayer be directed as incense before you, Ps 140:2 (141:2)]; and resin too, by which broken pieces of marble are joined together. There is a spiritual resin, to make firm the fracture of your soul, to join what has been torn apart, to bind fast what has been loosened. For this spiritual resin strengthens and sets together certain shattered members of the soul, so that they are joined without hurt. And so Jeremiah seeks it, so as to take care to heal Babylon, if he can, saying: Take resin for her corruption, if in some way she will be healed. We have cared for Babylon, and she is not healed. The synagogue was not healed, because that resin passed over to the Church. Therefore merchants came from Gilead, that is, from the possession or the sojourning of testimony, carrying their wares over to the Church, so that this resin might cure the sins of the Gentiles. Of them it is said: Grow strong, you slackened hands and loosened knees. Sincere faith is resin. Peter applied it when he said to the lame man: In the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth, rise and walk. And rightly he rose and walked. He had it when he said to the paralytic: Aeneas, the Lord Jesus heals you: rise and spread your bed for yourself. And he rose and spread his bed for himself. He had it when he said to the dead woman: Rise in the name of our Lord Jesus Christ. And the dead woman rose. By the blending of this resin those stones are fitted together from which God is able to raise up children to Abraham. By the medicine of this resin the lame man is set upright, the paralytic is restored, the dead woman is raised up.

18. That too, their sprinkling his tunic with the blood of a kid, seems to signify that, assailing him with false testimonies, they brought into the odium of sin him who forgives the sins of all. For us he is a lamb, for them a kid. For us, from whom he took away the sins of the world, the Lamb of God was slain; for them he is a kid, for they are the ones whose errors he heaped up, whose offences he piled high. And so he says: Fill up the measure of your fathers. And rightly Jacob, lamenting the losses of his posterity, wept as a father for a lost son, and as a prophet mourned the destruction of the Jews. And so he also tore his garment, which we read was done at the passion of the Lord Jesus by the chief of the priests, in whom there was not a private person but the charge of a public office. And the veil of the temple was torn, so that by such signs it might be shown that the mysteries were profaned, the people stripped of the garments of salvation, the kingdom divided and bound to be destroyed, because every kingdom divided will easily be destroyed. And truly divided, when what had been Christ's began now to be the devil's. For those could not remain undivided who were separating the Son from the Father.

Chapter IV

19. Joseph, then, was sold, led into Egypt, bought by the overseer of the cooks. No idle figure this, one who would cook what was raw among foods, so that minds might feast on the sweetness of faith; for no food is sweeter than knowledge and teaching. Before, there was in Egypt a raw unbelief, which no ardour of divine knowledge and no desire for true knowledge had softened, which the fiery words of the Lord had not cooked through. And Joseph was sold in Egypt because Christ was to come to those to whom it was said: By your sins you have been sold, and therefore he redeemed by his own blood those whom their own sins had sold. But Christ, sold by taking on our condition, not by guilt, is not held by the price of sin, because he himself did no sin. So by the price he took on our debt, not debts of his own; he took away the bond, removed the moneylender, released the debtor: one man paid off what was owed by all. It was not permitted to us to go out from slavery. He took this on for us, to drive back the slavery of the world, to restore the freedom of paradise, to bestow a new grace by the honour of fellowship with himself. So much for the mystery.

20. But as regards the moral sense, since the Lord our God wills all to be saved, he gave through Joseph too a consolation to those who are in slavery and furnished them a lesson, so that they might learn that even in the lowest condition one's character can be superior, and that no state is without its share of virtue, if each one's mind knows itself: that it is the flesh that is subject to slavery, not the mind, and that many slaves are freer than their masters, if, though placed in slavery, they think they must keep away from slavish works. Every sin is slavish; innocence is free. Hence the Lord too says: Everyone who commits sin is a slave of sin. For how is every greedy man not a slave, who auctions himself off for a paltry little profit of money? He who has heaped up what he will not use fears everything, lest he lose what he has heaped up, bound to guard it at the greater peril the greater the things he has sought. How is he not a beggar to whom what he possesses is little? For even if he seems rich to me, to himself he is in want, nor does he comfort his longings with proofs, since he does not know how to believe what he wishes for. And how is he too not a slave who is subject to lust? First he burns with his own fires and is consumed by the torches of his own breast. To such the prophet rightly says: Walk in the light of your fire and in the flame which you have kindled. He takes on every fear, he lies in wait for the sleep of each one; to gain his desire for one, he becomes the slave of all. He is a slave, then, and in a wretched slavery indeed, who himself makes masters for himself, who himself wishes to have people to fear; for nothing is so peculiar to slavery as always to fear. But that man is always free, in whatever condition of slavery, who is not captured by passion, not held by the chains of avarice, not bound by fear of a charge, who looks on the present untroubled, whom the future does not terrify. Does not this man seem to you to be master in slavery, and that one to be a slave in freedom? Joseph was a slave, Pharaoh was a king: happier was the slavery of the one than the kingship of the other. Indeed all Egypt would have collapsed through famine, had he not subjected his kingdom to the counsel of a slave.

21. Slaves by birth, then, have something to glory in: Joseph too was a slave. Those who have come from freedom into slavery by some necessity have something to console themselves with. They have something to imitate, so that they may learn that they can change their condition, not their character, and that there is freedom even in home-born slaves, and steadfastness in slavery. Masters too have something to hope for through good slaves. Abraham found a wife for his son through a home-born slave. The Lord blessed the house of the Egyptian because of Joseph, and the blessing of the Lord came upon all his substance, both in the house and in the fields. And he turned over, it says, everything whatever he had into the hands of Joseph. We notice that what masters could not govern, slaves governed.

Chapter V

22. But why should I set out that slave's arrangements concerning a private house, when he governed an empire? Yet it is a greater thing that he first governed himself and, though he was comely in appearance and very handsome in face, did not divert the beauty of his countenance to another's injury but kept it for his own grace, thinking himself the more beautiful if he were shown to be more handsome not at the cost of chastity but by the cultivation of modesty. That, he thought, was true comeliness, which would not capture others' eyes nor wound frail minds, but would win the judgment of all, to be a harm to none and a praise to himself. Now if some woman looked on him with wanton eyes, the crime is hers alone who looked wrongly, not his who would not wish to be looked at wrongly; nor is there guilt in his being seen. It was not in the slave's power not to be seen: the husband ought to have guarded against his wife's eyes. If the husband feared nothing about his wife, Joseph held this to be a testimony to chastity, not a licence for carelessness. Yet let men too learn to guard against the eyes of women; even those who do not wish to be loved are passionately loved. And so Joseph was passionately loved, who would scorn the woman who loved him. And Scripture excused him well, saying: The wife of his master cast her eyes upon Joseph, that is: he did not display himself, nor did he catch her off her guard, but she cast her own nets and was caught in her own toils, she spread her own snares and stuck fast in her own bonds.

23. And she said to him: Sleep with me. The first weapons of an adulteress are those of the eyes, the second those of words; but he who is not captured by the eyes can resist a word. Defence is at hand where the affection is still free. Therefore it is written that he would not. First, then, he overcame her in the encounter of the mind, repelling her onslaught as with the shield of his soul; then he hurled his speech like a spear, so that she might call herself back. And he said to his master's wife. Rightly is she called his master's wife, not his mistress herself, she who could not wrest what she wished to obtain. For how was she a mistress, who had not the power of mastery, who did not keep the discipline of a mistress, who offered slaves the incitements of lust? He was the master who did not take up the torches of the lover, who did not feel the bonds of the seductress, whom no dread of death terrified, who preferred to die free of crime rather than choose partnership with a criminal power. He was free who believed it shameful not to return favour for favour. And so he does not excuse himself like a fearful man, nor take care like one afraid of danger, but as one indebted to his master's kindness and to his own innocence he flees the crime of an ungrateful man, and as a just man he shudders at the stain of sin and the contagion of guilt. The adulteress hurled a third dart by the persistence of her soliciting, but Joseph did not listen to her. You have here what to beware of after the first words. Lust is not only slippery but also bold, importunate, wanton, and the adulteress has nothing she stands in awe of. She who did not grieve at the first losses of modesty lies in wait to capture.

24. And so, when he had gone in for the sake of his duty and the charge entrusted to him, with witnesses and household servants out of the way, she seized him, saying: Sleep with me. Joseph is excused by the testimony of Scripture, because he could not abandon the service entrusted to him by his master. For it is not enough that he went into the inner parts of the house secure of himself, as one who could not be captured: a just man ought to have foreseen not to give the raging woman an opportunity, lest she perish by a sin on his account. But he who saw that his master's wife was set against him had to beware also of offending his master by neglecting his duty. At the same time he thought her boldness was still one of speech, not of seizing.

25. He is excused for going in; he is praised for slipping away, and for not valuing the garments of the body more than the chastity of the soul. He left, as not his own, what the adulteress was holding with her hands, and judged to be another's what could be seized by the touch of an unchaste woman. A great man indeed, who when sold knew nothing of a slavish spirit, when passionately loved did not love in return, when asked did not consent, when seized fled away; who, when he was approached by his master's wife, could be held by his garment but could not be captured in his soul, and did not even endure her words for long, judging it a contagion if he lingered longer, lest the incitements of lust should pass over through the adulteress's hands. And so he stripped off his garment, shook off the crime, and, leaving behind the cast-off clothing by which he was held, he fled, despoiled indeed, but not naked, because he was the better covered by the clothing of modesty; for no one is naked but him whom guilt has made naked. Indeed we have in the earlier passages that Adam, after he deserted the commandments of God by transgression and contracted the debts of grievous sin, was naked. Hence he himself says: I heard your voice in paradise and I was afraid, because I am naked, and I hid myself. For he understood that he was naked, he who had lost the insignia of divine protection. And therefore he lay hidden, because he did not have the garment of faith, which he had surely laid aside by transgressing. You see a great thing. That man was naked who did not lose his tunic; this man was not naked who stripped himself of the garments which he left in the hands of the adulteress. The same Scripture declared the one naked and denied it of the other. And therefore this man despoiled himself rather than made himself naked, since he kept the incorrupt garments of the virtues, despoiling himself of the old man with his deeds, so as to put on the new, who is renewed in knowledge according to the image of the Creator; but that one remained naked, who could not clothe himself again, stripped of the singular covering of virtue. Hence he received a tunic of skin, because as a sinner he could not have a spiritual one. Joseph therefore left his garments and laid bare the shamelessness of the adulteress, which afterwards could not lie hidden.

26. And so he went outside, and she herself made public the attempts at her adultery, raising her voice because the Hebrew had fled leaving his garments behind. So she herself was betraying what she ought to have concealed, so as to harm the innocent man by a fabricated charge. But the just man does not know how to accuse, and so the unchaste woman did this with impunity. Her, then, I would call truly stripped, even while keeping another's garments, since she had lost all the coverings of chastity; him I would call adorned enough, defended enough, whose voice was not heard while his innocence spoke. So Susanna afterwards, while she kept silent at the trial, spoke better through an oracle, and therefore she merited the defence of a prophet, she who did not seek the help of her own voice. Him, then, I would call the more blessed when he was sent into prison, because he was undergoing martyrdom for chastity. For purity is a good gift, but of lesser merit when it carries no danger; but where it is defended even at the peril of one's life, there it is crowned more fully. With his case unheard, with the truth of the matter unexamined, Joseph is sent to prison as one guilty of a crime, but the Lord did not desert him even in prison. Let the innocent not be troubled when they are assailed with false charges, when, with justice overborne, they are thrust into prison. God visits his own even in prison, and so there is more help there where there is more danger.

27. But what wonder if Christ visits those placed in prison, he who recalled that he himself was shut up in prison in his own, as you have it written: I was in prison, and you did not come to me? Where does divine mercy not penetrate? Joseph found such grace that he who had been shut up in prison himself rather kept the bars of the prison: the keeper of the keys gave up his office and committed all those shut in to his power. And so not only did Joseph not feel the prison, but he also relieved others of the hardship of the prison.

Chapter VI

28. The authors of this injury, then, are a woman and eunuchs: but the woman is one of the Egyptian women, who are accustomed to mingle wanton talk with men, to provoke the modest, to pursue those who flee, to press hard upon the bashful. Since she could not defend her own vices, she accused the innocent, adding faithlessness to faithlessness, keeping what belonged to another, herself condemning others, setting no limit to her fury. For what cause was there for her cruelty, except that she saw her lusts resisted and her desires cheated, consent being denied her? See for what reason the prison is opened: that it may receive the innocent; chains are loosed from the guilty, that they may be laid upon the faithful; adulterers of the truth are set free, that he may be shut in who refused the adultery of faith.

29. But what shall I say of those eunuchs? They ought to be an example to the other eunuchs, that their position is fragile and slight, and all their hope lies in the king's will; for them a slight offence is the greatest danger, while their prosperity is but a mean service. The one gloried in being overseer of the wine, the other in being overseer of the bakers. Both gave offence, and they were sent into prison and entrusted to holy Joseph by the keeper of the keys of the prison himself; and when they had been there many days, they saw a dream. And when Joseph visited them again, he found them sad and troubled in mind, because they were troubled by a dream for which they could find no interpreter. Is it not through God, he said, that the interpretation of a dream comes? Tell it to me, then. And the overseer of the wine told his dream: There was a vine before me, and on the vine three roots, and it was blossoming, bearing shoots, and the clusters of grapes were ripe, and Pharaoh's cup was in my hand. And I took it and pressed it into the cup and gave the cup into Pharaoh's hands. And Joseph said to him: The three roots are three days. Yet three days, and Pharaoh will bear in mind your chief office and will restore you to your former office, and you will give the cup into Pharaoh's hands. But be mindful through your own self, when it is well with you, and you will show mercy to me and you will be mindful of me before Pharaoh and you will bring me out of this prison, for I was stolen away by theft from the land of the Hebrews, and here I have done nothing evil, but they sent me into the pit of this prison.

30. The other's dream I do not care to tell. You surely remember my words, that even then I shrank from interpreting it in the case of one whose end I shrink from, whose death I dread. Let us speak first of the one who seemed to himself happy because he was overseer of the wine, and who believed it the highest summit of all power that he gave the king his cup. This was his glory, this his magnificence in this world: deprived of it, he grieved; restored to it, he rejoiced. But this is a dream, and all the power of the world is a dream, not the truth. Indeed, it was through a dream that he saw his chief office restored to him. And Isaiah says that men of this kind, who in this world delight in prosperity, are just so. As one who eats and drinks in his sleep seems to himself, while he sleeps, to be filled with food or drink, but when he wakes begins to hunger the more — then he understands how empty was that food and drink of the dreamer — so he who sleeps in this world and does not open his eyes to the divine mysteries, as long as he is weighed down by the sleep of the body, thinks this worldly power to be of some moment — he sees it as though in sleep —; when he wakes, he discovers how empty is the pleasure of this world.

31. Consider now that true Hebrew, that interpreter not of a dream but of the truth and of a glorious vision, who came from that fullness of divinity and freedom of heavenly grace into this bodily prison; whom the allurement of this world could not change, nor any corruption of worldly pleasure overthrow; who, when tempted, did not fall; when desired, did not desire; who finally, seized by the garment of the body by a kind of adulterous hand of the synagogue, put off the flesh and ascended free of death. The harlot slandered him when she could not hold him, him whom the prison did not terrify, whom the underworld did not hold. Nay more, from the place to which he had descended as though to be punished, from there he set others free: where the bonds of death were being drawn tight upon himself, there he himself loosed the bonds of the dead.

32. Consider, then, this Hebrew saying to that overseer of the eunuchs, who had incurred the king's displeasure and whom he had restored to his office: Be mindful through your own self, when it is well with you, and you will show mercy to me and you will be mindful of me. He repeated it a second time for this reason, because he knew that the man would not remember what injury he had escaped once he had recovered his power. He admonished him a second time for this reason, because he set him free a second time, so that, if the memory of the first benefit did not hold him, that of the later might at least come to him, and he might not despise the author of his safety nor violate him by faithless betrayal. But, what is worse, forgetfulness of a benefit quickly creeps in with prosperity. Restored to his office, he did not become mindful of the interpreter, but forgot him. But although he was forgetting, Christ was not forgetting; he was speaking to him, and speaking through a slave, saying to him: Be mindful through your own self, that is, at least in consideration of your own office, recall what you have heard. But even if you have now forgotten, you will be mindful of me, so that you may escape danger, you who have forgotten a benefit. Yet, lifted up by power, he did not remember. And how great is this power, where the service is of wine? See the source of all the boasting! Because he was overseer of the eunuchs who served the wine in the royal cups.

33. And it came to pass, he says, after two years. Call me a liar about this time of our own eunuch, unless even the day agrees, for after two years he recovered his office and did not remember, but was reminded. For he who did not believe his own dream learned that even kingship itself in this world is a dream. He learned too that the . . . . of kings are dreams, and that their powers are not perpetual. But let grief pass quickly over this passage, lest it grow raw again by the very recollection; it gives me no pleasure even to remember my own words, which at that time either grief poured out or the insult to the Church wrung from me.

34. Reminded of himself, then, through the king's dream, he says: I remember my sin. This is a late confession indeed, but would that it were a true one! After the sin you confess what you ought to have guarded against before the sin. How quickly you had forgotten Be mindful of me! You know, surely, that these were the words at that time, but you had ears dulled by the pride of power, and drunk with wine you did not hear the words of sobriety. At least now be mindful of me, you who confess your sin late. You who make your request of a slave, why do you deny the Lord? Be drunk now not with wine but with the Holy Spirit. Remember what he suffered with whom you slept your sleep and dreamed your dream. He too was an overseer, and overseer of the king's banquets, those that belonged to the bakers' work. He believed himself exalted because he had the king's bread in his power: he did not know that this power has many windings. He threatened others, though he himself was soon to be handed over to the extreme punishment; and he did not listen to him who, though a slave of the Lord, nevertheless spoke the oracle: that by the command of the very king on whose account he flattered himself so greatly he would lose his head and be left as food for the birds. At least this example ought to have called you back, so that you would not put your trust in faithlessness.

35. There are other examples too of the pride and fragility of royal ministers, which the history of a later time contains. Doeg too was an overseer, and overseer of the king's animals for the training of mules, that is, of eunuch animals. He too denounced the priest of the Lord and by fraud stirred up the king to endanger the priest, and he too was a Syrian. Am I lying, when both country and deeds agree? Haman too, the king's chamberlain and himself an overseer, while he strove with shameless rashness to invade the churches of the Lord and to despoil and persecute the faithful people, paid for his sacrileges with heavy punishments.

Chapter VII

36. But let us return to that overseer of the wine, who, as though drunk with much wine, long forgot the author of his benefit, yet at length, in order to provide the king with an interpreter, made known the course of what had happened, not as one grateful, but as one shrewd. When he learned of it, the king too ordered Joseph to be summoned, and, having had him brought out of the prison, asked him whether he could interpret his dream for him. Delighted by his explanation, he removed the injury and conferred honour. And so see whether these things too do not fit the present. The injury was inflicted before he became known to the king; favour was returned once he was known to the king. Thus the king is free from blame, because the injury that the holy man received was another's doing, and the favour he received is the king's own.

37. Now the dream and its interpretation is as follows: Whatever, he says, God does he shows to Pharaoh. The seven good cows are seven years, and the seven good ears are seven years. Pharaoh's dream is one. And the seven lean cows that came up after them are seven years, and the seven lean ears, blighted by the wind, are seven years. There will be famine for seven years. And the word that I spoke, 'Whatever God does he shows to Pharaoh': behold, seven years of great plenty are coming in all the land of Egypt; but seven years of famine will come after these, and they will forget the plenty in all Egypt. And the famine will consume all the land, and the plenty of the land will not be recognized because of the famine that is to come after this; for it will be exceedingly severe. And as for Pharaoh's repeating the dream twice, it is because the word from God will be true and God will hasten to do it.

38. An old dream, recent events. The earlier things were consumed by the later, and where formerly there was an abundance of plenty, there arose a want of all things. But if anyone had given the ruler counsel of this kind, to keep something of the earlier plenty for the later period of his reign, the remaining time too would have overflowed with a generous abundance for giving. But the immoderate squandering of the earlier age left even the later destitute, and men, plundering everything, were unwilling to call in some Joseph. Although I am no Joseph — for who could claim that? — yet I kept crying out that those fat cows signified not only wantonness but also neglect of reverence for God — for of the unbelievers it was said: Fat bulls have besieged me, and of the people of the Jews it is written: He was made fat and gross and was enlarged, and he forsook God, who made him — and that therefore that dream of worldly overflow could not be perpetual: there would be a time when hard famine would follow upon these things.

39. Yet I do not think that this dream was shown to one or two, but set before all, in that the seven years of this world, fat and rich with worldly plenty, are swallowed up by those ages that are to come, in which there is perpetual rest and the spiritual observance of the Law, such as that tribe Ephraim, fruitful to the God of the fathers, may keep: a good cow, not distended with a bodily udder, but abounding in spiritual milk and grace, on the beauty of whose neck God says that he sits, as it is written: Ephraim is a heifer taught to love victory. But I passed over the beauty of her neck. Let not the oil of the sinner, then, fatten our head, nor ought false fruits to delight us, lest it be said of us too: You have sown impiety and gathered the vintage of its iniquities. You have eaten false fruit, because you hoped in your chariots. Nor does it trouble me that the dream has lean ears blighted by the wind, because David too was better then, when he was wasting away like a spider; and a troubled spirit is a sacrifice to God, and those come out more excellent whom the evil spirit has exercised in this world with grievous injuries.

40. Hence I think that Joseph earned rewards that were rather mystical, because he spoke of mystical things. For what does the ring placed on his finger mean, except that we should understand that the high priesthood of faith was conferred on him, so that he himself might seal others? What does the robe mean, which is the clothing of wisdom, except the primacy of prudence granted him by that heavenly King? The gold necklace seems to express good understanding; the chariot too signifies the lofty summit of his merits. And who is it who took a wife from the Gentiles, except him who gathered the Church to himself from the nations, and received from her a son, Manasseh, through whom he forgot all his sorrows, which he had because of the sacrileges of the Jews? He received another son also, Ephraim, through whose progress it became clear that the humility taken on in the flesh both did not humble his divinity and heaped up his glory.

41. Indeed, all who were suffering famine were sent to Joseph. Who are these, except those of whom it was said: They will return at evening and suffer hunger like dogs? And the famine was not of one place but of the whole earth, because there was none who did good. Therefore the Lord Jesus, taking pity on the fasting of the world, opened his granaries and laid open the hidden treasures of knowledge and wisdom of the heavenly mysteries, so that no one should lack nourishment. For Wisdom said: Come, eat my bread, and therefore The Lord feeds me, and I shall lack nothing is said by him alone who is filled by Christ. Christ, then, opened his granaries and sold, seeking not coin of money but the price of faith and the wage of devotion. And he sold not to a few in Judaea, but he sold to all, so that he might be believed by all the nations.

42. And all the countries came into Egypt to Joseph to buy; for the famine had prevailed. For all are hungry whom Christ has not fed. Let us buy, then, the food with which we can drive away hunger. Let no one be held back by consideration of his poverty; let no one who has no silver be afraid. Christ does not seek silver, but faith, which is more precious than silver. Indeed, Peter bought him, who had no silver. Silver, he says, and gold I do not have, but what I have I give you. In the name of Christ Jesus rise and walk. And the prophet Isaiah says: You who thirst, go to the water, and all you who have no silver, go, buy and drink and eat without silver and without the price of wine. For he did not seek a price from us who paid the price of his own blood for us, because he redeemed us not with gold and silver but with precious blood. Therefore you owe that price with which you were bought. Even if he does not always demand it, still you owe it. Buy Christ for yourself, then, not with what few have, but with what all have by nature, but few offer through fear. What Christ asks back from you is his own. He gave life to all; he offered his death for all. Pay for your Author what you will pay by law. This is no small contract; not all see it easily. Indeed, those virgins in the Gospel whom the bridegroom shut out at his coming were left outside for this reason, because they did not buy the oil that was for sale. Hence it is said to them: Go rather to those who sell, and buy for yourselves. And not undeservedly is the merchant praised who sold all that he had and bought the pearl.

Chapter VIII

43. And Jacob said to his sons: Why are you idle? Behold, I have heard that there is grain in Egypt. Go down there and buy food for us. Jacob did not say this once; daily he says it to all his sons who come rather late to the grace of Christ: Why are you idle? Behold, I have heard that there is grain in Egypt. From this grain is the seed that rises again. He, therefore, who endures hunger ought to ascribe it to his own sloth. Behold, he says, I have heard that there is grain in Egypt. Certainly the young are accustomed to hear things sooner than the old, since, being out of doors, many of them go about. But it is the old man who first hears of this trading — but that old man in whom is the long-lived grey hair of faith; the old man is the first to understand, the old man in whom is a venerable old age, and the age of old age is a spotless life.

44. Nor do any undertake this trading except the sons of Jacob, and they of more advanced age. Therefore ten go; the youngest does not go. His father did not send him, lest, he says, infirmity befall him. Benjamin, the youngest, was still liable to infirmity. It is indeed the patriarch Benjamin of whom we read, but that Paul of the tribe of Benjamin was being prefigured. Rightly did Jacob have misgivings about his infirmity. Indeed, he was made infirm that he might be healed: he suffered blindness, but this infirmity was for his salvation.

45. Indeed, that blindness brought light. We have heard the history; let us learn the mystery. Without Benjamin the patriarchs had first set out; without Paul, the apostles. Neither came first, but each, summoned by the first, made the merchandise of the earlier ones more abundant by his coming. There is, he says, grain in Egypt, that is: where the famine is greater, there the plenty is greater. Great is the grain in Egypt. Indeed, God the Father too says: Out of Egypt I have called my son. From this grain comes that fruitfulness; for there could not have been a harvest unless the Egyptians had first sown the grain. There is, then, grain, which no one before believed existed; in this grain the patriarchs trade. And they indeed had brought silver, but good Joseph gave the grain and returned the silver; for Christ is not bought with money but with grace: your price is faith; with this the divine mysteries are bought. And this grain is carried by that ass, once unclean under the Law, but now clean under grace.

Chapter IX

46. Yet Benjamin, the youngest, was being held back and still clung to his father's affection. The bonds of the Law held him, his fathers' custom. The famine grew stronger, because he was coming late. Two intercede for him, Reuben and Judah, that is, humility and confession. He uses them as sureties with his father, to them he is entrusted: the one the firstborn, the other the one raised again. The Law belongs to the firstborn, the Gospel to the one raised again. By them Benjamin, the youngest, is led down, and he arrives accompanied by good fragrances, carrying with him resin, by which marble stones are joined together, because he too by his own preaching, as with a spiritual resin, was to join together living stones; carrying honey also, by which the harmful matter of a wound is eaten away within, without any harshness of cutting. For such was Paul's preaching: it did away with putrid affection and drained off corrupt humour with the sting of his argument, desiring rather to cauterize than to cut the sick inward parts of the mind. That incense is the emblem of prayer, and cassia and stacte the emblems of burial, the prophet David has taught us, saying: Myrrh and stacte and cassia from your garments. For Paul came to preach the cross of the Lord, the ever-green holm-oak, and nuts, whose shell is harder and fruit more tender — and rightly was the priestly rod of Aaron of nut-wood, and the staff of Jeremiah of the same kind — and double silver too. Who would doubt that these gifts are not idle, since both the life of the patriarch and the word of the apostle are ever green in the hearts of each one, and the speech of the saints, like silver tried by fire, shines with the brightness of wholesome precept? Nor is it undeservedly that they bring back double silver, in whom was prefigured the coming of Paul, who granted double honour to the elders who labour in word and teaching.

47. And Joseph saw them, and Benjamin his brother from the same mother. Now the Hebrews are seen, and seen by Christ, who is the true Joseph, when they come with the type of Paul, and he speaks to them gentle and mild words, so that they may take food together. But above, because they came without Benjamin, he did recognize them, but turned away from them, as it is written: And he spoke harsh things to them, because they themselves did not recognize him by whom they were recognized. It was by the merit of Paul, then, that they made progress, whom the Lord Jesus loved more than the other brothers, as a younger brother born of the same mother. Let the Jews observe whom they denied as Lord: he who, though crucified by their synagogue, yet loves them the more as sprung from the same parent, if even late they recognize the author of their salvation. But, conscious of their own offences, they do not believe that the mercy of Christ is so great that he forgives sin and remits injury. And therefore what they would be was prefigured in the patriarchs. They were being invited to grace, they were being called to the banquet of the saving table, and they suspected that a false charge was being prepared against them, that a trap was being laid.

48. And they began to wish to plead their case at the door of the house with the man who was over the house. They still hesitate to enter, and those who wish to establish their case prefer to be justified by their own works rather than to receive grace, and therefore they are refuted in the gates. But he who awaits the fruit of the virginal womb and the inheritance of the Lord, who trades in the merchandise of the Son, is not put to shame in the gate, but at the exit of this life drives back the enemy, so that he, privy to some graver fault, may not stand in his way as he hastens to higher things.

49. Hence, mystically, the steward of the house answered them. And understand who he is when you read of Moses, faithful in all his house. For Moses, Peter, Paul and the other saints are stewards of the house, but Christ alone is the Lord. It is written that Moses was faithful in all his house as a servant, for a testimony of the things that had been spoken, but Christ as a son in his own house; which house we are, if we hold fast the freedom and the glory of hope.

50. This steward of the house, then, answered them: May he be gracious to you, do not be afraid. For your God and the God of your fathers has given you treasures in your sacks: your good silver I have received. For they had said: We found each man's silver in our sacks. We have brought back our silver by weight. O great mysteries, and clearly expressed! This is to say: Why are you puffed up? Do you keep claiming that you have your own silver in your sacks? For what do you have that you did not receive? But if you received it, why do you boast as if you had not received it? Already you are filled, already you have become rich, you who believe that you have silver; but the God of your fathers gave you the silver. He is your God, he is the God of your fathers, whom you denied. But he pardons, but he forgives, but he receives you back, if you return. It is he who does not seek your silver, but gives his own. He himself gave you silver in your sacks. Now your sacks, which held clay, hold silver; and therefore he is yours who says: You have torn my sack and clothed me with gladness. Christ is the gift of gladness. He is your silver, he is your price. The Lord Jesus does not demand from you the price of his grain, he does not seek the weight of your silver. Your silver is base; the silver of the sack is not good.

51. Your good silver I have received, that is: that is not material silver, but your spiritual silver, that is, the good silver, which you have brought with faithful devotion as sons of Jacob, which is given without loss and counted out without any expense, since by such a price the detriment of death is shut out and the gain of life is acquired.

Chapter X

52. And they prepared the gifts, until Joseph came in at midday. The faith of Paul hastened the midday. Before, he was blind; afterwards he began to see the light of justice, since whoever reveals his way to the Lord and hopes in him, the Lord too will bring forth his justice as the light and his judgment as the midday. And when God appeared to Abraham at the holm-oak of Mamre, it was midday, and eternal light shone on him from the presence of the Lord. It is midday when the true Joseph enters his house to dine. For the day shines more brightly then, when we celebrate the sacraments.

53. And they brought in, he says, the gifts to him. We bring in the gifts; he lays on the banquet. He says: Set on the bread, which only the Hebrews eat; the Egyptians cannot eat it. But before the banquet, what condescension! What moral instruction in his conduct, his attentiveness and his graciousness! The brothers were still apprehensive about the false charge that they thought Joseph was bringing against them. He was inviting them to dinner: their feeling wavered, his graciousness persists. He is the first to address them, the first to ask: How are you?

54. And again he says: Is your father, the elder, well? It belongs to the superior to draw the inferior into conversation, to give him confidence to speak, to ask not only about the men themselves but also about their parents.

55. They answer: Your boy, our father, is well. He said 'elder' to honour him; they named him 'boy' to show the deference of humility, because old age is of honoured dignity, whereas boyhood seems subject, and nearer to modesty than to pride.

56. And lifting up his eyes, he saw Benjamin his brother from the same mother. It is a moral point that we see those we love before others, and that those whom the attention of the mind holds are the first to meet the gaze of the eyes, since for the most part, when we are distracted by the mind's occupation with other things, we do not see those whom we have before our eyes. So our sight is directed with the mind as its guide. Holy Joseph, then, saw Benjamin his brother, whom he held in his mind, whom he was looking for with his eyes, in whose absence he had scarcely seen his brothers, because seeing them availed nothing. And not content merely to have seen him, he asked as though he did not know: Is this your younger brother? It is the custom and the grace of love that we hold those dear to us not only with our eyes but also with our speech. Joseph had recognized his beloved, but he asked for this reason, that the voice might sound forth the one whom the mind held.

57. Indeed, he did not wait for an answer, but at once blessed him and was troubled by the fruit of his longing. And his inward parts were wrung, because the freedom to embrace the one he had longed for was being put off. Indeed, going into the storeroom he wept, and washed his face, and restrained himself. The stings of a great love quickly pierce the heart, unless the reins of longing are loosened. Joseph was being overcome by affection, held back by his plan: reason was contending with love. He wept, to temper with tears the heat of devoted love.

58. So much for the moral sense; mystically, however, the Lord Jesus saw Paul — the eyes in fact of the Lord are upon the just — and said: Is this your younger brother? He is still called the younger, since he did not yet bear the mature age of grey-haired faith, had not yet grown to the perfect man, to that, as he himself says, measure of the age of the fullness of Christ. Indeed, he is read of as a young man only there, where he was keeping the garments of those who stoned Stephen. And therefore he desired Philemon to be like not his youth but his old age, as he wrote: I beseech you the more, since you are such as Paul, an old man. Therefore he teaches that the younger widows are to be avoided, not on account of their age, but on account of a certain wantonness of the offences of youth and an immaturity of virtue. For the rest, the praise of chastity is greater in a young man than in an old one. And I think it is not foreign to the truth if we understand this: that, although Paul had this, and shuddered when he was rebuked, because blindness had befallen him, he nevertheless began to draw near by saying: Lord, what do you want me to do? Therefore he was called the younger by Christ, so that he who was being called to grace might be excused from guilt, because it belonged to a slippery age. Indeed, Christ saw him when the light shone around him, and because young men are called back from vice by fear more than by reason, he applied the goad and in pity warned him not to kick against the goad.

59. And he was troubled, just as you have it in the Gospel that he troubled himself in spirit when he was raising Lazarus; and there he wept, so that he might first wash away with his tears the offences of the dead man. But he wept within, and washed his face. The blindness of Paul is the weeping of Christ: he washes his face when the light that was lost is restored to Paul. Christ washed his face when Paul was baptized, through whom the Lord Jesus would be seen by many more. And therefore at the banquet his portion was made fivefold greater, because he was to be preferred to those before him not only in prudence of mind but also in the warfare of the body and the grace of chastity.

Chapter XI

60. And they drank and were made drunk together with him. From the beginning of faith a greater prerogative is conferred on Paul, of whom it was said to Ananias: Go, for he is to me a vessel of election, to bear my name before the Gentiles. From the beginning he is inebriated with an inebriation, but a sober one, so that he too might say with the saints: And your inebriating cup, how excellent it is!

61. And the silver cup is put into his sack alone. Benjamin did not know this: Paul was deceived, but he was being called. Someone is sent after him in the morning; for the night of blindness had advanced, the day of faith was drawing near.

62. First the sacks of each are examined in order. Divine Scripture teaches you good conduct. In order they had earlier reclined at the banquet facing him, the firstborn according to his age. You see that the place of honour is to be yielded to the elder. In order again the sacks of each are searched, that you may know that Paul was chosen by heavenly judgment. He sifted the rest, he preferred this one. In no one else's sack was the silver cup found but in his. What does it mean that it was put into the sack? Joseph indeed made them drunk in order to deceive them, he sent the cup in order to call back by a loving deceit the brother whom he loved; but divine mysteries shine forth.

63. Christ found in us this silver, which he himself had given. We have the silver of nature, we have also that of grace. Nature is the work of the Creator, grace is the gift of the Redeemer. Although we cannot see the gifts of Christ, yet he gives and works in secret, and he gives to all; but to keep them and not lose them belongs to few. Yet he does not give all things to all. Wheat is given to many, the cup to one, who is presented with the prophetic and priestly gift. For it is not everyone, but the prophet who says: I will take the cup of salvation and call upon the name of the Lord.

64. In the body of Paul, therefore, the voice of heavenly doctrine was already shining, since he was learned in the Law. But because he was not yet subject to the righteousness of God, the cup was within the sack, doctrine within the Law, the lamp within the bushel. Yet Ananias was sent, to give the blessing, to lay on his hand, to loose the sack. When the sack was loosed the silver shone out, and as the scales fell, like bonds of the sack, at once he saw. His bond was unbelief; his release became faith. And so, as though the sack were loosed, that is, when the veil of the Law was laid aside, which is laid over the heart of the Jews, converted to the Lord, free from the bond, having obtained the grace of freedom, he says: And so all of us, beholding the glory of God with unveiled face, are re-formed into the same image. For with the sandal of the Law loosed, he preached the Gospel with the bare foot of free speech. The Jews held him and wished to hinder him, but when the silver shone out in his sack, they tore their garments and went back. For Paul's free preaching for Christ stripped bare the people of the Jews and cut away all their grace,

65. and so they went back, who could not see before them. Those who lose Christ go back. Indeed in the Gospel too, when they were seizing the Lord Jesus for death, going back they fell to the ground. Rightly they were turning back, who were falling back from that heavenly grace into earthly ruin. Morally, then, they were unwilling to go back without their brother; mystically, to return without Paul; for they declared that, if he were lost, the old age of the parent people would be brought down to sorrow.

66. And therefore Judah wished to remain with Joseph, that he might not see the evils that befell his father; that is, he already foresaw and wished to guard against the evils that were to come upon the people of the Jews. But since there was not yet in that type of the leaders of the people of the Jews this same free preaching, Joseph wept; that is, in him Jesus wept.

Chapter XII

67. And he ordered everyone to withdraw, so that he might be recognized by his brothers; for he had not come, as he himself says, except to the sheep of the house of Israel that perished. And sending forth his voice with weeping, I am, he says, Joseph; does my father still live? That is: he spread out his hands to a people unbelieving and contradicting, seeking neither envoy nor messenger, but, as the Lord himself, wishing to save his people. I myself who spoke am present, he says, and made manifest to those who did not seek me, I appear to those who did not ask for me. What else, then, did he cry out at that time but I am Jesus, when, tested by the leaders of the Jews who asked: Are you the Son of God?, he answered: You say that I am; when he said to Pilate: You say that I am a king; for this I was born; when to the chief of the priests, who said: I adjure you by the living God, that you tell us whether you are the Christ, the Son of God, he replied: You have said it. Amen I say to you, from now on you will see the Son of man sitting at the right hand of power and coming with the clouds of heaven? This, then, is what he says: I am Joseph: I am of divine power. Does my father still live? That is: I do not deny my father, I acknowledge my brothers, if either you recognize your brother or the father recognizes his son. Does my people, then, still live, from the family of which people I have chosen a brother for myself?

68. Come near to me, because I have drawn near to you, and so far as to make myself a sharer of your nature by taking on flesh. Do not, at least, flee one who shares in your fellowship, if you do not recognize the author of salvation.

69. And they came near, he says, to him, and he said: I am Joseph your brother, whom you handed over into Egypt. Now therefore do not be sad, nor let it seem hard to you that you sold me here; for God sent me before you for life. How brotherly his love, how sweet his brotherhood, that he even excused the fratricidal deed, saying that it had been the work of divine providence, not of human impiety, since he was not offered up by men to death but sent by the Lord to life! What else does that intercession of our Lord Jesus Christ hold, who surpassed all brothers in love, when, placed on the cross, he says for the people: Father, forgive them; for they do not know what they do? What else does that holy greeting hold, when he says in the midst of the disciples: Peace to you: it is I, do not fear? And when, troubled and terrified, they thought they saw a spirit, he said to them again: Why are you troubled, and why do thoughts rise up in your heart? See, behold my hands and my feet, that it is I myself. Handle and see, for a spirit does not have flesh and bones, as you see that I have. These mysteries, then, which were to come in later times, were already revealed at that time.

70. Indeed they are expressed in the very same words, so that we may understand that it is he himself who spoke both earlier in Joseph and afterwards in his own body, since he did not even change the words. For he said then: Do not be sad, and below: Go up to my father and say to him: Thus says your son Joseph: God has made me lord of the whole land of Egypt. And in the Gospel he says: Do not fear. Go, tell my brothers to go into Galilee, and there they will see me. And below he says: All power has been given to me in heaven and on earth, that is to say: it was of divine disposition, not of human bitterness, that I should receive power. He who recounts the reward does not reproach them with the crime.

71. But what he has in Genesis: For God sent me before you for life, he renders in the Gospel, saying: Teach all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit; for this is the reward and the life of the saints, that they have redeemed others as well.

72. Notice also that this was not written idly in Genesis: And you will be near me, you and your sons and the sons of your sons; for this is what he said in the Gospel: Behold, I am with you all days until the consummation of the world.

73. How evident, too, is that mystery: that, as though every command had been fulfilled, Joseph embraces his brother Benjamin and fell upon his neck, just as, when the Gospel was fulfilled, Christ embraces Paul with what we might call the arms of his mercy, so as to raise him, who was bent down by an inward opinion, up to heaven, as it were by the neck. Hence he too, raised up by Christ, says: But our manner of life is in heaven.

Chapter XIII

74. And Pharaoh rejoiced that Joseph had recognized his brothers. Hence the news was spread abroad in Pharaoh's house, and he urged holy Joseph to invite his brothers to come with their father, and he orders their packs to be filled with wheat and to be put on wagons. Whence this kindness in a barbarian, unless that great mystery were being shown, that the Church no longer begrudges when the Jews are redeemed, and the Christian people rejoices at this union and helps them with what aid it can, and sends men to preach the good news of the kingdom of God, so that they may be called forth the sooner? To these two robes each are given.

75. And she sends Paul as her envoy, when she brings forth his words, he on whom three hundred gold pieces and five robes of different colour are conferred by Christ. He has three hundred gold pieces who preaches the cross of Christ, and therefore he says: For I did not judge that I knew anything among you except Christ Jesus, and him crucified. And rightly he receives gold pieces, because he preached not in persuasive words of wisdom but in the demonstration of the Spirit. And he receives five robes, either as the disciplines of manifold wisdom, or because, captured by no allurements of the bodily senses, he held victory where others met danger; he overcame all the pleasures of the flesh by singular continence and virtue; no weakness of the body dulled his intellect and his zeal; while he was in the body, he did not know that he had a body. Indeed, caught up into paradise, not knowing whether in the body or outside the body, he heard ineffable words which it was not lawful for a man to speak; and finally he smelled of nothing earthly on earth, as he himself teaches, saying: For we are the good fragrance of Christ to God among those who are being saved.

76. Paul therefore excels and his portion overflows, but yet the other preachers too have their own grace. They receive two robes each. Which ones? Those, surely, about which you ought not to doubt, because you have read what is said of Wisdom: She made double garments for her husband. One is mystical, the other moral. But not all are apostles, not all prophets, not all pastors, not all powers; not all have the grace of healings, not all speak in tongues. Where the rewards are different, the merits are different.

77. And gifts are sent ahead to the father too. The son honours the father; Christ invites his people with promises, invites them with gifts. Those asses carry these gifts, once useless and toiling, now useful; they carry, in a type, the gifts of Christ, they who in the Gospel would carry the giver of the gifts.

78. And he sent away his brothers, and they departed. And Joseph said to them: Do not be angry on the way. How well he teaches that we must beware of anger, since it can separate even brothers who love one another, and that discord must above all be shunned on the way, where the very companionship of the journey ought to hold the fellowship of inviolable grace! But what else did our Lord Jesus say, when he was about to depart from this body and dismissed his disciples, but that they should not be angry on the way, when he said: Peace I leave you, my peace I give you? For where there is peace, anger has no place, discord is banished, dissension is put to flight. This, then, is what he says: My peace I give you, that is, Do not be angry on the way. And see whether by 'on this way' he does not mean that indignation must be guarded against in this course of our whole life, because anger often leads even the innocent into crime; for while we grow angrier than is just and wish to restrain another's sin, we commit graver sins. Therefore the Apostle: Not avenging yourselves, he says, dearly beloved, but give place to wrath; that is, let us turn aside from it, lest it seize us. Hence also the Lord Jesus, sending his disciples out to preach the Gospel, sent them without gold, without silver, without money, without a staff; that is, to take away both the incitements of strife and the instruments of vengeance.

79. And they went up, he says, from Egypt and came into the land of Canaan to Jacob their father, and told him, saying that Joseph your son lives and he is ruler of the whole land of Egypt. What is the land of Canaan? That which was tottering. What, then, is so evident as that the times of the apostles are signified, in which they entered the tottering synagogues of the Jews and preached the power of the Lord Jesus, as we have it in the Acts of the Apostles, where Peter says: This Jesus, therefore, God raised up, of whom we are witnesses. Exalted, therefore, by the right hand of God, having received from the Father the promise of the Holy Spirit, he has poured out this gift which you see? We surely notice how he says both that he lives and that he is ruler of the whole earth, he who opens his granaries of spiritual grace and gives abundance to all. The apostles said these things, but the Jews did not believe; rather, they laid hands on them and thrust the preachers of salvation into custody.

80. Hence it is also written of Jacob: He was dismayed in mind; for he did not believe them. He was dismayed with the feeling of the unbelieving people; but after he learned the deeds of Christ, soothed by such great benefits and works, he took heart again, saying: It is a great thing for me, if my son Joseph still lives. I will go and see him before I die. The first and greatest foundation of faith is to believe in the resurrection of Christ. For whoever believes that he was raised seeks him with haste, approaches him devoutly, and venerates God with his inmost mind; for he believes that he himself will not die, if he believes in the author of the resurrection.

Chapter XIV

81. And Israel, raising himself up, came to the well of the oath and sacrificed a victim to the God of his father Isaac. Rightly is he raised up who hastens to Christ. Faith goes before devotion. First he raised himself up, afterwards he sacrificed; for he sacrifices well who has searched out the knowledge of the Godhead.

82. And God spoke to Israel in a vision by night, saying: Jacob, Jacob. And he said: What is it? Saying: I am the God of your fathers: do not fear, go down into Egypt; for I will make you into a great nation there. I will go down with you into Egypt, and I will lead you forever. How they do not understand what they themselves read, and deny Moses whom they praise, when they refuse to believe his writings! What is more evident than that in this passage they are invited to pass over to the Church of God, and that those who before were confined within the narrow borders of Judaea should migrate to the people of God, which, gathered from the whole world, from all nations and peoples, has been made into a great nation? Indeed, their sound has gone out into all the earth. Jacob, therefore, is called by his sons; that is, the people of the Jews is invited to grace by Peter, John and Paul.

83. Our God himself also urges him on with his own oracle, and promises him the advance of faith, the fruit of his gift, saying to him: Joseph will put his hand upon your eyes. Surely the holy patriarch was not anxious about who would close his eyes, although even in a simple understanding a moral affection is expressed. For if we desire to embrace often those whom we love, how much more, when we are about to depart from this body, do we delight in the very last touch of our dear ones, and are soothed by this provision for the journey! Yet mystically we may understand that the people of the Jews would afterwards come to know God. For this is the mystery: that the true Joseph will put his hands upon its eyes, so that the people that did not see before may now see. Come to the Gospel, read how that blind man was healed, on whom Jesus laid his hand and took away his blindness. For Christ does not lay his hand on those who are going to die, but on those who are going to live; or if on those who are going to die, rightly so, because we die first, so that we may live again; for we cannot live to God unless we first die to sin.

84. Seventy-five souls, then, go down into Egypt, as it is written, and this, mystically, in the number of remission, because after such hardness, after so many sins, they would be held unworthy unless remission of sins were granted them. Judah runs to meet him, that is, the confession of error. The people of the Jews, about to come, sends him ahead as the herald of its coming. Therefore the true Joseph also, that is, the arbiter and interpreter of the Godhead, runs to meet them, because confession now goes before those whom unbelief possessed before. For Christ is the interpreter of the Godhead, because no one has ever seen God, except the only-begotten Son, who is in the bosom of the Father; he has made him known. He will receive in the last times the people of the Jews, now of aged years and weary, not according to its merits but according to the election of his grace, and he will lay his hand upon its eyes to take away its blindness. He has put off its healing for this reason: that the people which before did not think it should believe might believe last, and might lose the prerogative of its earlier election. Hence also the Apostle says: That blindness in part has befallen Israel, until the fullness of the Gentiles should come in, and so all Israel should be saved.

85. The deeds of the patriarchs, therefore, are mysteries of things to come. Indeed Jacob himself speaks thus to his sons: Gather together, that I may announce to you what will befall you in the last days. Gather together and hear Israel your father.
