Commentary on the Song of Songs

Latin source: the commentary on the Song of Songs that William of Saint-Thierry (died 1148) compiled in the twelfth century from passages of Ambrose of Milan's works, chiefly On Isaac and the Soul, Death as a Good, Jacob and the Blessed Life, the Exposition of Psalm 118, On Virgins and the letters, arranged as an exposition of the Song verse by verse, from the text of J.-P. Migne, Patrologia Latina 15 (Paris, 1845), cols. pp. 1851–1961, a reprint of the Maurist edition (Paris, 1686), verified against the page images and collated in full with the Maurist edition. Seven readings of the printed text that make no sense are corrected from Ambrose's own text of the passages William excerpts (Prologue §3 ludere, "to play"; Chapter I §44 ita … uritur; Chapter II §14 quinquagesimo, "fiftieth"; Chapter II §34 intimo sensu, "with her inmost sense"; Chapter III §21 futuris; Chapter V §55 utrum; Chapter V §56 desideratur), and the editors' misprinted Scripture references are corrected. At Chapter V §73 the editions print the word onir, evidently corrupt; it is translated "ivory", as the verse and the sense require, with a note in square brackets. Scripture is translated as William gives it, from Ambrose's Old Latin; italics mark Scripture and the verse of the Song that opens each section. The Scripture references in parentheses and the verse labels are the editors', not William's; Psalms keep the Latin numbering, with the Hebrew number in parentheses. The chapters and the section numbers are the edition's (Chapter II numbers section 50 twice, as printed). The one Greek word (Chapter IV) is kept in Greek letters with an English gloss. The Maurist editors' preface and their notes naming the work of Ambrose from which each passage comes are not translated. This English translation is released into the public domain.

Prologue

1. Let him kiss me with the kiss of his mouth (Song 1:1): here it is not the incitements of foulness but the mysteries of chastity that are celebrated, where the matter is treated spiritually, and with a kind of loving contemplation, concerning the nuptials and the union of Christ and the Church, of uncreated spirit and created, of flesh and spirit. Let us therefore circumcise our heart: let us seek nothing base, nothing bodily; and everything earthly is base. Let us therefore set down nothing earthly, nothing carnal, nothing worldly, nothing bodily, nothing light and changeable in these heavenly words; for the words indeed of the Lord are chaste words, so that in them the spotless and modest purity of the heavenly mysteries may shine forth through spiritual interpretation. Let us not, by some adulterous notion, mingle earthly things with divine, nor violate that inviolable sacrament of the prophetic vision or of the everlasting oracle by the measure of our own nature. For whether it is a matter of kisses or of such things, whatever is offered to religion is wholly fitting, so that we should blush at no service that advances the worship and observance of Christ: as in that dancing of David before the ark; for that dancing was not the companion of delights and luxury, but meant that each one should lift up an unwearied body, and not let his limbs lie sluggish on the ground or grow numb with slow steps. For this teaching we did not invent, but received; so did the heavenly doctrine establish the order of the mystical song.

2. Let him kiss me with the kisses of his mouth; for your breasts are good, better than wine, and the fragrance of your ointments is above all spices. Your name is ointment emptied out (ibid.). This whole passage of delights sounds of play, stirs applause, provokes love. Therefore, she says, the maidens have loved you, and have drawn you; let us run after the fragrance of your ointments. The king has brought me into his tabernacle (ibid. 3). She began with kisses so as to come to the tabernacle; and she, impatient for the Lord's labor and for virtue put to exercise, is moved to open the bolts with her hand, to go out into the field, to dwell in the villages; yet in the beginning she runs after the fragrance of the ointment, and when she has come into the tabernacle, she will exchange the ointment for the villages. Finally, see what she comes to.

3. If it is a wall, she says, let us build upon it towers of silver (Song 8:9). She who was playing with kisses now raises towers, so that, towered with the precious pinnacles of the saints, she may not only frustrate hostile assaults but also build the safe bulwarks of good merits. Even in these temporal marriages the bride is applauded before she is commanded, lest harsh commands give offense before love, fostered by endearments, takes root. The heifer learns to love the sound of a kind of clapping, so that she may not refuse the yoke on her neck; and so she is accustomed to the word of playfulness before the whip of discipline. But once she has put her neck under the yoke, the rein constrains her, the goad urges her, her companions pull, and her yoke-mate encourages her. So too our Virgin had first to play in holy love: to admire the golden supports of the heavenly bed, and at the very entrance of the nuptials to see the doorposts crowned with garlands of leaves, and to drink in the delights of the resounding choir within; lest, made fearful, she should withdraw herself from the Lord's yoke before, being called, she bowed to it. For we must know certain steps of progress and their order, what ought to come first and what after; for to know what you are doing and not to know the order of doing it is not perfect knowledge, since things out of their order commonly give offense. And so, since there are as many opinions as there are men, if anything in our discourse is clear and refined, let all read it; if anything is mellowed, let the more mature approve it; if anything is modest, let it cling to the heart and tinge the cheeks; if anything is flowery, let flowering youth not disapprove of it. We had to stir up the bride's love, for it is written: You shall love the Lord your God (Deut. 6:5): we had to adorn the bride's hair at the wedding with certain curling-irons of speech, for it is written: Clap your hand, and strike with your foot (Ezek. 21:14): we had to strew the everlasting bridal chambers with roses.

Chapter I

1. Life is to be sought before doctrine; for a good life has grace even without doctrine, while doctrine without life has no integrity, For wisdom does not enter into a malevolent soul (Wisd. 1:4): and therefore he says: The wicked will seek me, and will not find me (Prov. 1:28): because the eye of the mind is blinded by wickedness, and, when iniquity darkens it, it cannot find the deep mysteries. First, then, the warfare of life must be practised, and morals corrected. When we have set these in order for their due course, so that there is correction of offence and the grace of purity, then let us come to the study of acquiring knowledge, in its own order and way. First, therefore, come moral things, second mystical: in the former is life, in the latter knowledge; so that, if you seek perfection, there is neither life without knowledge nor knowledge without life, but each supports the other. Following this order of instruction, Solomon wrote the book of Proverbs, in which he set out the moral part more fully, the natural part in Ecclesiastes, the mystical in the Song of Songs: although, if you examine diligently, you will find many mystical things in Proverbs too, and the sweetness of moral things in the Song of Songs. For this is certainly mystical: Wisdom has built herself a house, and set up seven pillars, she has slain her victims, etc. (Prov. 9:1). For in the Song of Songs the mystical shines out in that which is moral, in which the sweetness of endearments and the affection of a lover are expressed.

2. (Verses 1–2.) Let him kiss me with the kiss of his mouth; for your breasts are excellent above wine, and the fragrance of your ointments is above all spices. Picture, then, a virgin betrothed for a long time and burning with a just love, who has come to know many illustrious works of her beloved through the assurance of trustworthy witnesses; who, with her desires held in suspense, has often been put off, and can now no longer bear the delays; who would have done anything to see her bridegroom: at last, having obtained her wish, and troubled with joy at the bridegroom's unexpected coming, she does not seek the opening courtesies of greeting, nor the exchange of words, but at once demands what she had desired. So, then, the holy Church too, which at the beginning of the world was betrothed in paradise, prefigured in the flood, announced through the Law, called through the prophets, when she had long awaited the redemption of men, the beauty of the Gospel, the coming of the beloved, impatient of delay rushes into kisses, saying: Let him kiss me with the kiss of his mouth; and, delighted by the kisses, she adds: for your breasts are excellent above wine.

3. And, to speak more morally, I understand for myself that flesh which had been soaked in Adam with the serpent's poison, which was withering in the stench of crimes, which went forth among the daughters of Zion with lofty neck and nodding eyes, trailing their tunics as they walked and playing with their feet, with ringlets of hair and made-up faces and headbands, and, affected with every adornment, all the more unbecoming; and I understand that this same flesh, taught by very many oracles that he would come who, having shut out the serpent's allurements, would pour in the grace of the Holy Spirit, so that all flesh might see the salvation of God and all flesh might come to God, had withered with longing: but, fearing lest, as impatient, as wanton, as luxurious, as querulous, she should displease as she had before, although she was shaken by the waiting for the Lord's coming, which delayed longer than she could now bear: yet not murmuring nor transgressing, but lifting up pure hands in every place, without anger and dispute, in seemly attire, with modesty and sobriety, adorning herself not with braided hair or gold or pearls or costly clothing, but with those things that befit the grace of chastity and of good conduct, she prays, saying: Let him kiss me with the kiss of his mouth; for your breasts are excellent above wine. Already the flesh wished to cleave to Christ, already it hastened to be wedded, that there might be one spirit, and that what had before been the flesh of a harlot might become the flesh of Christ. Let him kiss, she says, me: the Word of God kisses us when the spirit of knowledge illumines our understanding.

4. Therefore, whether it is the Church, long held in suspense through many ages by the Lord's coming promised to her through the prophets, or the soul which, lifting herself from the body, having renounced luxury and delights and carnal pleasures, having put off also the anxiety of worldly vanities, has long been longing for the infusion of the divine presence and the grace of the saving Word, she is wasted with grief that he comes late, and is afflicted; therefore, as one wounded by love, since she cannot bear his delays, she turns to the Father and asks him to send her God the Word, and declares the cause of her great impatience, saying: Let him kiss me with the kisses of his mouth.

5. She seeks not one kiss but many kisses, that she may satisfy her desire; for she who loves is not content with the scantiness of a single kiss, but demands very many, claims more, and in this way is accustomed to commend herself the more to her beloved. Thus that woman in the Gospel was approved in this way: Because she has not ceased, says the Lord, to kiss my feet, therefore her many sins are forgiven her, because she loved much (Luke 7:46, 47). So this soul too desires many kisses of the Word, that she may be illumined with the light of divine knowledge; for this is the kiss of the Word, namely the light of sacred knowledge. For God the Word kisses us when the Spirit of divine knowledge illumines our heart and the very governing part of man; endowed by it with the nuptial pledge of love, the soul, glad and exultant, says: I opened my mouth and drew forth the spirit (Ps. 118 (119):131). For a kiss is that by which lovers cleave to one another and, as it were, enjoy the sweetness of inward grace. By this kiss the soul cleaves to the Word of God, in that the spirit of the one who kisses is poured over into her: just as those also who kiss each other are not content with a touch of the lips, but seem to pour their spirit into one another.

6. Let him kiss me with the kisses of his mouth. Would you apply it to Christ? Nothing is more pleasing. Would you apply it to your soul? Nothing is more delightful. Let him kiss me: he sees that you are clean from all sin, because offences are of the earth; therefore he judges you worthy of the heavenly sacraments, and therefore invites you to the heavenly banquet, that He may kiss you with the kisses of his mouth. Yet, on account of what follows, your soul, or the human condition, or the Church, saw herself cleansed from all sins, worthy to approach the altar of God. For what is the altar but the form of the body of Christ? She saw the wonderful sacraments, and says: Let him kiss me with the kisses of his mouth; that is, let Christ imprint a kiss on me. Why? Because:

7. Your breasts are better than wine, that is, better are your senses, better your sacraments than wine, than that wine which, although it has sweetness, has gladness, has grace, yet in it is worldly gladness, but in you is spiritual delight. Even then, therefore, Solomon was setting forth the nuptials either of Christ and the Church, or of spirit and flesh and soul. And, as though despising all her own delights and pleasures, longing to cleave to the heavenly commandments, she says: for the precepts of your testaments are excellent above every craving of the flesh and the pleasure of the world; for she remembered that she had formerly fallen in this way in Eve, when she preferred the pleasure of the body to the heavenly commandments. Showing, then, that she loves not only the beauty of the Word and, as it were, his face, but all that is within him, she adds to the grace of the kisses: For your breasts, she says, are good beyond wine, and the fragrance of your ointments is above all spices. She asked for a kiss; God the Word poured himself wholly into her, and bared to her his breasts, that is, his doctrines and the teachings of inward wisdom, and was fragrant with the sweet fragrance of his ointments: captivated by these, she says that the delight of divine knowledge is richer than the gladness of every bodily pleasure.

8. Ointment emptied out, or oil poured out, is your name; that is, this whole world reeked with the unclean impurities of various crimes; now the sweetness of modesty breathes everywhere, the ointment of faith, the flower of integrity. In the Word, too, breathes the fragrance of grace and the remission of sins, which, spread over the whole world, has filled all things as with ointment emptied out, because the heavy filth of vices has been wiped away from all. This ointment was emptied out upon the Jews, and was gathered up by the Gentiles: emptied out in Judea, it gave forth its fragrance in all lands. With this ointment Mary was anointed, and the Virgin conceived, the Virgin brought forth the good fragrance, the Son of God: this ointment was poured out upon the waters and sanctified the waters: with this ointment the three youths were anointed, and the flame bedewed them with moisture; with this ointment Daniel was anointed, and he softened the mouths of the lions and soothed their ferocity. This ointment flows daily and never fails. Take your vessel, virgin, and come near, that you may be filled with this ointment: take the ointment valued at three hundred denarii, but given freely, not sold, that all might have it freely. Virgin, anoint yourself; do not be grieved like Judas because this ointment is poured out, but bury Christ with you in yourself: close your vessel well, lest the ointment flow out: close it with the key of integrity, with modesty in speaking, with abstinence from boasting; she who has this ointment receives Christ: Your name is ointment emptied out.

9. (Verse 2.) Therefore the young maidens have loved you. Who are these young maidens but the souls of individuals, who have laid aside the old age of this body, renewed through the Holy Spirit? Your name is ointment poured out, and nothing can be more excellent than the power of its word. For just as ointment shut up in some vessel holds in its fragrance, and that fragrance, so long as it is confined within the narrow limits of that vessel, although it cannot reach many, yet keeps its strength; but when the ointment has been poured out of the vessel in which it was shut, it is said to be spread far and wide: so too the name of Christ, before his coming, was shut up among the people of Israel, as in a vessel, in the minds of the Jews: for Known indeed in Judea is God, in Israel his name is great. (Ps. 75 (76):1). This, then, is the name which the vessels of the Jews held confined within their narrow limits. Great indeed was the name even then, when it was held within the narrow limits of the weak and the few, but it had not yet poured out its greatness through the hearts of the Gentiles and to the ends of the whole world: but after he shone upon the whole world by his coming, he extended that divine name of his through every creature, not filled up by any addition, for fullness knows no increase, but filling what was empty, that his name might be wonderful in all the earth. The pouring out of this name, then, signifies a certain abundant overflowing of graces and a richness of heavenly goods, for whatever is poured out overflows from abundance. Therefore the young maidens have loved you. Prudence is good, she says, but mercy is sweet; for few attain the former, the latter reaches all: on account of this indulgence of yours, she says, souls renewed in spirit love you.

10. (Verse 3.) Draw us after you; we will run toward the fragrance of your ointments. Having now kissed the Word of God, the holy soul knows no measure and is not satisfied, saying: You are sweet, O Lord, and in your delightfulness teach me your justifications (Ps. 118 (119):68). Having kissed the Word of God, she desires him above all beauty, loves him above all gladness, delights in him above all spices, longs to see him often, to gaze on him frequently, longs to be drawn that she may be able to follow: Your name, she says, is ointment emptied out, therefore we young maidens love you; therefore we strive, but cannot lay hold of you: draw us, that we may be able to run; that by the fragrance of your ointments we may receive the power to follow. She hastens also to see the inner mysteries, the very rest of the Word, the very dwelling of that highest good, and his light and brightness in that bosom and recess of the Father; she hastens to hear his words, and when she has heard them she receives them as above all sweetness.

11. Let the prophet who tasted it teach you; he says: How sweet are your words to my throat, above honey and the honeycomb to my mouth (Ps. 118 (119):103)! For what else does the soul desire that has once tasted the sweetness of the Word, that has once seen his brightness? Moses, placed on the mountain for forty days receiving the Law, did not seek the food of the body: Elijah, hastening to that rest, asked that his soul might be taken from him: Peter, himself also beholding on the mountain the glory of the Lord's resurrection, did not wish to come down, saying: Lord, it is good for us to be here (Matt. 17:4). How great, then, is that glory of the divine substance, how great the gifts of the Word, into which even the angels long to look!

12. The soul, therefore, that sees that, does not seek this body, and understands that she ought to have the least familiarity with it; she renounces the world, withdraws herself from the bonds of the flesh, and puts off all the ties of these pleasures. Thus Stephen saw Jesus and did not fear being stoned; indeed, while he was being stoned, he prayed not for himself but for those by whom he was being killed. Paul too, caught up to the third heaven, did not know whether he was in the body or without the body; caught up, I say, into paradise, he no longer felt the use of his own body, and, hearing the words of God, he was ashamed to come down to the infirmities of the body. And so, knowing what he had seen in paradise, or what he had heard, he cried out, saying: Why, as though living of this world, do you still make decrees? Do not touch, do not defile, do not taste, things which all tend to corruption by their very use (Col. 2:20 and 21). For he wished us to be in the figure of this world, not in possession and use, so that we may use this world as though not using it, as passing through, not as settling down; walking as in the image of the world, not in desire of it, that by the swiftest reasoning we may pass beyond the very image of this world. Thus he himself, walking by faith, not by sight, was absent from the body and present with God: and while he was on earth, he lived not among earthly but among heavenly things. Therefore let our soul, which wishes to draw near to God, lift herself from the body, let her always cleave to that highest good, to that good which is divine, which is always, and which was from the beginning, and which was with God (1 John 1), that is, the Word of God: this is that divine one In whom we live, and are, and move (Acts 17:28).

13. We will run toward the fragrance of your ointments. This fragrance is breathed forth by the soul that begins to open to Christ, so that she receives first the fragrance of the Lord's burial, and believes that his flesh did not see corruption nor wither with any odour of death, but rose seasoned with the fragrance of that eternal and ever-green flower. For how could he wither even in the flesh whose name is ointment emptied out? He emptied himself that he might be fragrant for you; this ointment always was, but it was with the Father, it was in the Father, it was fragrant only to angels and archangels, as within the vessel of heaven. The Father opened his mouth, saying: Behold, I have set you as a covenant of my race, as a light of the Gentiles, that you may be for salvation to the end of the earth (Isa. 49:6). The Son came down; all things were filled with the new fragrance of the Word; the heart of the Father uttered a good Word; the Son was fragrant, the Holy Spirit breathed it forth and spread it through the hearts of all: For the love of God has been poured out in our hearts through the Holy Spirit (Rom. 5:5). The Son of God himself at first held in his fragrance in the body as in a vessel, awaiting the time, as he says: The Lord gives me a tongue of learning, that I may know when I ought to speak a word. The hour came, he opened his mouth, the ointment went out, when power went out from him. And therefore she hastens to the Word and asks to be drawn, lest perhaps she be left behind; because the Word of God runs, and is not bound. Thus He exults like a giant to run his course (Ps. 18 (19):6). And because His going forth is from the highest heaven, and his meeting even to its highest point (ibid. 7), seeing herself unequal to such great swiftness, she says: Draw us.

14. Good is the soul that asks not for herself alone but for all: Draw, I say, us; for we have the desire to follow, which the grace of your ointments breathes into us: but because we cannot match your course, Draw us, that, supported by your help, we may be able to tread in your footsteps. For if you draw us, we too will run, and we will catch the spiritual breezes of swiftness. For the burden is laid down by those for whom your hand is a support; and your oil is poured in, by which he was healed who had been wounded by robbers.

15. But lest it seem shameless to you that she says: Draw us, hear him saying: Come to me, all you who labour and are burdened, and I will refresh you (Matt. 11:28). You see that he gladly draws us, so that we may not remain behind as we follow: but let him who wishes to be drawn run, that he may lay hold, and run forgetting what lies behind and reaching out to what lies ahead, for so he will be able to lay hold of Christ; whence the Apostle too says: So run that you may lay hold. She too wishes to reach the prize, she who desires to lay hold. Prudently, then, she asks to be drawn, because not all are able to follow. Thus, when Peter said: Where are you going? the Word of God answered: You cannot follow me now, but you will follow afterwards (John 13:36; Matt. 14:30). He had entrusted to him the keys of the kingdom of heaven, and yet he judged him unequal to following him. Yet he did not put off this soul, because she did not presume, but asked. Thus,

16. (Verse 3.) He has brought me into his chamber. Blessed is the soul that enters the inner recesses of the Word, for, rising up from the body, she becomes more remote from all things; and she searches and seeks that which is above itself, that divine thing, if in any way she can attain it: and when she has been able to lay hold of it, having passed beyond the things that are intelligible, she is established in it and is fed by it. Such was Paul, who knew that he had been caught up into paradise, but did not know whether it was out of the body or in the body. For his soul had risen up from the body, and had withdrawn and lifted herself from the inward parts and bonds of the flesh, and, become a stranger to himself, he kept within himself the ineffable words which he heard and could not divulge, which he perceived it is not lawful for a man to speak. The good soul, then, despises things visible and sensible, and does not stay in them, nor does she linger and rest in looking at these things: but she ascends to those things which are eternal and invisible and full of wonders, lifting herself up with the pure sense of a devout mind. For, striving after perfection, she aims at that sole good of the Godhead, and thinks nothing else is to be sought, because she holds what is highest.

17. The king has brought me into his chamber. The kiss is a simple thing, but the secrecy of the chamber is full of business; for in this place she testifies that she wishes to know the mysteries more fully, to enter the inner recesses of the heavenly mysteries, and that the treasures of wisdom and knowledge hidden in Christ may be opened to her: and that she may be able to do this, perhaps she says what she said above: Let him kiss me with the kisses of his mouth. It signifies the grace of the Holy Spirit coming upon her, as the Angel said to Mary: The Holy Spirit will come upon you, and the power of the Most High will overshadow you (Luke 1:35). But when the king brought her into his chamber is declared by the time of the passion, the piercing of the side, the shedding of the blood, the ointment of the burial, the mystery of the resurrection; so that she received the kiss as a bride, but the Church was brought into the chamber of Christ no longer as one only betrothed, but also as one wedded: and she has not only entered the bridal chamber, but has also obtained the keys of lawful union. And therefore, as one placed in the bridal chamber, she says: A bundle of the drop is my brother to me, he rests between my breasts (Verse 12).

18. And if we seek the chamber, he himself teaches us, who says: But you, when you pray, enter into your chamber, and, having shut the door, pray to your Father in secret (Matt. 6:6). The chamber of the Church is the body of Christ. The king brought her into all the inner mysteries, he gave her the keys, that she might open for herself the treasures of knowledge, throw open the doors of the sacraments that were shut before, and know the grace of him who rested, the sleep of him who died, the power of him who rose again. In that chamber the bride found the justices of the Lord Jesus. What are those justices? Surely the sacraments of baptism, as we read, for when John said to him as he came to baptism: I ought to be baptized by you, and do you come to me; Jesus answered: Let it be so now, for thus it is fitting for us to fulfil all justice (Matt. 3:15). In that chamber she learns the justifications of the Lord, she has come to know the counsel of God, than which nothing can be more sublime; for this is the divine thing, that in which the remission of sins takes place.

19. She seeks to learn from the Lord these things, which men could not teach unless God had first taught them: and after the apostles learned them from Christ, the Church says: The king has brought me into his chamber, that is, into that secret place in which are all the treasures of his learning and knowledge. And therefore it is said to you: When you pray, enter into your chamber (Matt. 6:6). which signifies the secret place of the mind and soul. Into this chamber of justices the bride asked to be brought, when she had run out after the fragrance of the ointment, which always flows and never fails.

20. The king has brought me into his chamber; the Greek has "into his storeroom" and "into his cellar"; where there are good libations, where good fragrances, sweet honeys, where diverse fruits, where various dishes are, that your meal may be seasoned with very many dishes. Thus, brought into that secret place of that divine bridegroom:

21. Let us exult, she says, and be glad in you; let us love your breasts above wine; for she exults not in riches and treasures of gold and silver, not in the fruits of possessions, not in powers, not in banquets, but in God alone, saying to him: Let us love your breasts above wine. Yet she drank in such a way that she was not swallowed up by the wine, but by its grace drew in gladness of heart, and did not totter with drunkenness of the body. Thus, drinking this wine, she opened her eyes and saw the straight way, she left the crooked ways, saying: Equity has loved you, that is, winding paths do not follow you: but only the path of justice can reach you, for he who loves justice does not turn away from Christ. For how should an innocent conscience fear the judge of justice and the rewarder of merits? Yet the same soul, knowing that she has been darkened by fellowship with the body, says to other souls, or to the other heavenly powers appointed to the sacred mystery: Do not look at me, because I have been darkened, because the sun has not looked upon me.

22. (Verse 4.) I am dark and comely, daughters of Jerusalem. Picture the Lord Jesus reclining at the banquet, John leaning on his breast, the others marvelling that the servant was leaning on the Lord, that that sinful flesh was reclining on the temple of the Word, that that soul, bound in the bonds of the flesh, was searching out the hall of the divine fullness. While the others marvel at these things, then, the soul of John answered: I am dark and comely, daughters of Jerusalem; dark through guilt, comely through grace. The flesh too says, I am dark and comely; dark with the dust of the world, which it gathered in its struggle, comely with the spiritual eye, with which it wiped away the dust and filth of this world: dark through vice, but comely now through the washing, which has washed away every offence: I am dark because I sinned, comely because Christ now loves me; for the one whom he had banished in Eve, he receives back in the virgin, he takes up from Mary.

23. The Synagogue too says it, whose mysteries seem to most to be expressed in this place; when she saw that she was rejected because of the impiety of the whole people, she yet consoled herself, because she saw not only Christ himself from her own people, but also Peter, John and James cleaving to Christ, because she had the oracles entrusted to her. And therefore she said: I am dark and comely, daughters of Jerusalem: dark through unbelief, comely through the Law: dark through the fall, comely because the sun has loved me, and I was made first the congregation of God. Do not flee from me because I am dark; I am dark for this reason, that the sun of justice, who used before to illumine me, has left me. I have lost the colour of my face, the keenness of my eyes, with which I used to see the sun, has been dulled: I walk in darkness, I who do not know the day of Christ. Yet do not despise me, since he who has left me can look upon me again. He is accustomed to have mercy again, to gather the scattered, to seek out the deserted, to collect the forsaken. All you tribes of Israel, come together, look upon me, comely before my offences; do not look at me, because I have been darkened; that is, do not look only at this, that I have been darkened; the sun has not looked upon me, and therefore I have been darkened. But the sun shines upon the just and the unjust, upon the just for grace, upon the unjust for mercy; to the former granting the reward of merits, to the latter forgiving sins. And it did not shine upon the Gentiles before; now it shines, now he rises for them who used to rise for me; he who forgave them will forgive me also. Do not think, because I have been darkened, that the sun has utterly left me, and no longer looks upon me, no longer seeks me out in my sickness. He has turned his eyes from me, because I did not keep his commandments: he will be reconciled when he sees my repentance for my offences. The sun has not seen me, because I did not receive him when he came, I did not open the windows that the light of life might enter: when I have opened them, he will illumine my eyes, he who came to illumine the whole world, so that even those who do not see may see.

24. Or the Church from the Gentiles says: Do not look at me because I am dark, because the sun has not looked upon me; because the sun of justice for a long time judged the congregation of the Gentiles, bound as with the winter frosts and ice of Gentile error, unworthy for him to light it up with the serene light of his countenance. Did it not seem to you the stiffness of the winter season, when God was known only in Judea? But now the fullness of summer light shines, when Christ is all and in all. Is not the earth the Lord's, and its fullness (Ps. 23 (24):1)? And truly the whole world is in the Church, because there is not only Jew or Greek, not barbarian or Scythian, not slave or free: but we are all one in Christ (Col. 3:11). The sun shines for all, the day gives light to all. I am black, and comely. She put "black" first, to increase the beauty: she did not say: I am black comely, lest what is black should be thought comely: but she says: I am black by former sin, but comely by confession of sin, and by zeal for amendment, and by love of virtue. Although, then, the syllable Et ("and") is, as the grammarians say, conjunctive, yet it has a distinction by which confusion is distinguished and separated; so that if you say that Ambrosius Bassus is inside, one man is thought of: but if you assert that Ambrosius and Bassus are inside, two are certainly understood.

25. Like the tents of Kedar, like the skins of Solomon; for tents are made from the hides of dead animals. She, therefore, who said this was dead to sin and alive to God: let us also, then, die to sin, that we may live to God. Filled with spirit, with the delight and sweetness of gladness, we shall be spiritual hides, free of bodily infirmity, and keeping in the whole bosom of the mind the grace of the divine mysteries poured into us. That this body is called the tents of Kedar or the skins of Solomon is clear from many passages: yet understand it also from this, that Adam and Eve, when they laid aside the image of the heavenly one which they bore before, and put on the image of the earthly man, are said to have been clothed in tunics of skin; for the sin committed had made them bodily instead of spiritual.

26. Nor is it absurd to understand that the bride says she has become like skins scorched by the heat of the sun; in that, although placed in the harshness of tribulations, she has yet mortified her body, so that she cannot feel that harshness: for as the skin of a tent does not feel the heat of the sun, because it is the skin of a dead animal, so she, dead to sin, could not feel the heat of sin: either because she was not broken by necessities, or because we are a body of affliction, and ought always to carry about the death of the Lord Jesus in our body.

27. (Verse 5.) The sons of my mother have fought against me; that is, the passions of the body have assailed me, the allurements of the flesh have discoloured me: therefore the sun of justice has not shone upon me, because, bereft of protection, I could not keep my devotion and full observance; for this is: I have not kept my vineyard, because I brought forth thorns and not grapes, that is, committing sins instead of fruits. And when she speaks of the Word, with the splendour of the word shining upon her, she turns to him and says:

28. (Verse 6.) Where do you pasture, where do you rest at midday? Rightly she says, where do you pasture, because the Word of God is royal: where do you rest, because it is moral: at midday, because it is mystical: since indeed at midday Joseph, set at the banquet with his brothers, revealed the mysteries of times to come. But David also says: Reveal your way to the Lord and hope in him, and he will do it, and he will bring forth your justice as the light, and your judgment as the midday (Ps. 36 (37):5). And Paul himself declared that a light shone round him at midday, when he was turned from persecution to grace.

29. She complains, then, that she has been forsaken, that she has been left destitute, poor instead of rich; for she abounded in the gift of graces: but she began to be in need when the abundance of the divine presence was denied her. And therefore she asks to be held even as a hired servant, she who before claimed for herself the grace of a more precious union.

30. They have set me as keeper in the vineyards; my own vineyard I have not kept (Verse 5). Some think that this is said of those who bind the Synagogue to the precepts of the Law, that she should keep the Law and guard her vineyard, which she could not keep. Thus she guarded it that it might yield grapes, but it yielded thorns. What the vineyard is, then, David comes to testify to us, saying: You have brought a vineyard out of Egypt, and you have cast out the nations and planted it (Ps. 79 (80):9). The sons of her mother can also be the apostles, who truly fought against the Synagogue, saying: Behold, we turn to the Gentiles: and they began to sow the word of God among the nations. The prophets too can be understood, who, by warning and announcing that the Synagogue should keep her vineyard, achieved nothing; for she, who ought to have borne rich fruits of virtue, made thorns of wickedness: and therefore she confesses that she could not keep her people, and rightly seeks late to hold him whom she lost when she held him: far unlike her who says: I found him whom my soul loves, I held him, and I will not let him go (Song 3:4). Thus that one invites the bridegroom into her garden; this one, asking where he pastures, says to him:

31. Tell me, you whom my soul has loved. Why do you say whom my soul has loved, and not whom it loves? Why did you let go him whom you held? You loved him by the faith of the fathers, you lost him by unbelief: you held him by the bonds of love, you lost him by the long rope of faithlessness. Therefore you do not know where he pastures, where he rests; for if you knew, you would not ask. For you say: Where do you pasture, where do you rest at midday? You know that the midday is the Church, which holds Christ, and you seek him in the nights. Tell me, she says, O Christ, answer, if only because you were formerly dear and beloved to me; although I have lost the prerogative of so great a love, yet you, as one who is dutiful, answer me: Where do you pasture, where do you rest? You have left me, you have gone away to the Gentiles, you have withdrawn far from me, and have become nearer to those from whom you were far. But you have become near, because they believed by your blood. You withdrew from me, because I did not receive the cross as the redemption of the world, but as the condemnation of a guilty man. But those who received him as the author of salvation are at midday; for them you give light, upon them you shine forth, for them your grace is warm like midday. To me you were the morning, when I still believed, but I did not believe fully, because I was not found at midday, like Joseph with his brothers, who kept midday: you have become midday for those who feed on your riches and hope in you. Therefore, as David said: You will bring forth their justice as the light, and their judgment as the midday (Ps. 36 (37):6).

32. You seek, then, as a stranger, you who were the nearest; as one poor, you who were rich: you wish to follow, you who used to go before, and would that you might at least follow those whom you ought to have gone before! You desire to be a hired servant, you who before used to gather hired servants; and so this is the voice of a hired servant, saying: Lest perhaps I become as one veiled over the flocks of your companions. You who before used to receive proselytes from the Gentiles now yourself wish to be received among the nations as a proselyte, and to be gathered in as a newcomer. The Word of God answered the longing soul:

33. (Verse 7.) Unless you know yourself, O beautiful among women. "Know yourself, O man" is said to your soul, unless you have known yourself fair among women. Know yourself, O soul, that you are not of earth, not of clay, because God breathed into you and made you a living soul. You are a magnificent work, formed by the breath of God: Attend to yourself, as the Law says, that is, to your soul: let not worldly and earthly things hold you, nor earthly things delay you. Hasten with all your intention to him by whose breath you subsist: A great thing, he says, is man, and precious is a merciful man: but a faithful man it is a task to find (Prov. 20:6). Learn, O man, where you are great and precious. The earth shows you to be vile, but virtue makes you glorious, faith rare, the image precious. For where is anything so precious as the image of God? which ought first to pour faith into you, so that a certain likeness of the author may shine in your heart, lest he who questions your mind should fail to recognize the author. Or is anything so precious as humility, so that, looking closely at the nature of body and soul, you subject yourself in the one and recognize yourself in the other? The allurement of the flesh, prone to vice, urges mercy; because what you bestow on another you pay to yourself: whatever proceeds from you runs back into you, and whatever you do, you pay to yourself: and whatever good you do profits you. The vigour of a living soul, the sense of reason, capable of understanding and judgment, so that it may seem a worthy house for so great an inhabitant, should not lose the prerogative of its nature. Unless you know yourself, O beautiful among women. You who complain that you have been forsaken: unless you know yourself, unless you repent of your fall, unless you prove the intention of devotion, unless your faith and sincerity increase, your complaint will profit nothing. Or thus: unless you know yourself, that you are beautiful, unless you keep the beauty of your nature, and the allurements of the body do not sink you, nor hindrances hold you back, the nobility of a better creation will avail you nothing.

34. Know yourself, therefore, and the beauty of your nature, and go out as one freed from the bonds of the feet, and stretched forth with bare footstep, so that you do not feel the coverings of the flesh: let not bodily bonds entangle the footstep of your mind, so that your foot may appear beautiful. For such are those who are chosen by the Lord to announce the kingdom of heaven, of whom it is said: How beautiful are the feet of those who bring the good news of peace (Isa. 52:7). Such was Moses, to whom it is said: Loose the shoe from your feet (Exod. 3:5); so that he who was to go forth to call the people to the kingdom of God might first cast away the hides of the flesh and walk with bare spirit and with the footstep of the mind; this, then, is what he says: Go out in the shoes of the flocks, and feed your kids in the tents of the shepherds.

35. By the flocks we understand a kingdom, because it belongs to power to preside over flocks. Each one presides over himself with a certain royal power, if he restrains in himself the luxuries of the body and brings his flesh into servitude; and therefore it is said: The kingdom of God is within you. Whence he beautifully says to the soul: Go out, that is, go out from servitude, go out from the rule and dominion of the flesh, and go out not in the flesh but in the spirit, go out to the governance of power. And therefore he added: Feed your kids, that is, rule those things that are on your left; for if they are not ruled, they easily slip. Restrain the wantonness, the lasciviousness of your body and its irrational luxury, tame its fickle movements: feed them not in bodily tents, but in the tents of the shepherds who know how to rule the flock; for lovely are the tents of Israel, like groves casting shade over a river, in which the soul, placed as though in battle array, wages a good warfare and spies out the enemy's attacks.

36. Unless you know yourself, O beautiful among women. What is it to know oneself, but that each should know that he is a man made in the image and likeness of God, capable of reason, who ought to cultivate his land like a good farmer with a certain plough and pruning-hook of wisdom, so that what is hard may be split, or what is overgrown cut back; who ought to govern the inner portion of himself by the command of the mind? Whence also in the Law it is written: Attend to yourself, lest a hidden word arise in your heart (Deut. 15:9): attend, he says, to yourself; he certainly does not say to your money, not to your possessions, not to the strength of your body, but to your soul and your mind, from which all counsels, deeds and thoughts flow. Attend to yourself, then, there, where you know that you are of greater worth. "Know yourself": this the Gentile men assign to Pythian Apollo, as though he were the author of this saying; whereas they transfer to their own what was taken from ours, and Moses, who wrote the book of Deuteronomy, was far earlier than the philosophers who fashioned these things.

37. Whence Solomon too, following the divine oracle, wrote in the Song of Songs: Unless you know yourself, O beautiful among women; that is, unless you know yourself as a rational mortal, and quickly confess your sins, quickly tell your iniquities that you may be justified, unless you are converted and first accuse your own offences, the day of death comes and there is no longer any remedy of conversion; you are overtaken while you are thinking. Light your torch before the bridegroom's door is shut on you; he is not accustomed to wait long for the negligent. Unless you know yourself, he says, O beautiful among women, and say: I am dark and comely: I am dark, because I have sinned, comely, because I am loved, because I am of the race of Abraham, a chosen race, beloved of God, the grace of the fathers will profit you nothing: For God is able from these stones to raise up children to Abraham (Matt. 3:9). And elsewhere in the Gospel you have read that the devil bound a daughter of Abraham with the bonds of his wickedness, and the Lord loosed her on the sabbath day. Unless, then, you know yourself, it will profit you nothing. Even if you say, I am a daughter of Abraham, you who do not believe, you who do not correct your error: Abraham indeed is saved, but the nobility of your race will not help you, unless faith has saved you. Let not the promise given to the fathers deceive you: I do not accept the person of a man, I do not accept the prerogative of race, unless I see a nobility of character fitting the race, so that the choice of a just race may be made.

38. But if you know yourself, and recognize that you are liable to sin, you must go out in the heels of the flocks. Go out, then, barefoot, and feed your kids in the tents of the shepherds. He who is without sin feeds lambs, feeds sheep: but let her who is liable to sin feed the kids, which are on the left; for she cannot be on the right hand of the good shepherd. Let her rather follow him who says to Peter: Come behind me, Satan (Mark 8:33). By following, Peter deserved to be placed on the right hand; and therefore it is said to him: Feed my little lambs (John 21:16). But hear where she is to go out: To the tents, he says, of the shepherds, that is, to the Gentiles, to the dispersion. And therefore he said: I will strike the shepherd, and the sheep of the flock will be scattered (Zech. 13:7), that the whole world might be filled with the flock of Christ, signifying that the Synagogue will be saved then, when she too has joined herself to this dispersion which has filled this world; for the tents of the shepherds are the kingdoms of the earth.

39. (Verse 8.) To my mare among the chariots of Pharaoh I have likened you, my nearest one. In this, while the likeness of the Church seems to be compared to the running of swift horses, her rich grace is esteemed. Do not, then, understand one mare of a rich king, but the word used absolutely for the cavalry, as we have often said, as "his sheep" is said for a flock of sheep. Pharaoh, then, as a most powerful and very rich king, had powerful horses; whence Scripture too says: The chariots of Pharaoh (Exod. 14:9), as a most powerful kind for the use of wars and a support in fighting: thus with these chariots Pharaoh easily overtook the fleeing Hebrews. Just as, then, horses yoked together easily draw a chariot, and patiently take the yoke, and carry it with grace, and grow gentle by taking up that yoke: so too the congregation of the nations, untamed in its Gentile ways, used formerly to toss itself about. But when it took up the yoke of him who says: Take my yoke upon you, for it is light; and my burden, for it is sweet (Matt. 11:29 and 30); and the bride of Christ began to be exalted by the concord and gentleness of the peoples, and to be carried around the whole world, like a chariot with swift horses she was caught up above the world and ascended to the bridegroom.

40. For Christ has his horses, of which the prophet says: You sent your horses into the sea, troubling many waters (Hab. 3); because the apostles, by preaching the Gospel, stirred up the peoples of the Gentiles, who are moved like many waters and are roused into waves of waters, so that, rising up from the earthly ceremonies of idols, they might believe in Christ. And above he says: You mounted your horses, your cavalry is health. O wonderful team of twelve good horses, whose bridles are of peace, whose reins are of love: bound to one another by the bonds of concord, and subject to the yoke of faith, carrying on four wheels the mystery of the Gospel to the ends of the whole world, bearing the good charioteer, the Word of God: by whose whip the allurements of the world have been put to flight, the prince of this world cast out, the course of the just fulfilled. O great contest of rational horses! O wonderful mystery! A wheel ran within a wheel and was not hindered, the New Testament in the Old Testament; it ran within that by which it was announced. The wheels went in four directions, and did not turn back; because the spirit of life was in them, which ran into the four parts of the whole world: and it ran without stumbling, because the good life of the horses squared with it. The horses, then, ran, because he who had mounted the horses did not sleep.

41. Jesus, then, is the charioteer of our souls; he wishes us too to mount our horses, that is, our bodies, and always to keep watch, lest it be said to us: Those who mounted horses have slumbered (Ps. 75 (76):7). This sea must be crossed without sloth; it is scarcely crossed even by those who keep watch. But if anyone sleeps, he will not be able to cross, but is sunk, like the Egyptian, whose soul and body both perished. For The horse indeed and its rider he has thrown into the sea (Exod. 15:1), those who did not follow the law, but persecuted it.

42. To my mare among the chariots of Pharaoh I have likened you. The labour of virtue seeks victory, so that it cannot be compared to that mare which is Solomon's, swift to run, apt for bearing; since fruitfulness of the soul is desired and sought. This mare, then, is precious, and the chariots of Pharaoh swift. But in this place we have undertaken to speak of the soul, of this mare which is reckoned like this soul, that is, of prophetic or apostolic virtue: in that she is numbered in the flock of those who by the fruitfulness of their preaching have filled the spaces of the whole world. And although they were placed in the body, they suffered no loss in their spiritual course. And therefore she is praised because, with the heavenly precept illuminating her, she is now beautiful, now lovely, she who displays in her face the beauty of chastity, and lifts up the necklaces of her neck, on which are the insignia of patience and humility. And therefore, praising his bride, he said:

43. (Verse 9.) How beautiful your cheeks have become, like a turtledove's, your neck like necklaces. The face is freer where there is the consciousness of love; and to bear the yoke of Christ is sweet, if you consider it the ornaments of your neck, not burdens. Lift up your eyes, then, always to your Lord, and seek God, that you may find him. Raise your neck: you wear necklaces, not chains: even dumb animals rejoice in necklaces, and seem to themselves to be adorned with trappings rather than tied. Cheeks like a turtledove's: let them bear the insignia of modesty: The necklaces of the neck: let them lift up the confidence of freedom; for the yoke of Christ is light, and therefore the neck is not pressed down by it, but lifted up.

44. (Verses 10–11.) Likenesses of gold, he says, we will make for you, with studs of silver, until the king is at his reclining. For from among those who are of the Law and the prophets, men had before believed only moderately in the glory of the Lord Jesus; and his inheritance, spread through the peoples, the more often it was tested, the more it was proved. For the frequent persecutions of the Church have produced for us the titles of the just, the victories of martyrdom. And so, as good gold, so the Church, when it is burned, feels no loss, but rather its brightness is increased, until Christ comes into his kingdom and reclines his head on the faith of the Church. He, when he had come to the lost sheep of the house of Israel, had nowhere to lay his head: but now faith is fragrant, and therefore the Church says: My nard has given its fragrance; and she says it with confidence, awaiting the reward. The ointment of grace is fragrant, in which the virgin brought forth, and the Lord Jesus took up the sacrament of the incarnation.

45. (Verses 12–13.) A bundle of the drop is my brother to me, he will remain between my breasts, or he will dwell. She had said earlier that her nard had given her the fragrance of the bridegroom, and that through the ointment with which she had anointed him she had received the fragrance of his odour: but now she says: My brother is fragrant to me of a drop, and this not spread abroad nor scattered at will, but bound together and compressed, so that the fragrance of his sweetness may be rendered denser and stronger. And he, she says, being such, dwells and abides between my breasts, and makes his rest and dwelling in the place of my breast. A bundle, she says, of the drop is my kinsman, a cluster of henna is my brother to me, he will rest between my breasts. For the Lord Jesus, taking up a body, bound himself with the bonds of love, and joined himself not only to our members and natural passions, but also to the cross; and therefore, like a cluster, he rests in the faith of the Church and in moral grace.

46. A nard of henna is my kinsman in the vineyards of En-gedi. If we seek the place, that of a certain region which is in Judea, the place in which opobalsam is produced is so called: if the interpretation, it signifies in Latin "temptation". In those vineyards there is a tree which, if one pierces it, gives out ointment; this is the fruit of the tree. If the tree is not cut, it is not so fragrant and does not so give off its scent; but when it has been pierced by the hand of the craftsman, then it distils a tear. So too Christ, crucified on that tree of temptation, wept over the people, that he might wash away our sins, and from the bowels of his mercy poured out ointment, saying: Father, forgive them, for they do not know what they do (Luke 23:34). Then, therefore, when he was pierced on the tree by the lance, there came out from him blood and water, sweeter than every ointment, a victim acceptable to God, pouring out the fragrance of sanctification through the whole world. And like balsam from a tree, so power went out from his body; whence he says: I perceive that power has gone out from me (Luke 8:46). Hence it is more expressively called opobalsam, because by the piercing of the tree the balsam bursts forth through the hollow of the puncture.

47. Jesus, then, when pierced, poured out the fragrance of the remission of sins and of redemption. For he was also compressed, when, being the Word, he was made man: and he became poor, though he was rich, that we might be enriched by his poverty: he was powerful, and he offered himself to be despised, so that Herod scorned him and mocked him: he moved the earth, and he hung on the tree: he covered heaven with darkness, and crucified the world, and was crucified: he bowed his head, and the Word went forth: he was emptied out, and he filled all things: God descended, man ascended: the Word was made flesh, that the flesh might claim for itself the throne of the Word at the right hand of God: he was a wound, and ointment flowed: the beetle was heard, and God was recognized. And Christ too answered.

48. (Verse 14.) Behold, you are good, my nearest one, behold, you are good: your eyes are doves. Because the Church had come to know the mystery, and was preaching the Lord Jesus crucified for the redemption of the whole world, she deserves to hear: Behold, you are good, you who call me good; Behold, you are good, you who have seen the beauty of my glory, and are yourself beautiful and comely. But what is it for Christ to say: You are good, or you are comely, but that word of the Gospel: Be steadfast, daughter, your sins are forgiven you (Matt. 9:22)? So she to whom Christ has forgiven sins rightly says: Reward your servant, that I may live, and keep your commandments (Ps. 118 (119):17). She has nothing in the reward to despair of; because the Lord Jesus came to save the world, not to destroy it. Therefore he is unmindful of injury, mindful of grace, as he himself testifies in the prophetic book, saying: I am, I am he who blots out your iniquities, and I will not remember them: but you, remember, and let us be judged; tell your iniquities first, that you may be justified (Isa. 43:25 and 26). Whoever, then, tells his iniquities to God is justified: and whoever is justified does not fear the reward, but demands it: he who does not fear the reward lives.

49. (Verses 15–16.) Behold, she says, my kinsman is fair, and indeed beautiful. Our reclining-place is shaded, the beams of our houses are cedars, our panelled ceilings are cypresses. The Church praises the beauty of the bridegroom, whom each one praises more with silent affection, and the faithful interpreter of the mysteries proclaims him more by keeping silent. For he who divulges secrets diminishes the beauty of Christ; therefore let no one cast his pearls before swine, lest he cause the precious ornaments to be trampled. Not, then, in the tabernacle of the talkative and the chatterer, since in much speaking sin is committed, but in that of a serious man, who is sparing of speech and not intemperate in talk, and who by sobriety of words avoids the drunkenness of loquacity, does Christ recline his head.

50. And rightly she indicates a shaded reclining-place; because the power of the Most High overshadows those placed in the Church. With this shade David asked to be protected, that the sun might not burn him by day, nor the moon by night (Ps. 120 (121):6). This shade spiritual grace provides for those who flee the scorching heats of this world and the summer heat of the world. Shaded, then, is the reclining-place of Christ and the Church, on whom that eternal rest of God the Father breathes. In this shade, then, let us rest, wearied by the heats of our sins; if lust has scorched any, let the cross of the Lord cool them, on which he reclined himself that he might take up our debts. If guilt has wearied any, let Jesus receive them into his lap and cherish them with a soft embrace; whence I dare to say that the flesh of Christ is the reclining-place of the Church.

51. The beams of our houses are cedars. By the appearance of the cedar the glory of the forefathers who were just is designated. For the just man will flourish like the palm tree, and like the cedar which is in Lebanon he will be multiplied (Ps. 91 (92):13); for as the cedar does not rot, so neither is the glory of the forefathers corrupted by any age.

52. Our panelled ceilings are cypresses. This kind of tree never loses its greenness; in winter and summer alike it nourishes its foliage, and does not change to a different colour. This tree alone the wind never strips of its honour, it alone is never stripped of its old covering, nor clothed with new flower. So too apostolic grace knows no failing, but flourishes in its old age. The soul, then, knows no corruption, she who is vigorous in flourishing members, and always sustains with patient magnanimity the summits of justice and of the other virtues; and therefore she does not flow away nor fail; because nothing in her is cracked or slack, nothing unsteady, nothing slippery, nothing that could pour out of her through a fault of speech. There follows the Church, or the faithful soul; I say the soul, because nothing is so much man as the soul.

Chapter II

1. (Verses 1–2.) I am the flower of the field, and the lily of the valleys, as the lily in the midst of thorns; for the faith of the believing people has gone out into the whole earth, and Christ has set his feet in a broad place; and therefore he beautifully says that he is the flower of the field. Paul too was a flower, who said: We are the good fragrance of Christ to God (2 Cor. 2:15). And truly a flower, who could bring forth things new and old from the treasure of his heart. I am the flower of the field. And well is the Church a flower, which announces the fruit, that is, the Lord Jesus Christ, of whom it was said to Mary: Blessed are you among women, and blessed is the fruit of your womb (Luke 1:42).

2. In another sense also I show you a flower to be plucked, namely him who said: I am the flower of the field, and the lily of the valleys. Many think this comparison more fitting according to the nature of the flower and the habit of the plant set before us; because lilies require no tending of yearly cultivation, nor, as for the yield of the other fruits, so also for the generation of this flower, does labour come round in its cycle, plied by anxious husbandmen; for in whatever droughts of the field, all that is nourished into the flower is quickened by a certain generative power from itself, and within itself by a sap that is ever green; so that when you see that the stalk of the young leaves has withered, the nature of the flower nevertheless stays green; for its greenness is hidden, not lost. But when it has been called forth by the caresses of spring, it resumes the garment of its shoot, the tresses of its flower, or the adornments of the lily.

3. It is a delight also to observe that lilies are produced not in the rough places of the mountains and the untilled parts of the woods, but in the pleasantness of gardens. For there are certain gardens bearing the fruit of diverse virtues, according to what is written: An enclosed garden, etc. (Song 4:12), because where there is integrity, where chastity, where religion, where the faithful silence of secrets, there is the brightness of angels, there are the violets of confessors, the lilies of virgins, the roses of martyrs. Well is Christ a lily, because where the blood of martyrs is, there is Christ, who is the sublime flower, spotless, harmless, in whom no roughness of thorns gives offense, but grace poured around him shone forth. For the thorns belong to roses, and they are the torments of martyrs; the unharmed Godhead, which did not feel torments, has no thorns.

4. As the lily in the midst of thorns. This is a clear indication that virtue is besieged by the briars of spiritual wickedness, so that no one may carry off its fruit except one who approaches with caution. Take wings, then, O virgin, but wings of the spirit: that you may fly over vices, if you desire to touch Christ, Who dwells on high, and looks upon the lowly things (Ps. 112 (113):5). And his appearance is like the cedar of Lebanon, which sets its crown in the clouds and its root in the earth; for its beginning is from heaven, its latter parts are on earth, and they have put forth fruits close to heaven. Search more diligently for so good a flower, to see whether you may find it somewhere in the valley of your breast; for it often breathes upon the lowly. It loves to be born in gardens, in which Susanna found it while she walked: ready rather to die than to be violated.

5. Otherwise: I am the flower of the field, and the lily of the valleys, as the lily in the midst of thorns. Behold another place in which the Lord is wont to dwell, nay, not in one, but in many: I, he says, am the flower of the field; because he frequents the open simplicity of a pure mind. And the lily of the valleys; for Christ is the flower of humility, not of luxury, not of pleasures and wantonness, but the flower of simplicity, the flower of humility. A flower keeps its fragrance even when cut, increases it when crushed, and does not lose it when plucked; so too the Lord Jesus on that gibbet of the cross neither withered when crushed nor vanished when plucked, and, cut by that piercing of the lance, he bloomed more beautiful with the sacred colour of the blood poured out, himself not knowing how to die, and breathing out on the dead the gift of eternal life.

6. (Verse 3.) As the lily in the midst of thorns: does not the flower of good fragrance rise up amid the harshness of labours and the contritions of souls? for God is appeased by a contrite heart. This is the desert, daughters, that leads to the kingdom; this too is the desert that blossoms like the lily, according to what is written: Rejoice, barren one, and exult, desert, and let it blossom like the lily (Isa. 35:1). In this desert, daughters, that good, fruitful tree which bears good fruits begins to spread out the arms of its deeds and to lift up the summit of its divinity, beside which the trees of our wood may sprout; because as the apple tree among the trees of the wood, so is my brother in the midst of the sons; so that the Church, seeing this and now glad at the success of our faith, may say: In his shadow I desired and sat down, and his fruit is sweet in my throat.

7. (Verses 3–4.) Note that often in this book of the Song of Songs Solomon has plainly expressed that threefold wisdom, namely moral, natural and mystical, as he had said in Proverbs: that whoever wished to hear his wisdom should write it down for himself in threefold manner. So the bride says of the bridegroom in the Song of Songs: Behold, you are beautiful, my kinsman, behold, you are beautiful and comely; our resting place is shady, the beams of our houses are cedars, our panelled ceilings cypresses (Song 1:15 and 16). We can take this of moral things; for where do Christ and the Church rest, if not in the works of his people? In short, where there was unchastity, where pride, where iniquity, there the Lord Jesus says: But the Son of Man has nowhere to lay his head (Matt. 8:20). And what are we to take of natural things? In his shadow, she says, I desired and sat down, and his fruit is sweet in my throat; for he who rises above earthly things, and to whom worldly things are dead (since the world is crucified to him, and he to the world), flees and despises all things that are under the sun. Of mystical things too she says: Bring me into the house of wine, set love in order in me. For as the vine embraces its vineyard, so the Lord Jesus, as an eternal vine, embraces his people with certain arms of love.

8. Consider each in turn: in moral things he is a flower, the lily among thorns, as he himself says: I am the flower of the field, and the lily of the valleys (above, v. 1). In moral things, then, he is a flower, as in natural things he is the sun of justice, who, rising and rising again, gives light, and, setting, casts shadow. Take care that he does not set for you, for it is written: Let not the sun go down upon your anger (Eph. 4:26). In mystical things he is love, because Christ is the fullness of the law. And therefore the Church, which loves Christ, is wounded with love.

9. Beautifully, too, the Church is called a lily; for as the lily gleams, so too the works of the saints shine. Most beautifully also is it said: The lily of the valleys; because grace shines out more among the lowly. But this lily is in the midst of thorns, that is, among the Jews and heretics, that is, among the cares of this world, which prick the mind and soul of man. We can also take it otherwise: that as the lily stands out among thorns, so the Church of God shines out above all assemblies.

10. It is worth considering also that this lily is surrounded with brightness, but that what it has within is red, and this too of good fragrance; because the flesh of Christ, surrounded as it were by the moral brightness of the Godhead, had the protection of heavenly grace. In short, in later passages she says: My brother is white and ruddy (Song 5:10): white with divine brightness, ruddy with the appearance of the human colour which he assumed in the sacrament of the incarnation. And rightly that very part which is red smells sweet, because the flesh of Christ is without sin; the faithless, in handling it, defiled their hands; the saints, in venerating it, became fragrant with the fragrance of piety.

11. The Lord Jesus received this fragrance of his fragrant Church, and said: Behold, my nearest one is good. And the Church says to Christ: Behold, you are good, my brother, behold, you are good, like an apple tree among the trees of the wood. A most beautiful comparison! For what could be said more beautiful and more fitting than that she should compare the bridegroom to an apple tree? For a fruit of this kind has so pleasing a fragrance that it surpasses the fragrance of the other fruits. And Christ, fastened to the wood like an apple hanging on the tree, poured out the good fragrance of the world's redemption, which wiped away the heavy stench of sin, and poured forth the ointment of a life-giving drink.

12. As an apple tree, she says, among the trees of the wood, so is my kinsman in the midst of the sons; because, above the prophets and apostles, he soothed the inmost hearts of men with the sweetness of his words. But not only fragrance, but also sweet food is in the apple: therefore Christ is sweet food.

13. In his shadow I desired, and sat down; for she had received the law, she followed it, she ran this way: so, resting in the law, she was resting in the shadow of Christ. A good shadow, which defends us from the sun of iniquity! But who doubts that the law of God is the shadow of Christ? What is the law of the feast day, of the new moon, of the sabbaths, if not a shadow of things to come? What is the day according to the law of Moses: That I may see six days, and again the seventh day, and so on through perpetual ages? What is the month according to the law of Moses, so that, knowing the month, I may know the first new moon of the month, and the things truly holy that are to be offered at the new moons? What is the year according to the law of Moses, consisting of those six days of which he says: Six years shall the Hebrew serve, and in the seventh year he shall be set free (Deut. 15:12); so that six years are found in which one works the land, but in the seventh year he leaves it to the proselytes, and the poor, and the beasts of the earth? What is the seventh year, in which all debts are remitted to the Hebrews?

14. In this shadow rested the faith of the Fathers, the holy devotion of the prophets. Say, then, O religious congregation, or holy soul: In his shadow I desired, and sat down. The jubilee year is a shadow: for who sees its mysteries, so as to know face to face the things which are prescribed by the Law to be fulfilled in the fiftieth year? The feast days according to the Law, from the first month, on the tenth day of the month, up to the fourteenth, and from there up to the twenty-first of the month, are a shadow. What shadow is that festival between the first and the seventh month, in that nameless time? The sanctified freedom on the sabbath is a shadow. A shadow is the feast day of the new moon of the seventh month, and the memorial of trumpets, and the tenth day of the seventh month, the day of propitiation. You see that the monad and the decad are two feast days. Likewise the fifteenth day, the day of Tabernacles for eight days: all these are in shadow. In shadow is the first day proclaimed, and the eighth day proclaimed is in shadow, which are prescribed for the scenopegia according to the Law.

15. Set before me some believers of the circumcision, learned in the Law, men of careful observance, and now enlightened by the knowledge of the Gospel, fattened with spiritual grace: in them the Church, which sees Christ, which receives the bridegroom, which is fed with his food, says: In his shadow I desired, and sat down, and his fruit is sweet in my throat. What is his sweet fruit, if not the preaching of the Lord's passion? as he himself says: Behold the inheritance of the Lord, sons, the reward, the fruit of the womb (Ps. 126 (127):3). For what fruit can be sweeter in our throat than the remission of sins?

16. And his fruit is sweet to my palate. It is spiritual manna, that is, the rain of spiritual wisdom, which is poured from heaven upon the gifted and upon those who seek, and bedews the minds of the pious, and sweetens their throats: whoever, then, has understood the infusion of divine wisdom delights in it, and seeks no other food, nor lives by bread alone, but by every word of God. He who is more curious asks what that is which is sweeter than honey: the minister of God answers him: This is the bread which the Lord has given you to eat. Hear what this bread is: The word, he says, which God has ordained. This ordinance of God, then, this nourishment, nourishes the soul of the wise, and enlightens and sweetens it, shining with the flash of truth, and soothing it as with a honeycomb, so with the sweetness of diverse virtues and with the speech of wisdom. Honeycombs indeed of honey are good words, as it is written in Proverbs. Therefore, when she has tasted the fruit of sweetness, she hastens impatiently to more perfect things, saying:

17. (Verses 4–7.) Bring me into the house of wine, set love in order in me, strengthen me with ointments, pack me round with apples; for I am wounded by love. His left hand is under my head, and his right hand will embrace me. I have adjured you, daughters of Jerusalem, by the powers and the strengths of the field, if you rouse and rouse again love, until it please. Rightly she seeks him, rightly she longs for him; because our commander has arranged all things well on this course and journey. First of all he thought that this tabernacle should be strengthened on the foundation of faith. Then, if any dwelling of ours is harsh and arid and steep, this Commander nevertheless sets in order what is disturbed, waters what is arid, makes fruitful what is desert. If there is any bitterness, any temptation, any weakness, our leader both tempers what is bitter and soothes what is anxious; he loosens what is hard and strengthens what is feeble.

18. Wherever an earthly king wishes to rest his army, he chooses not an ignoble village, not one lacking supplies, not sandy places bare of growing things, but a city noble for its buildings, well stocked and rich in provisions, or a pleasant field, or one green with pastures, or wooded and level places suited for encampments. If, then, the kings of the earth know how to provide comforts for those who follow them, how much more does God, who is good, know how to arrange what will profit those who love him? And first, if an unknown road must be taken, guides of the roads are chosen to go before the column; but these commanders think this an affront to themselves: God, however, went before when the Hebrews made their journey, and indeed spoke to them in the pillar of cloud. And that you may know that he went before: God, it says, went before them: by day in the pillar of cloud, showing them the way; but by night in the pillar of fire, and the pillar of cloud did not fail by day (Exod. 13:21 and 22). The Church, following this pillar which goes before us, which does not fail, lest she herself should fail, was refreshed by its shadow; and therefore she says: In his shadow I desired, and sat down, and his fruit is sweet in my throat (above, v. 3); because she was fed by the Lord, and led into a place of pasture and to the water of refreshment.

19. Bring me into the house of wine. From the provisions prepared, she seeks to go forward to another dwelling, in which she may receive the grace of the mysteries and the sweetness of gladness. From here too, advancing on the true road, she says: Set love in order in me. A good camp, where there is the fullness of love. Love cannot be without faith; for the Church has as it were three sureties, hope, faith, love: when hope has gone before and faith has been founded, love is set in order, and the Church is joined in union.

20. Strengthen me, she says, with ointments, pack me round with apples. You have other dwellings, to which the Church comes on in turn with delight. These dwellings are those of the cross of Christ and of his burial, in which the Church was wounded, but with the wound of love; for it is a wound which Christ received, but an ointment which he poured out. It is an apple which hung; this apple the Church tasted, and said: And his fruit is sweet in my throat. And that you may know that the Lord is an apple, you have read above: As an apple tree among the trees of the wood, so is my kinsman. We too confess a wound when we preach Christ crucified, but we are a good fragrance to God; because the cross of Christ is to the Jews a stumbling block, to the Greeks foolishness, but to us the power and wisdom of God. With this wound the Church is wounded when she preaches the death of her Savior, but this wound is one of love. In short, he who does not believe denies; he who loves confesses. The Manichaean denies, the Christian confesses; and therefore it is written: More profitable are the wounds of a friend than the willing kisses of an enemy (Prov. 27:6). Beautifully, then, does the Church say: For I am wounded by love.

21. Let us bare our limbs to the good wound, let us bare them to the chosen arrow: this arrow is Christ, who says: He has set me as a chosen arrow (Isa. 49:2); it is good, then, to be wounded by this arrow. This advance belongs to no middling dwelling; not all can say that they have been wounded by love. The apostles said it, when they were stoned for Christ and preached Christ; Paul said it, when he was beaten thrice with rods, and day and night argued before the Gentiles that Christ was to be adored; the martyrs say it, who are wounded for Christ; and because they were found worthy to be wounded for his name, they love the more. Coming, then, to this dwelling, to this advance, that she should offer her sons for Christ, that she should receive the wounds of love, the Church, as though finding good nourishment of faith, herself tasted the fruit of piety, and began to exhort the others, saying: Taste and see that the Lord is sweet. And the Church went on to say:

22. His left hand is under my head, and his right hand will embrace me. These too are dwellings of the Church as she walks on the royal road; For there are many dwellings with my Father (John 14:2). The Lord Jesus testifies: And behold, I come, and I will fetch you (ibid.). A good dwelling is that of wisdom, and whether at her left hand or at her right, a good dwelling; because it is the royal road. And so the wise messengers say: We will go by the royal road, we will not turn aside to the right or to the left, until we pass your borders (Num. 20:17). This the messengers sent by Moses said to the king of Edom, that is, the earthly one; because all earthly things, whether on the right or on the left, are evil. Folly is an evil dwelling, intemperance an evil dwelling; and therefore the Hebrew passes through these dwellings: he does not turn aside to them, but passes through, that he may come to the left hand of wisdom and the right, and abide in them, where are the riches of simplicity, where glory, where length of life. Length indeed of life is in her right hand, as Solomon says: But in her left hand riches and glory (Prov. 3:16): in the right hand is life, in the left rest. Would that I might rest upon her left hand, and not seek pillows! Woe indeed to those who sew pillows, Ezekiel says (Ezek. 13:18). These dwellings the good leader prepares for his Church, and through the ways of wisdom he directs her journey. In short, the wise man praises her ways: Her ways are good ways, and all her paths are in peace (Prov. 3:17).

23. His left hand is under my head, and his right hand will embrace me. The bride says this of Christ, the soul says it of the Word of God: but Christ is one and the same, the Word of God and wisdom. Blessed, then, is the soul which wisdom embraces. Great is the hand of wisdom, great her right hand; it embraces the whole soul; for she is wholly protected who has been betrothed to the Word of God. For the fullness of wisdom is to fear God: the soul, then, that fears God protects herself with full defense. Wisdom puts her left hand under her neck, and her right hand about her in embrace. Each of her arms indeed is stretched out for useful things; yet each hand of wisdom has its own properties: In the right hand is length of life, but in the left riches and glory. Her hand is indeed endowed with good gifts on either side; yet they differ in their gift in this, that they embrace both present and future times; so that the left is the rewarder of present things, the right of things to come.

24. We can recognize this also from the prophecy of the holy patriarch Israel; for when he had placed his right hand upon Ephraim, and his left upon Manasseh, and Joseph, in consideration of age, wished to change them, so as to place his father's right hand upon the head of Manasseh, the elder son, he would not, and said: I know, son, and this one too shall become a people, and this one shall be exalted, but his younger brother shall be greater than he (Gen. 48:19). In what greater? Surely in this, that he was preferred in the blessing, as Ephraim's descendants say: May God make you as he made Ephraim and Manasseh (ibid. 20): or because his seed is the multitude of the Gentiles, which in this world has chosen labour, believing in the Lord Jesus, that it may have the consolation of things to come. But the seed of Manasseh is the people of forgetfulness, which, having forgotten its own author, is exalted for a time in this world, but hereafter will pay grave penalties; because it has denied its God and Lord.

25. We have said this in order to prove that the things which are to come are the better. In short, the left hand of wisdom is under the bride's head, but the right is above, which embraces the whole bride. So the former is as it were the support of present rest, on which the soul leans and rests. She has where to lay her head, because the left hand of wisdom is riches and glory. For this time these things soothe, and are in part a comfort, and therefore: The Son of Man had nowhere to lay his head (Matt. 8:20); because though he was rich, he became poor (2 Cor. 8:9). Nor did he seek any glory of this world; because he had come to help not in part, but the whole human race, saying: You are angry with me because I made a whole man well on the sabbath (John 7:23); that is, whole: not to enrich him by a bestowal of means, not to heap him with the insignia of honours, not with the increase of worldly glory; for these do not have the fullness of blessedness and grace: but whole, that is, so that it might embrace length of eternal life. For the life at the right hand of wisdom is not like common life, but is length of life, so that he who receives life from wisdom obtains not a scant life, but perpetuity and the length of eternity.

26. A good garment is eternity of life. So the bride, supported by pomegranates, that is, by diverse and innumerable fruits, and above all by the fragrances of faith, grace, wisdom and glory, and by the riches of eternal life which are about the left and the right hand of the bridegroom, rouses Christ through holy souls with the applause of congregations giving praise, saying:

27. I have adjured you, daughters of Jerusalem, by the powers and the strengths of the field, if you rouse and rouse again love until it please. The field is that which the Lord has blessed, not this earthly one, either bristling with woods, or broken by torrents, or useless for vines, or stony with barren gravel, or gaping and dry with drought, or soaked with blood, or untilled with briars and thorns, but that field of which the Lord says: And the beauty of the field is with me (Ps. 49 (50):11). In this field is found that grape which, when pressed, shed blood and washed the world: in this field is that fig tree under which the saints will rest, refreshed by the sweetness of spiritual grace: in this field is that fruitful olive, flowing with the ointment of the Lord's peace: in this field bloom the pomegranates, which cover very many fruits with one defense of faith, and cherish them with a kind of embrace of love.

28. Since, then, wisdom and honourable brightness are thus perfect, if they have love (for the fullness of the law (Rom. 13:10) is love), she wishes love to be roused and roused again: roused in the Old Testament, roused again in the New. Love is God, as we read; love is Christ (1 John 4:8). He is roused as a lion, and a lion's whelp; that he may come up from the shoot of Judah (Gen. 49:9): he is roused again as one sleeping, as one lying down; because not by a human majesty, but by his own and the Father's, he was raised from the dead. Hence Scripture says: Who shall rouse him? For neither an angel nor a Power could rouse him by a majesty not his own, since he himself roused others. So when she says here: If you rouse and rouse again love, until it please, she speaks of those who can fittingly preach his resurrection, so as to kindle in their hearers the ardour of faith and devotion: or Christ is roused in those who first approach, roused again in those who, after they have approached, have fallen asleep. Christ, then, sleeps for the negligent, and is roused in the saints.

29. So she rouses and rouses again this love, until she receives his voice and summons his presence: for when sought he not only comes, but even comes leaping. But the bridegroom was already present, to prove that which he says while we are still speaking: Here I am. He is present, because he was roused by the daughters of Judah, as though roused again by the daughters of Jerusalem. The Church hears the sound of his voice and says:

30. (Verse 8.) The voice of my kinsman: behold, he comes here, leaping upon the mountains, leaping across the hills. This book is like a band of soldiers, having many noble persons, like a triumphal band, and signifying many actions, which we can understand rather than hold as expressed. For as though she had heard the bridegroom coming and talking with some who were travelling together with him, the bride says: The voice of my kinsman. While she is speaking with the daughters of Jerusalem, and asking them to rouse and rouse again the bridegroom, suddenly perceiving, as it were, the sound of a voice sent from afar, she says: The voice of my kinsman, announcing him whom she was earlier asking to have announced to her. Him whom she desired to be roused by others, believing him now, roused again by her own prayer, to be coming from the Father, she says gladly: The voice of my kinsman. Well did she add, my kinsman; so that not other women, but she alone, his kinswoman, might claim his coming for herself.

31. Behold, she says, he comes: I am still seeking him, and he is already coming; I am still canvassing for votes that he may come, and he is already near: I desire love to be roused for me, I think myself wounded by love, and love itself hastens the more to me: I said, Come; he leaps and leaps across: I ask him to come with grace, he works increases of graces; and as he comes, he brings with him increments of grace, and gains by coming, because he himself too is eager to please his beloved. He leaps upon the heights, to ascend to the bride; for the bride's chamber is the judgment seat of Christ. He leaps upon Adam, leaps across the Synagogue; he leaps upon the Gentiles, leaps across the Jews.

32. Let us see him leaping. He leaps from heaven into the Virgin, from the womb into the manger, from the Jordan to the cross, from the cross into the tomb, into heaven from the sepulchre. Prove to me, David, that he leaps, prove that he runs; for you said: He has exulted as a giant to run the way, his going forth is from the highest heaven; and his meeting reaches to its highest point, nor is there anyone who hides himself from his heat (Ps. 18 (19):6 and 7). And now he leaps, and now he runs, from the heart of the Father upon his saints, from the East upon the West, from the North upon the South. He it is who ascends above the setting sun, he above the heavens of heavens to the East, he ascends upon the mountains, he upon the hills.

33. Would that I, wretched as I am, might say, that my soul might say: Behold, he comes, and comes not upon earthly things, not upon the valleys, but comes: Leaping upon the mountains; for God is the God of the mountains, not of the valleys: where, then, he leaps, he leaps upon the mountains. If you are a mountain, he leaps upon you; he leaps upon Isaiah, he leaps upon Jeremiah, he leaps upon Peter, John, James. Mountains are round about him (Ps. 124 (125):2): if you cannot be a mountain and have not the strength, be at least a hill, that Christ may ascend upon you: and if he leaps across, that his passing may guard you.

34. We have spoken of Christ and the Church; let us speak of the soul and the Word. The soul of the just man is the bride of the Word; if she longs, if she desires, if she prays, and prays constantly, and prays without any disputing, and is wholly intent upon the Word, suddenly she seems to hear the voice of him whom she does not see, and with her inmost sense she recognizes the fragrance of his divinity, as those who believe well often experience. Suddenly the nostrils of the soul are filled with spiritual grace, and she perceives the breath of the presence of him whom she seeks breathing upon her, and she says: Behold, this is he himself whom I seek, he himself whom I desire.

35. Is it not so that, when we are pondering something from the Scriptures and cannot find its explanation, while we are in doubt, while we are seeking, he suddenly seems to us to ascend as it were upon the mountains, that is, upon the loftiest doctrines? Then, appearing to us as it were upon the hills, he enlightens the mind, so as to pour into our understanding what seemed hard to find: so the Word, from absent as it were, becomes present in our hearts. And again, when something is somewhat obscure to us, the Word is as it were withdrawn, and we long for his coming as though he were absent; and again he appears, he shows himself to us as though he were present to us in knowing the things we seek.

36. He leaps, then, often in the heart of each one; he leaps across and goes out and returns: if you follow, if you seek him again, if through the pleasant disputation of faithful teachers you ask that the Word, which had gone out and passed across, be roused again for you. Like her who sought and found, who said: My brother has passed by, my soul went out at his word (Song 5:6). So even if he leaps upon the mountains, follow; even if upon the hills, follow. For on the mountains and on the hills are found the Lord's hunters, who track down those who are caught for life; for so God said through Jeremiah: Behold, I send many fishers and many hunters, and they shall hunt them upon every mountain and upon every hill (Jer. 16:16). There, then, let the people of God be sought and found, in the teaching, grace and discipline of Peter and of Paul; that they may not be in the valley, where weeping is, but on the mountains, from which Christ enlightens each one; and when we read Peter, Christ enlightens; and when we read Paul, Christ enlightens. Paul healed, Christ enlightened; for at the invocation of the name of the Lord Jesus he arose, by whose gift he was healed. Peter raised a dead woman, Christ enlightened; and therefore he says: You give light wonderfully from the everlasting mountains (Ps. 75 (76):5).

37. We, therefore, who cannot be mountains, let us stand on the mountains or on the hills; so that when the Lord has sent his fishers and hunters to hunt those who are upon every mountain or upon every hill, that is, in the precepts of the law and the prophets, and who have their whole way of life in the knowledge of the New and the Old Testament, they may find us ready, and, sent as reapers at the proper time, may gather us like good ears of grain; for if anyone is found outside the mountain or the hill, he cannot be gathered like a good ear by those who, to make mention of another comparison, will be sent to separate the wheat from the chaff. The Lord, then, has ministers skilled in very many works: the same are fishers, the same are also hunters, and reapers. If at the time of harvest, when these are directed to reap, you wait ripe, you will be able to behold him leaping on the mountains, and you will see the Lord Jesus like a young roe or a fawn of the harts upon the mountains of Bethel. For he leaps upon the Church, which is the house of bread, because he strengthens the hearts of the faithful; whence the bride too says of him:

38. (Verse 9.) My kinsman is like a roe or a fawn of the harts upon the mountains of Bethel; for he leaps upon the Church, which is the house of God, because he strengthens the hearts of the faithful. Rightly like a little roe, because the roe feeds on the heights. It is called dorcas from seeing, for the dorcadion is of keener sight. What is more fitting than this for Christ, who saw the Father whom no one has seen: or, if anyone has seen him in Christ, the Son himself revealed him? Rightly like a fawn of the harts: a fawn, as it were a son, in whom the strength of his father's nature has grown, so that hidden things do not escape him, serpents flee from him, poisons do not harm him. In short, the serpent, when he was brought out of his lairs, he who was being driven out of a man, said: Why have you come before the time to torment us? (Matt. 8:29). Let us, then, behold this fawn leaping, so that we may not be able to fear the serpent.

39. Behold, he stands behind our wall, looking in through the windows, peering through the nets. He comes, then, and first he is behind the wall, that he may dissolve the enmities of soul and body, taking away the wall which seemed to bring a hindrance to concord. Then he peers through the windows: what the windows are, hear the Prophet saying: The windows have been opened from heaven (Isa. 24:18). So it signifies the prophets, through whom the Lord looked upon the human race before he himself came down to earth. And today, if any soul seeks him much, she will merit much mercy; since to him who seeks much, much is owed. If, then, any soul seeks him more zealously, she hears his voice from afar: and although she asks of others, she hears his voice before the very ones of whom she asks. She sees him coming to her leaping, that is, hastening and running and leaping across those who, with feeble heart, cannot take in his power: then peering through the riddles of the prophets, by reading them and holding fast to their words; for she sees him peering, but as it were through a window, not as one yet present. She sees him peering out over the nets. What is this? Unless perhaps it is that those nets are nets for us, they are not nets for him, because that soul is still among the things of sense and of the world, which are wont to catch the mind of man and wrap it in a kind of fold of their own.

40. Behold, he stands behind our wall, looking in through the windows, peering through the nets. For the Church, or the soul of the just, had seen him leaping like a fawn upon the mountains, and suddenly, looking at him for the first time behind the wall of her house, peering through the windows, peering out through the nets, she exults and rejoices, because she too is loved by the Bridegroom. He, as though himself also wounded by the beauty of love, though at first he was absent from his beloved, was present when asked for kisses (for he is asked when it is said (Song 1:1): Let him kiss me with the kisses of his mouth); then he did not spurn the prayers and caresses of the bride, who offered him the breasts he loved, and kindly brought her into the inner rooms of the house: then, as though playing with a sportive love, because he wished to test the feelings of the one who loved him, often going out that he might be sought by the Bride, often returning that he might be invited to kisses, he stood behind the wall, peered through the windows, peering out over the nets; so that he was neither wholly absent nor entered as it were wholly, and he himself called the bride to himself; that the exchanges of coming to one another might become more pleasing, and they might kindle love with mutual words.

41. (Verse 10.) Arise, he says, come, my nearest one, my fair one, my dove; and you, if you have a well-founded wall, not that middle wall which separates the members of one house, but one built upon the foundation of the apostles and prophets, so that its structure, fitted together, grows into a temple, and does not divide its inner parts but protects them. If, then, you have within you the building of God, and your windows are always open toward the East, the Word comes, He stands behind your wall (Song 2:9); the eyes indeed of the Lord are upon the just (Ps. 33 (34):16); he peers through your windows (Song 2:9).

42. What are these windows? We read of windows of which Jeremiah says: Death has entered through the windows (Jer. 9:21), through which avarice has entered, lust has entered. Your eye is a window. If you have looked upon a woman to lust after her, death has entered through your eye: if you have looked upon a widow's property, or the treasures of a minor, and have stirred up your desires, Death has entered through the window. As, then, death enters through these, so life enters too: if, looking upon the beauty of a consecrated maiden, you venerate the Lord Jesus, because the age of old age, a spotless life, has come into tender years, and you yourself offer your daughter, that she may be consecrated with the holy veil: if you look upon the property of a minor not as an eager usurper, but watch over it with devout affection as a careful parent: through these windows Christ peers, that he may call the bride.

43. Peering out over the nets: well does it say peering out; because he alone is the one whom the nets of sins have not entangled. We were all within the nets, nay, we are still within the nets; because no one is without sin but Jesus alone, whom, though he knew no sin, the Father made sin for us; for indeed he handed him over to the snares, handed him over to the nets: sending him not in the sin in which all men were, but in the likeness of sinful flesh, that concerning sin he might condemn sin in the flesh. The flesh was sin, in this sense, that it had been condemned by an inherited curse; it was sin as the allurement and handmaid of sin. The Lord Jesus came, and in the flesh subject to sin he waged the warfare of virtue: our members have now become not weapons of lust, but weapons of virtue; for where there were the incentives of lust, there now are the dwellings of chastity. He came, then, to the snares, but willingly: he came to the nets, but secure. All things were full of nets, crammed with snares; hear him who says: In this way in which I walked, the proud hid a snare for me (Ps. 141 (142):4). And in the book of the wisdom of Sirach you are warned to know that you walk in the midst of snares (Sir. 9:20). As many vices, so many nets: as many sins, so many snares.

44. Inherited bonds were already holding you; Jesus came to the snares to release Adam: he came to set free what had perished. We were all held by nets; no one could rescue another, since he could not rescue himself. Such a one, then, was needed, whom the bonds of human generation, liable to sins, would not hold, whom avarice had not caught, guile had not bound. That one was Jesus alone, who, when he had surrounded himself with the bonds of this flesh, was not caught, was not bound: but, bursting and loosing them, he called the Church the more to himself, Peering through the snares, peering out over the nets; that she too might learn not to be held by bonds. In short, he was not far from bonds to this extent, that he even underwent death for us; yet he was not made the slave of death, but was free among the dead; for he was free who had the power to loose death. In short, let him teach you himself who says: Loose this temple, and in three days I will raise it again (John 2:19). Let us hear, then, what he says:

45. (Verses 11–13.) Arise, come, my nearest one; that is, rise from the dead, arise from the bonds by which you were held encircled. Arise, because I have risen again for you: loose the bonds of iniquity, because I have already loosed them for you: come, because the nets are now loosed, the Virgin has given birth, a boy has been born of a virgin; he owes nothing to the inheritance of woman, he is not held as the son of a woman: see the middle wall of partition now loosed, which divided the concord of the inner affections, and stirred up in opposite directions the dissensions of bodily passions. Come, then, secure; I long to see your face, and to hear your voice: come, that you may no longer see me through the nets, but that, face to face, you, the beloved, may possess the countenance of your lover.

46. And here let these things have been run through in the moral sense. But those mystical things, if we can, let us look upon at least in outline. The Bride, devoted to the Lord, was sitting within the house, within the wall of the Law and the prophets, founded by the building-up of spiritual stones, which enclosed and fortified the royal house, full of joy and gladness. She was marvelling at the royal treasures, and was looking at them anxiously, desiring to take to herself the wisdom which might show her these riches. She was in a secret place, but she was seeking an interpreter of that very secret. The Lord Jesus came, leaping upon the mountains. To us he seemed to come late, but he was hastening. In short, he leapt and leapt across, that he might leap across the bodily and stony doctrines of the Jews. He stood behind the wall of the house which was in the Old Testament, peering through the window of the Law and the hollows of the prophets.

47. Not yet were the forecourts of that house open, not yet had the keys of wisdom opened the bars of the gates by which the inner parts of the Law were shut; yet, peering out from the upper part, that is, the spiritual, he calls the Church, that through the Law and the prophets, rising aloft on the summit of the Gospel, she may tread with fearless step on the nets and knots of Jewish interpretation. And therefore she is called Nearest, that she may cleave to Christ and not seek worldly things: therefore Fair, that she may carry the beautiful feet of those who bring good tidings: therefore Dove, that she may seek spiritual things and leave earthly things behind.

48. Behold, he says, the winter has passed, the rain has gone, it has gone its way, the flowers have appeared on the earth, the time of cutting has come. Before the coming of Christ it was winter: Christ came and made summer. Then all things were in want of flowers, bare of virtues: Christ suffered, and all things began to be made fruitful with the shoots of new grace. The rain of flowing luxury has gone, and the clouds that arose from foul crimes are now dispersed by the summer serenity of a pure conscience. Therefore those whose flight is in winter do not escape; because they do not follow the Lord's passion, do not take up their cross and follow Christ.

49. Rain hinders flowers: but now flowers are seen on the earth. Good flowers are the apostles, who have poured out the fragrance of their diverse writings and works. The time of cutting has come, when the ripe grain is stored in the barns; and he who reaps receives wages. The voice of the turtledove has been heard, because it has found a nest for itself; for the Church is the house of chastity. The fig tree, which was ordered to be cut down for its unfruitfulness, has now begun to bear fruit. The vineyard brought over from Egypt is no longer overrun by beasts, its walls torn down and laid low; nor is it unsightly with briars, but now fragrant with flowers.

50. Arise, he says, come, my nearest one, my fair one, my dove, my perfect one: nearest surely by the desire of faith, fair by the beauty of virtue, dove by spiritual grace; for the wings of the dove covered with silver signify that eternal power, and the flight of the dove declared the presence of the Holy Spirit. Christ therefore calls her to himself, that she may come, since she was coming already endowed with spiritual treasures. For behold, he says, the winter has passed, the rain has gone, it has gone its way, the flowers have appeared on the earth. See how the holy Church is invited. The winter, he says, has gone its way, that she may not, being bare, dread the winter: the winter not of the season, but of weakness, which strips the fertile field of the soul of every flower. For there is a winter not of the earthly sun; there is a winter of the mind, when cold slips into the soul, when the warmth of the soul vanishes, when the vigour of the senses is loosened, when excessive moisture overflows and weighs down the mind, when the inner sight grows dim. And therefore the Lord says: See that your flight be not in winter or on the sabbath (Matt. 24:20); for it is good that the day of judgment or of death should come then, when the gentle mildness of the soul is vigorous, when the heavenly mystery shines with serene light, when our heart burns within us. For then Christ is present, as Ammaon and Cleopas testify in the Gospel, saying: Was not our heart burning within us on the way, when he opened the Scriptures to us? (Luke 24:32). And the soul is vigorous when the flower too is seen on the earth. Who is this flower of good fragrance, if not he who said: I am the flower of the field and the lily of the valleys (above, verse 1): of whom it is also written in Isaiah: A rod shall come forth from the root of Jesse, and a rod shall ascend from the root, and a flower shall come forth from the rod (Isa. 11:1). The root surely is the family of the Jews, the rod Mary, the flower of Mary Christ: when he shines in our land, and is fragrant in the field of the soul, or blooms in his Church, we can neither fear the cold nor dread the rain, but can await the day of judgment. And therefore the Church, that she might see this flower, was hastening with all zeal, as she herself testifies in later passages, saying: On my bed by night I sought him whom my soul loves (Song 3:1).

50bis. The fig tree has put forth its green figs. The comparison of this tree is fitting for the Synagogue, because, as this tree, abounding in flowing leaves, disappoints the hope of its owner with an empty expectation of the hoped-for yield, so also in the Synagogue, while its teachers, unfruitful in works, glory only in words, as in abounding leaves, the empty shadow of the Law prevails; and the long-awaited hope of a false yield mocks the prayers of the believing people.

51. There is also something in the nature of the tree by which you may the more believe that the figure of the Synagogue is expressed in the comparison with it. For if you inquire diligently, you will find the habit of this tree's nature distinct from the usage of the other trees; for the others bear the flower before the fruit, and by their flower, as a herald, signify the fruits to come: this alone from the beginning puts forth fruits instead of flowers: in the others the flower is shaken off and the fruits are born; in this, fruits fall that fruits may follow. So those first ones, empty of fruit, come forth in place of the flower: thus, by a kind of premature habit of birth, those which do not know the order of nature cannot keep nature's benefit. For where the bud is wont to thrust itself out from the middle of the bark, there all the tiniest fruits of this kind break forth: of which we read in the Song of Songs: The fig tree has put forth its green figs. And so, while the other shrubs are white in early spring, the fig alone does not know how to grow white with a flower of its own; perhaps because there is no riper use in their fruits. For as others follow, those which are spat out as degenerate, and are weak with a withering root, are stripped off when those are renewed whose sap is more useful. Yet some very few remain and do not fall, on which this good fortune has smiled: that they come forth from between two twigs, where a short tendril breaks out, so that, covered by a twofold protection, as in the lap of their parent nature, they grow on with the nourishment of a fuller sap. These, called forth by the mildness of a gentler air, and grown up over longer seasons, when they have put off the wild temper of their earlier sap, are preferred above the others in appearance and in the grace of ripeness.

52. Look now at the worship and the minds of the Jews, who, as the first fruits of the ill-bearing Synagogue, have fallen as in the likeness of falling fruit, so that the fruits of our race, which will endure beyond the age, might follow. For the first people of the Synagogue, weak as it were with a withering root of works, could not draw in the richness of natural wisdom. And therefore it fell like a useless fruit, so that, as from the same tendrils of the fruitful tree, the new people of the Church might come forth from the richness of the ancient religion. So that which was ceased to be, that this which was not might begin: yet the first from Israel, whom a branch of stronger nature had brought forth, under the shadow of the Law and of the cross, coloured in the bosom of both with a twofold sap, after the example of a ripening green fig, surpassed the others by the grace of most beautiful fruits; to whom it is said: You shall sit upon twelve thrones, judging the twelve tribes of Israel (Matt. 19:28).

53. The vineyards in flower have given their fragrance (above, v. 5). Well is the Church designated by the vineyard; for as the vine is wedded to trees, so the body is joined to the soul, and the soul also to the body: as the vineyard, when it is tied, is raised up, and when cut back is not diminished but increased; so the holy people, when bound, is set free: when humbled, is lifted up: when cut back, is crowned. Moreover, as a tender shoot, cut from an old tree, is grafted into the offspring of another root: so this holy people, the scars of the old shoot smoothed away, grows on, cherished in that new sign of the cross as in the lap of a devoted parent: and the Holy Spirit, poured into this prison-house of the body, as though let down into deep trenches of the earth, washes away with the watering of saving water whatever is foul, and raises the disposition of our members to heavenly discipline.

54. This vineyard the careful husbandman is wont to dig, to water, to stretch out, and, having cleared away the heaps of earthly mass, now to scorch the works of our body with the sun, now to drench them with rain; he is wont to hedge the field about, lest the bud be harmed by briars, lest the shade of the leaves run riot, and the fruitless boasting of words, by overshadowing the virtues, hinder the ripening of our natural disposition.

55. (Verse 14.) And come, he says, my dove, in the shelter of the rock, beside the outwork; that is, come beside the Gospel: the bulwarks of your faith are the deeds of Christ: the supports of your wall are the words of the Lord: the passion of the Lord's body is your strength.

56. Show me your face, and make your voice known; for your voice is sweet, and your face is comely. Your voice is sweet; for with the mouth confession is made unto salvation: and your face is comely; because she who does not blush for her author is not put to shame by her redeemer. She shows her face, then, bearing before her the seal of the cross: and she makes her voice known, taking up the authority of preaching. For in the covering of the body of Christ, by which there was redemption from sin, she has found the defense of spiritual grace; so that she may both perceive and speak things that bring salvation to her. Sweet, then, is the voice which spoke in the divine testimonies: Comely the face, which was not put to shame in the sight of kings.

57. Arise, he says, come, my nearest one; that is, arise from the delights of the world, arise from earthly things, and come to me, you who still labour and are burdened, who are anxious about the things that are of the world: come above the world, come to me, for I have overcome the world: come near me, now beautiful with the beauty of eternal life, now a dove, that is, gentle and mild, now wholly full of spiritual grace. Rightly, then, she ought no longer to fear the nets, since he calls the soul to himself who could not be caught by the temptations and nets of the world. For since we men walk in the midst of snares, through the appetite for food we are liable at once to nets and snares. He, placed in the body, did not fear the nets, but peered out over the nets, that is, over the temptations of the world and the passions of the body; nay, he made others too rise up over them.

58. Arise, come, my nearest one, do not fear the nets; the winter has now passed. The Pasch has come, pardon has come, the remission of sins has come, temptation has ceased, the rain has gone, the storm has gone and the shaking. Before the coming of Christ it was winter; after the coming of Christ there are flowers; hence he says: The flowers have appeared on the earth. Where before there were thorns, there now are flowers; The time, he says, of cutting has come. Where before there was desert, there is harvest. The voice of the turtledove has been heard in our land: well did the prophet add our, as if marvelling that where before there was unchastity, there is chastity. The fig tree has put forth its green figs. That which, as unfruitful, was ordered to be cut down has now begun to bear fruit. But why do you hesitate because he said green figs? It shakes off the earlier ones, that it may bring forth later and better ones: as the fruit of the Synagogue is cast away, while that of the Church is renewed.

59. And although there is full tranquillity, and the mysteries have grown up, yet again he says: Arise, secure in the shelter of the rock, that is, safe in the protection of my passion and the defense of faith. For they sucked honey from the rock and oil from the firm rock. Clothed with this covering, the souls of the pious are no longer naked, and this is their outwork. And therefore he says to this soul too: And come, you my dove, in the shelter of the rock beside the outwork: show me your face, and make your voice known. He exhorts her to confidence, that she may not blush for the cross of Christ, nor for its seal. He exhorts her to confession; he wishes all snares to be removed, that the good fragrance of faith may breathe, that the day may shine, that the shadow of hostile night may do no harm; since he who is beside Christ says: The night has gone before, but the day has drawn near (Rom. 13:12). There is both the shadow of worldly things, which passes away, and the day of heavenly things, Christ, who shines for his saints.

60. (Verse 15.) Catch us the little foxes that destroy the vineyards, that our vineyards may flower. Scripture calls him a man who is in the image and likeness of God: but the sinner it is wont to call not a man, but either a serpent, or a horse neighing after females, or a little fox, or a beast of burden. Of one of these the Lord says: Go, tell that fox (Luke 13:32), that is, of Herod. And elsewhere, when he perceived that he was being questioned deceitfully, he said: Foxes have holes, and the birds of the air nests, where they may rest (Matt. 8:20); because they deceitfully linger in certain lairs of earthly passions.

61. Hence also Samson tied foxes together in pairs, bound torches to their tails, and let them loose through the harvests of the Philistines (Judg. 15): signifying by this figure that wicked and deceitful men, and above all heretics, have a free tongue for barking, but hampered outcomes, or quarrelsome beginnings, and the end of their deceit consigned to burning. And therefore he let loose three hundred foxes too, because the faithless desire to commend themselves by the preaching of the cross, but cannot hold its mystery; who, with this preaching contrived, false and feigned, try rather to burn the fruits of others: whereas the true cross of the Lord surely does not burn up the merits of others, but makes them fruitful.

62. And rightly it is written: Catch us the little foxes that destroy the vineyards, that our vineyards may flower. By this it is shown that either the Lord Jesus or the Church commands that the wiles of the deceitful are to be driven out of their vineyards, lest they harm the tiny vineyards, since they cannot harm vines already grown. For the heretic can tempt one who is imperfect; he cannot supplant one who is perfect.

63. (Verse 16.) My brother is mine, and I am his. This is the voice of a virgin; for to keep the body spotless from union with a man, and, by the guarding of integrity, to carry the palm of chastity in a body of clay up to the way of life of the angels, is a private benefit and a public praise. How rare on earth is he who can say this, how far from vices, how set apart from every stain of sin, who has nothing in common with the world, saying: My beloved is mine and I am his, he will dwell in the midst of my breasts. That is, let him claim for himself nothing of this world, who has no possession of bodily desires, whom lust does not inflame, avarice does not goad, wantonness does not make effeminate, luxury does not discolour, ambition does not lay low, envy does not waste away, no care of worldly business makes anxious: a true minister of the altar, born for God, not for himself; for Levi, as the interpretation has it, signifies: He himself taken up for me; it signifies: And he is mine; it signifies also simply: Taken up; it signifies: And taken up for me.

64. He is the same, both Levi to me and to God. Just as the same is both priest to me and to God. And he is both my advocate and the Lord's intercessor: offering sacrifice for me, and the same offering himself to the Lord. In short, elsewhere too Levi is said, by interpretation, to mean For me (Pro me); for indeed, if Levi is named by me, Levi is for me: if he offers for me, he is for me: if he intercedes for me, he is for me: but if he is called by the Lord, he is called He himself to me (Ipse mihi): that is, not a tributary to another, not a payer of tithes, not bountiful out of his possession. He himself to me, that is, as one abundant in place of all his goods. From him I seek no tithes, no fruits, no gifts, no offerings; he himself is to me in place of an offering, he himself is in place of tribute. Not bountiful to me with his possession, but he himself is my possession, he himself my fruit, he himself my understanding. Taken up by me, or taken up for me: this cannot be without divine grace. For as a possession cannot be mine unless I buy it, so there cannot be a Levi unless he has been taken up by the Lord; for when he has been taken up, he is rightly called, He is mine. In short, when Moses had been told to divide the settlements for the Jewish people by their several tribes, and to distribute a portion to each tribe, God excepted the tribe of Levi, saying to the sons of Levi: He shall have no portion, nor lot, in the midst of his brothers; for the Lord God is their part (Deut. 10:9): and elsewhere: I the Lord am their portion (Num. 18:20).

65. An earthly allotment is denied to these, so that, while they claim no worldly portion for themselves, they may become a heavenly possession: or that they may know how to possess this alone, that is, the service of faith and devotion, being much richer than those who spread out the wide extents of their possessions. For however far they extend their boundaries, the land gives out, and the sea encloses their mad desires, and they pay tributes greater than their fruits. But this man, possessing nothing, serves as a soldier for none but God; his portion is above the earth, it does not give out with the earth, it is not enclosed by the sea. He whose portion is God is the possessor of all nature. In place of fields, then, he is enough for himself, having a good fruit which can never perish. In place of houses he is enough for himself, that he may be the dwelling of the Lord and the temple of God, than which nothing can be more precious. For what is more precious than God? Or what does this man lack who can say: May it not befall me to glory except in the cross of our Lord Jesus Christ, through whom the world is crucified to me, and I to the world (Gal. 6:14)?

66. In him the prince of this world can claim no partnership for himself, since he finds nothing of his own in him. And therefore the Lord, who came to teach that God might become our portion, says: For the prince of this world comes, and finds nothing in me (John 14:30). And rightly, wishing us to be his imitators, he says: Do not possess gold, nor silver, nor money (Matt. 10:9). Hence Peter, showing that his portion is in God, not in the world, says: Silver and gold I have not, but what I have, this I give you: in the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth, rise and walk (Acts 3:6); that is, this is my portion, my portion is Christ: In the name, then, of Christ Jesus rise and walk (ibid.). That is, in my portion I am rich, in my portion I am mighty. Rightly I presume such fruits of this portion, that salvation and life are bestowed; for this is the patrimony of that portion which I have chosen for myself: My beloved is mine, and I am his.

67. Who feeds among the lilies. The lily signifies chastity. So the good shepherd knows where to feed his flock, what pastures are good for his sheep. What are our pastures, that is, those of the faithful, if not Christ? In whose pastures the Prophet rejoiced to be placed, saying: In a place of pasture, there he has set me (Ps. 22 (23):2); for there he feeds us and refreshes us. Good pastures are the divine sacraments. There you pluck a new flower, which has given the good fragrance of the resurrection: you pluck the lily, in which is the splendour of eternity: you pluck the rose, that is, the blood of the Lord's body. Good pastures also are the books of the heavenly Scriptures, in which we are fed by daily reading, in which we are restored and refreshed, when we taste what is written, or, having sipped it with the tip of the mouth, chew it over again and again. With these pastures the flock of the Lord is fattened. Good pastures of Christ also are the mountains of the valleys; for in these too Christ feeds like a young roe or a fawn of the harts, that is, in the splendours of the saints.

68. (Verse 17.) Until the day breathes and the shadows decline. Zacchaeus climbed into a sycamore tree to see Christ: for us too Jesus spread out his hands, to overshadow the whole world. How are we not in shadow, who are protected by the veil of his cross? How are we not in shadow, whom the Crucified defends from the malice of the world and the heat of the body? Or do we not know that the Word of God, coming into this world, did not come as the Word, such as he was in the beginning, such as he was with God: but emptied himself, taking the form of a servant (Phil. 2:7)? He came in a light cloud, and, being the power of the Most High, he overshadowed Mary, that he might transfigure the body of our lowliness, to be made conformable to the body of his glory. As he, then, changed his form when he was born of the Virgin, so the words of God too seem to us to be transfigured when they are read in the Gospel, when their appearance is seen in the Scriptures as through a mirror; because the whole truth cannot be grasped here, but when that which is perfect has come, they shine no longer through discussion, nor through appearance, as transfigured, but in truth whole and clear.

69. Let him, then, bend the soul to the yoke of the Word, and guide her with his reins, so that by the will of God she may be drawn away from vices and catch the fragrance of eternal life; for this life is not perfect, but this life is in shadow. Therefore, that the man Christ Jesus might be born of the Virgin, the power of the Most High overshadowed the future mother; because he came down in shadow, beginning from shadow to work the salvation of man, and about to complete it in the brightness of the eternal sun. This life, then, is a shadow. Hasten to the sun, that it may defend you from the cold of this shadow, and pour summer warmth upon you. And therefore he counsels us to pray that our flight be not in winter or on the sabbath, not signifying a season and a day, but forbidding us to be cold in merits, and to be fasting from good works.

Chapter III

1. (Verse 1.) On my bed by night I sought him whom my soul loved: I sought him and did not find him. I will rise, then, and seek in the city, in the marketplace, in the streets, him whom my soul loved. This soul had already received good pledges of love; but because we must always be watchful, always intent, and because the Word of God leaps away like a little roe, or like a fawn of the harts, let the soul always keep watch, and reach out toward him whom she seeks and whom she desires to hold. Therefore, lamenting him as one who has slipped away from her: On my bed, she says, in the nights I sought him whom my soul loved. Let him who seeks well seek on his bed, seek in the nights: let neither the day nor the nights be a holiday: let no time be free from the duty of piety; even if he has not found at first, let him persevere in seeking. And therefore she says:

2. (Verse 2.) I will rise, then, and seek in the city, in the marketplace, in the streets. And perhaps she has not yet found him for this reason, that she sought in the marketplace, where there are lawsuits; in the streets, where there are markets of goods for sale; for Christ is not bought with money. Let holy devotion allow no time empty of proclaiming to pass by. If those who study worldly learning indulge very little in sleep, how much more ought those who desire to know God not to be hindered by the sleep of the body, except so far as suffices for nature? David each night washed his couch with tears; he rose even in the middle of the night to confess to the Lord: and do you think the whole night should be given over to sleep? Then above all must you pray to the Lord, then must protection be asked for, sin guarded against, when it seems to have secrecy. Then most of all, when darkness is round about me and walls cover me, must one consider that God beholds all hidden things. Do not say, then, surrounded by darkness: Who sees me? And whom do I fear, hedged about and shut in by walls? For The face of the Lord is upon those who do evil (Ps. 33 (34):17). Then, if you do not see a witness, do you not see yourself? Do you not fear the testimony of your own conscience? Do you not know that that gloom of night is not a covering of sin but an incitement to it? When bodies are heated with sleep and food, then the vigor of the mind too is slackened by drowsiness, dissolved by sleep. Then the lust of impure intercourse creeps in, then the heart is troubled by the squalor of uncleanness, chastity is not seen, purity is not considered, the glory of modesty is not called to mind.

3. It was night when Judas betrayed, when Peter denied. At that time, therefore, above all, the justifications of God must be gone over again in the mind, the commandments of exhortation read again: let those precepts on chastity not be absent, so that the mind, occupied with them, may quench the flaming of lust, the burning of the flesh. Hold this in your mind: I will wash my couch every night (Ps. 6:7). For who that is given over to debauchery, wrapped up in shameful deeds, washes his couch every night? He who commits what ought to be wept over does not know how to weep; and though he himself is to be lamented, he has no tears for his own punishment. But he who chastises his body, and is a careful governor of himself, and, groaning and grieving over the offence of his former fall, seeks how he may wash it away with the tears of penitence, he washes his couch every night. Let us not, then, sleep whole nights through, but devote the greatest part of them to reading and to prayers. Hear the voice of the Church, seeking Christ even in the nights: On my bed, she says, in the nights I sought him whom my soul loved.

4. Take it according to the letter: that she sought in the nights by praying, by entreating, even by lamenting with tears. She sought in the nights, because He made darkness his hiding place (Ps. 17 (18):12), that we might learn to seek him more carefully. The Church, then, gathered from the Gentiles, sought in the prophets, and therefore she believed. Then, the Evangelists and Paul set down testimonies to the Lord's coming from the prophets; whence it is written: For night to night shows knowledge (Ps. 18 (19):3). She sought in the nights, in persecutions and adversities, in tribulations and hard sorrows. It is night for all who have no perfect security; whence the Lord says: The night will come when no one can work. While I am in this world, I am the light of this world (John 9:4). Let us not, then, work in darkness; for if our works shine, we do not work in darkness, but in the light.

5. It is day for those with whom Christ is present, it is night for those from whom he withholds himself. It is no great thing for you if you give thanks to the Lord at the time when you are in prosperity and good fortune; but it is if you cleave to Christ at the time when the persecutor harasses you, when some storm troubles you. Have you lost a son? In that grief, in that night, in that desolation, remember the Lord your God, lest, as though you had not been heard, you be ungrateful and transgress in your affliction. Have you been driven into exile? Remember the Lord your God, lest you prefer to God your love for the fatherland that is forbidden you. Oppressed by the power of some rich man, have you lost your own means, do you lack a living? Remember the Lord your God, lest the night of want lead you away from the affection of devotion. For this is the commandment of the Law, that you seek more in the night, when you are more heard by the Lord, and can say: In tribulation I called upon the Lord, and the Lord heard me in a wide place (Ps. 117 (118):5).

6. But neither is it enough for grace to seek perfunctorily; you must persist and be intent upon the task. Then, this one, whether the Church or the soul, who sought on her bed, sought in the nights, did not find at first; because perhaps she sought on her bed. But after she rose and went into the city (see whether it is not into that city to which the Lord, when he was about to celebrate the Passover, sent his disciples, saying (Matt. 26:18): Go into the city to a certain man), after she sought, as I said, in the marketplace, where the oil is sold which those awaiting the Bridegroom were accustomed to buy; where there is right, where there are laws. For if the law is spiritual, the marketplace too is surely spiritual, where those skilled in the eternal law dispute. This marketplace is not tumultuous with lawsuits, but glorious with the judgment seats of Christ. Where did she seek, I say? In the marketplace, and afterwards she sought in the streets, from which those were gathered who were to come to the dinner of the householder of the Gospel, those who, when called, did not think they should excuse themselves from such a feast. After she had sought, I say, in the marketplace and the streets, she fell in with those who go about the city, and then at last she was able to find what she sought; perhaps because, placed in tribulation and fear, she finds grace the more. Then she says afterwards:

7. (Verse 3.) Those who go about the city found me; they took my cloak from me. She had, then, a struggle: but for what reason they did not take her cloak from her here, I am at a loss to discover; unless perhaps because there she said she had asked: Have you seen him whom my soul loved? She who spoke of Christ was not stripped of her cloak, and found him whom she sought. Learn how Christ is to be sought: surely by those who do not seek perfunctorily, but hold him fast by force, like this one who says: I held him and will not let him go: she found him with faith, she bound him fast by meditation.

8. If we take them as good watchmen, they are surely angels. But he who passed by the angels found the Word; and therefore there was no great interval when he passed from them and found Jesus. How then did they take her cloak below, unless perhaps because, in the course of an offence against the faith, the Church, while she is stripped, is loved the more by the Lord, putting off the old man that she may put on the new, whom sins do not cover, but the secret places of the mind make bright; or because, when she was about to come to Christ, she laid aside the garment of worldly wisdom? And he was not stripped of his cloak after he had begun to be more perfect.

9. Let us, then, be mindful of the heavenly justifications; so that while we sing them with the secret voice of the mind, we may be mindful in the night of the name of the Lord, and may say, as it is written: This has come to me, because I have sought out your justifications (Ps. 118 (119):56); that is, this remembrance has come to me, that I should be mindful even in the night of your name, not lulled by drunkenness, not loosened into sleep by feasting, not occupied with worldly cares, lest forgetfulness of your worship should creep upon me: but chastising my limbs by daily meditation, and exercising the intent of my mind; so that by constancy this course may become customary for us, so that we may venerate the Lord Jesus even in the nights with all our affection.

10. In my chamber, in the nights I sought him whom my soul loved. We can also understand it thus. He seeks Christ on his bed who seeks him with tranquillity, with peace. He seeks him in the nights, since he spoke in parables; He made indeed darkness his hiding place, and night to night shows knowledge (Ps. 17 (18):12). Next, since the things we say in our heart ought to pierce us with compunction on our beds.

11. But yet not even so does she find him, and therefore she says: I will rise, that is, I will raise and lift up my intent, that I may seek tirelessly, seek carefully: I will enter into the city. There is also a soul that says: I am a fortified city, I am a besieged city (Isa. 27:3). There is a city fortified through Christ; there is that city, Jerusalem in heaven, in which interpreters of the divine law and men skilled in the disciplines abound, through whom the Word of God is sought. I will seek, she says, in the marketplace of that city, in that marketplace where the jurists treat of rights, where the oil is sold which the virgins of the Gospel buy, that their torches may always shine and the smoke of iniquity may not put them out. I will seek, she says, in the streets in which the waters overflow that burst forth from those fountains from which Solomon says we must drink.

12. They found me, she says, the watchmen who go about the city. While she seeks Christ, then, she finds the watchmen who are in ministry; of them she asks: Have you seen him whom my soul loves? But the soul that seeks God passes even the watchmen. For there are mysteries which even the angels long to see; whence Peter says: They have been announced, he says, to you through those to whom they preached the gospel, by the Holy Spirit sent from heaven, into whom the angels long to look (1 Pet. 1:12).

13. (Verse 4.) When I had passed from them but a little, I found him, I held him, and did not let him go. He, then, who has passed the watchmen finds the Word. John passed them, who found the Word with the Father. There are also many others who seek Christ in leisure and do not find him; and they seek him in persecutions and find him. And therefore, as if after temptations, because he is present in the dangers of his faithful: When I had passed from them but a little, she says, I found him, and held him, and did not let him go. For everyone who seeks finds; and he who has found ought to cleave, so that he may not be able to lose.

14. And since through the Gospel we see on earth the heavenly mysteries prefigured, let us come to the Magdalene, let us come to the other Mary. Let us consider how they sought Christ in the nights on the bed of his body, in which he lay dead, when the angel said to them: You seek Jesus who was crucified: he is not here, for he has risen: why then do you seek the living with the dead (Matt. 28:5 ff.)? Why do you seek in the tomb him who is already in heaven? Why do you seek in the bonds of burial him who loosens the bonds of all? The tomb is not his seat, but heaven; therefore one of them said: I sought him, and did not find him (Song 3:1). Yet while they are going to bring word to the apostles, Jesus, pitying them as they sought, meets them, saying: Hail. And they came near and held his feet and worshipped him.

15. Jesus, then, is held, and he delights to be held so, because he is held by faith. Then, that woman too delighted him who touched him and was healed of the flow of blood, of whom he said: Someone has touched me; for I perceive that power has gone out from me (Luke 8:46). Touch, then, and hold him by faith, and clasp his feet faithfully, that power may go out from him to heal your soul. And if he says: Do not touch me, hold him. For I have not yet ascended to my Father, he said (John 20:17). Once he said: Do not touch me, when he had risen: or perhaps he said it to her who thought he had been taken away by theft, and not raised by his own power. Then, in another book you have it that to those who held his feet and worshipped he said: Do not be afraid (Matt. 28:10).

16. Hold him, then, you too, O soul, as Mary also held him, and say: I held him, and will not let him go: to whom both were saying: We hold you. Go to the Father, but do not forsake me, lest I fall again; and with Mary, who does not stray but holds the tree of life, snatch up with you the one who clings to your feet. I will ascend with you, do not let me go, lest the serpent pour out his venom again, lest he again be able to bite the woman's footstep, so as to supplant Adam. Let your soul say, then: I hold you, and I will bring you into the house of my mother, and into the secret place of her who conceived me (Song 3:4); that I may know your mysteries, that I may drink in your sacraments.

17. I held him, and will not leave him, until I bring him into the house of my mother, and into the chamber of her who conceived me. What is the house of my mother and her chamber, if not the inward and secret part of your nature? Keep this house, cleanse the inmost recesses of this house, so that when the house is spotless, and not defiled by the filth of any adulterous conscience, a spiritual house may rise up into a holy priesthood, joined together by the cornerstone, and the Holy Spirit may dwell in it. She who thus seeks Christ, who thus entreats Christ, is not forsaken by him; nay, she is even visited by him often.

18. Or the house of the mother is perhaps the house in which the discipline of morals shines forth; and that secret place is the one in which are the higher mysteries, in which the honeys of divine grace give forth their fragrance. Is not the remission of sins sweeter than any honey? Is not the resurrection of the dead more pleasing than any flower? Receive, then, Eve, no longer covered with fig leaves, but clothed with the Holy Spirit, and glorious with new grace; for now she does not hide as one stripped, but comes forth as one wrapped in the splendor of a shining garment, because grace clothes her: but neither was Adam naked at first, when innocence clothed him. Seeing her, therefore, cleaving to Christ, and still ascending with him (for he deigns often to meet those who seek him, and to come down to them, that he may lift them up), the daughters of Jerusalem say:

19. (Verse 6.) Who is this who comes up from the desert? Be like that soul who stirs up in herself the love of Christ, whom the powers of the heavens marvel at as she ascends; because she ascends without stumbling, and ascends from this world with joy and gladness, like a shoot of the vine, and like smoke lifts herself up to the heights, fragrant with the fragrance of a holy resurrection and with the sweetness of faith, as you have it written: Who is this who comes up from the desert, like a shoot of the vine, made fragrant with the smoke of incense, with myrrh and frankincense, from all the powders of ointment. Beautifully he expressed its fineness by the comparison with powder and by the mention of ointment; because in Exodus we read that the incense was fine and compounded of many things, and that this is the prophetic incense, which is the prayer of the saints, that it may be directed in the sight of the Lord, as David too says: Let my prayer be directed as incense in your sight (Ps. 140 (141):2).

20. Who is this who comes up from the desert? This place of earth seems a desert and untilled, overgrown with the briers and thorns of our sins. They marvel, that is, how the soul that before was left in hell cleaves to the Word of God, and has ascended like a shoot of the vine, lifting herself up to the higher things like smoke born of fire, and seeking the heights, and moreover is fragrant with good fragrances. And that fragrance breathes the sweetness of holy prayer, which is directed as incense in the sight of the Lord (Ps. 140 (141):2). And in the Apocalypse we read that The smoke of the incense went up from the prayers of the saints (Rev. 8:4), which incense is borne up through the prayers of his angels, namely upon that golden altar which is before the throne of God. And she is fragrant as the sweet ointment of holy prayer, because it is compounded of petition for things eternal and invisible, not for bodily things: yet above all she breathes of myrrh and frankincense, because she has died to sin and lives to God. Whence also in what follows: I will go my way, she says, to the mountain of myrrh, and to the hill of frankincense. There follows:

21. (Verse 7.) Behold, sixty mighty men round about her vine-shoot, armed with drawn swords, and trained in the disciplines of battle. Note who these are, strong and trained in the disciplines of battle. It belongs to a strong man, and a man of the Church, not to look away when something threatens, but to look ahead and, as it were, to scout from a kind of watchtower of the mind, and with provident thought to go to meet the things to come; lest perhaps he say afterwards: I fell into these things because I did not think they could happen. Then, unless adversities are scouted out, they quickly seize us. As in war an unforeseen enemy is scarcely withstood, and, if he finds men unprepared, easily overwhelms them, so evils not scouted out break the mind the more. In these two things, then, lies that excellence of mind: first, that your mind, exercised by good thoughts, should see with a pure heart what is true and honorable: Blessed indeed are the pure in heart, for they too shall see God: and should judge that which is honorable to be the only good. Next, that it be troubled by no occupations, toss on no desires.

22. Nor indeed does anyone do this easily: for what is so difficult as to look down, as from some citadel of wisdom, on riches and all the other things that seem great and lofty to most people? Next, to confirm your judgment by steady reason, and to despise what you have judged light, as being of no profit? Next, if anything adverse happens, and it is thought grave and bitter, to bear it so that you think nothing has happened beyond nature, when you read: Naked I was born, naked I shall go out: what the Lord gave, the Lord has also taken away (Job 1:21); and he had certainly lost both his sons and his means: and to keep in all things the character of the wise and just man, as he kept it who said: As it pleased the Lord, so has it been done; blessed be the name of the Lord (ibid.). And further on: You have spoken like one of the foolish women: if we have received good things from the hand of the Lord, shall we not endure the things that are evil (ibid. 2)?

23. It is, then, no mediocre fortitude of mind, nor one set apart from the rest, that wages war in company with the virtues; but one that alone defends the ornaments of all the virtues and guards their judgments, and that contends in implacable battle against all the vices, unconquered in labours, brave in dangers, sterner against pleasures, hard against allurements, to which she does not know how to lend an ear, nor (as the saying goes) to give them a greeting; that she neglect money and flee avarice as a kind of stain that makes virtue effeminate; for nothing is so contrary to fortitude as to be conquered by gain. Often, when the enemy has been driven off and the line of the adversaries turned to flight, the warrior, while he is caught by the spoils of the slain, has fallen pitiably among the very men he laid low: and legions cast down by their own triumphs, while they are busy with the spoils, have called back upon themselves the enemy who had fled.

24. Let fortitude, then, drive back and trample down so monstrous a plague; let it be neither tempted by desires nor broken by fear; for virtue is consistent with itself, so that it bravely pursues all vices as the poisons of virtue. Let it drive off anger, which takes away counsel, as if with weapons of a kind, and shun it as a sickness. Let it beware also of the craving for glory, which has often done harm when sought too immoderately, and always when usurped. For to be softened by flattery seems not only to be no part of fortitude, but even a mark of cowardice. Seeing the camp of these strong men in the spirit, Jacob says: This, he says, is the camp of God (Gen. 32:2).

25. And what are the sixty? We know that the hebdomad belongs to the Old Testament and the ogdoad to the New, namely when Christ rose again and the day of new salvation dawned for all: on which day the brightness of full and perfect circumcision poured itself into human hearts. And seven and eight make fifteen. These, then, are the seven and eight, that is, the fifteen, for which Hosea hired the harlot whom the Lord had commanded him to hire. The hebdomad, then, and the eighth signify the fulfilment of the two Testaments and the fullness of faith: and the number fifteen taken four times, which makes sixty, the perfection of the Gospel.

26. And what is the vine-shoot round about which these mighty men stand? Naphtali, he says, is a vine let loose, putting forth beauty in its offspring. One branch of the vine is cut off, which seems useless, lest the vine run riot to no purpose in the exuberance of its branches; and another is cut back a little and let loose, that it may bear fruit, whose beauty is put forth in generation; because, while it lifts itself up to the heights, it embraces the vine, and climbing to the top clothes, as it were, the necks of the vines' yoke with the necklace of a precious branch. It too is comely in generation, because it overflows with many fruits on full branches. This is comely, but that is far more beautiful which signifies the branch clinging to the spiritual vine, of which we are the branch, and we can bear fruit if we remain in the vine; but if not, we are cut off. The holy patriarch Naphtali was an abundant branch, whence Moses too says: Naphtali, the fullness of those who receive, shall be filled with blessing from the Lord: he shall possess the sea and the south-west (Deut. 33:23); explaining what Jacob had said, what a vine let loose is, that is, released from the bonds of death through the grace of faith: in which is signified the people of God, called to the freedom of faith and the abundance of grace, spread through the whole world, which clothes the yoke of Christ with good fruit, and embraces the wood of that true vine, that is, the mysteries of the Lord's cross, and does not fear the danger of confessing him, but rather, even when placed in persecutions, glories in the name of Christ.

27. And these strong men hold swords in their hands, which they would not refuse for the name of Christ, or with which they distinguish bodily things from spiritual, the shadow from the truth. For the word of God is living and effective, and more piercing than any two-edged sword, and reaching even to the division of soul and spirit, of joints also and marrow, and a discerner of the thoughts and intentions of the heart: and there is no creature invisible in his sight, but all things are naked and open to his eyes (Heb. 4:12 and 13). For the word of God is a twice-sharpened sword. There are also two other swords in the Church, of which it is said: Lord, behold, here are two swords, namely of the ecclesiastical power and of the worldly, to which a false mercy is opposed.

28. There is a just mercy: but there is also an unjust mercy. Then, in the Law it is written of a certain man: You shall not have pity on him (Deut. 19:13). And in the books of Kingdoms you read that Saul incurred offence for this reason, that he had pity on Agag, the king of the enemies, whom the divine sentence had forbidden to be spared (1 Sam. 15:9). As if someone, moved by the pleading of a robber's children and swayed by the tears of his wife, should think that he ought to be released, in whom the passion for robbery still breathes: will he not hand innocent men over to destruction, who sets free one who is plotting the destruction of many? Certainly, if he holds back the sword, loosens the chains, eases the exile, why does he who could not wrest away the will not snatch away, by whatever milder way he can, the opportunity for robbery? Then, between two, that is, accuser and accused, contending at equal peril of their life, the one if he should not prove his charge, the other if he should not be convicted by the accuser, suppose the judge does not follow what belongs to justice, but, while he pities the accused, condemns the one who proves his case, or, while he favours the accuser who cannot prove his, condemns the innocent: this, then, cannot be called just mercy.

29. In the Church itself, where it is most fitting to show mercy, the form of justice ought to be kept most of all, lest anyone who has been kept away from the fellowship of communion should extort by the easiness of the priest, with a brief little tear, ready for the occasion, or even with more abundant weeping, the communion which he ought to ask for over a very long time. Does not the priest, when he indulges one unworthy man, provoke very many to the contagion of falling? For easiness of pardon gives an incentive to sin. This has been said so that we may know that mercy must be dispensed to debtors according to the word of God, according to reason: otherwise, let the sword carry out what is its own.

30. (Verses 9–10.) King Solomon made himself a couch of the wood of Lebanon; its pillars he made of silver, its reclining-place of gold, its ascent of purple, its back strewn with gems. What is this couch, if not the form of our body? For in the gems air is shown by the appearance of brightness, in the gold fire, water in the silver, earth through the wood; of which four elements the human body consists, in which our soul reclines, if it does not stand, without rest, on the roughness of the mountains or the dryness of the ground, but rests, lifted high above vices, supported by the wood. Whence David too says: May the Lord bring help to him upon the couch of his pain (Ps. 40 (41):4); for how can that be a couch of pain, when that which has no feeling cannot feel pain? But the body is a body of pain, as it is a body of death: Unhappy man that I am! who will deliver me from the body of his death (Rom. 7:24)?

31. And since we have brought in a little verse in which we made mention of the Lord's body, lest perhaps it should trouble anyone reading that the Lord took up a body of pain, let him remember that he both grieved and wept at the death of Lazarus, and was wounded in his passion, and from the wound came forth blood and water, and he breathed out his spirit (John 11:35). Water for the washing, blood for drink, spirit for the resurrection. For the one Christ is for us hope, faith, love: hope in the resurrection, faith in the washing, love in the sacrament. Yet as he took up a body of pain, so also he turned the bedding in sickness; because he turned it to the benefit of human flesh: for by the passion sickness was undone, death by the resurrection. And yet you ought to mourn for the world, to rejoice in the Lord: sorrowful unto penitence, eager for grace; although the Teacher of the Gentiles has prescribed by a salutary precept that we ought both to weep with those who weep and to rejoice with those who rejoice (Rom. 12:15).

32. The middle he paved with love, for the daughters of Jerusalem. Who are these daughters? It ought not to be hidden that those who were begotten earlier seem superior, those begotten later weaker. For the births of this world are compared to a woman's womb; since those are stronger who are born in the vigor of youth, weaker those born in the time of old age. For this world has grown weak with the multitude of its generation, like the womb of one who bears, and, as a creation growing old, lays down the strength of its youth, as the vigor of its powers is now withering. These, then, are the daughters of Jerusalem, that is, the more tender and weaker souls: to these he shows himself, that his image may shine in their confession, shine in their love, shine in their works; and, if it can be, that his whole form may be expressed in them.

33. These daughters of Jerusalem, then, seeing the bride ascending and not resisting, and delighted by the fragrances of her merits, recognizing too that she is the bride of that peaceful Solomon, follow her also with assiduous escort even to the couch of Solomon: because true rest is owed to her in Christ. For Christ is the couch of the saints, on which the hearts of all, wearied by the battles of the world, find rest. On this couch Isaac rested, and blessed his younger son, saying: The elder shall serve the younger: reclining on this couch Jacob blessed the twelve patriarchs: reclining on this couch the daughter of the ruler of the synagogue rose from death: lying on this couch the widow's only son, dead, called by the voice of Christ, loosed the bonds of death. The bride, then, having been brought to the bridegroom's rest, they sing the wedding song, saying to the daughters of Jerusalem:

34. (Verse 11.) Go out and see in King Solomon the crown with which his mother crowned him on the day of his espousals. They sing the epithalamium, and call the other powers of the heavens, or the souls, to see the love which Christ has toward the daughters of Jerusalem. Whence he also deserved to be crowned by his mother as the son of love, as Paul shows, saying: For God has rescued us from the power of darkness, and transferred us into the kingdom of the son of his love (Col. 1:13). The son of love, then, is himself also love: not having love from accidents, but having it by his own substance, like the kingdom of which he says: For this I was born (John 18:37).

35. And therefore he says, Go out, that is, go forth from the anxieties and thoughts of the world: go forth from the straits of the body: go forth from the vanities of the world, and see how great a love the peaceful king has on the day of his espousals: how glorious he is, because he gave resurrection to bodies and joined souls to himself. This is the crown of a great contest, this the splendid gift of Christ's espousals, his blood and his passion. For who could give more than he who did not spare even himself, and laid down his life unto death for us?

36. And what is the crown of glory, if not the Church, which crowns her head, Christ? What is the joy of the whole earth, if not the house of the Christian people, the hall of the saints, of whom it is written: Their sound has gone out into all the earth (Ps. 18 (19):5)? Therefore, because the Church is the crown of glory, it is said: Go out, daughters of Zion, and see King Solomon, in the crown with which his mother crowned him on the day of his espousals, and on the day of the joy of his heart.

37. It is said to the souls that they should go out from the hovels and enclosures of the body, go out beyond the thoughts of the body, go forth from desires, and from cares, and from the other passions of this flesh and of slippery affection: that they should ascend above the world, go out of this world, run to meet Christ, be present and ready, shining with burning torches. The angels of Christ, as it were, speak these words: You cannot see his brightness, his glory, unless you go forth from the cares of human frailty, daughters of Jerusalem. As if they were saying: Why do you seek the living among the dead (Luke 24:5)? Christ is not sought within this world, he who willed that his disciples should be above the world.

38. What is the crown with which Christ is crowned, if not the crown of glory? Joseph had the crown of chastity, Paul of justice, Peter of faith. There are crowns of the single virtues. Christ alone has the crown of glory, with which the Church crowned him. In this crown are all crowns; for glory is not the portion of one crown, but the reward of all crowns.

Chapter IV

1. (Verse 1.) Now the Bridegroom, turning to the praise of the bride, says: How beautiful you are, my friend, how beautiful you are! Your eyes are like doves, apart from your silence. Beautiful in preaching, beautiful in conduct. Her eyes also, he says, are like doves. The eyes are men, namely those adorned with spiritual senses, who are keen to see the mysteries and ready to penetrate the secrets of divine Scripture, shining with rational milk, in whom there is no spotted confusion of any guile, but the pure and spotless simplicity of a simple affection. Therefore he afterward mentioned these doves, washed in the abundance of waters, in milk.

2. Now the rest, the teeth and the cheeks, and the lips of the bride like a scarlet cord, we understand to be the virtues of the soul and the diversities of teachers: who either supply spiritual nourishment to the mind by diligent dispensing, or bind the hearer by the preaching of the Lord's cross, as by a kind of line of the Word; or are bashful in modesty and most pleasing in the flower of youth. Even if they are held back from touch by modesty, yet let the fragrance of Christ breathe in them; and as the ointment descends upon the cheeks as from the priestly head, so in them too let the beauty of doctrine shine forth.

3. Your eyes are like doves, apart from your silence; because she both sees spiritually and knows that there is a time to be silent and a time to speak; so that she may bring forth her speech in due time, lest by untimely speaking she incur sin.

4. Your eyes are like doves. Here the simplicity and grace of the dove, that is, of the Church, is revealed. See, then, the simplicity of the eye: for he who sees a just man and rejoices himself also wishes to be just; for it is a beautiful thing that one should delight in others in that which he himself wishes to keep in himself, if he can. For it is implanted in good men that the modest man loves the chaste, the wise man the prudent, with dutiful affection, the merciful man the liberal: that they love their own virtues in others. For many, too, the sight of a just man is an admonition to correction, but for the more perfect it is joy. For if there is so great a power in natural things that an animal, when seen, benefits those who have jaundice, so that even the body of that creature when dead is said to benefit those who have fallen into an affliction of this kind, if it has been shown to them, can we doubt that the sight of the just man heals, whose eyes are like doves, simple and modest? So then, a worthless irrational animal has such power that it can heal a man in the brief moment in which it is seen: shall a just man, if only he is looked upon with faith by one who desires to receive benefit from him, confer nothing? How beautiful, then, that you should be seen and be of benefit!

5. A just man, then, is a good thing. For this reason, indeed, the apostle Paul went up to Jerusalem, to see the just; and he stayed with Peter fifteen days, that he might gain something from living with him (Gal. 1:18). For this reason Paul himself and Barnabas, when they entered Jerusalem, were magnificently received by the Church and the apostles and the elders; but when they wished to go away, they were asked not to depart; and, as we read afterward of Paul, he was escorted with tears. Do not even the very rays of the eyes seem to pour a certain power into those who faithfully desire to see him?

6. But just as the just man gladdens the heart of the innocent when he is seen, so the wicked are tormented by acquaintance with the just, because they are reproved even by the silent conduct of the saints. Chastity torments the incontinent, liberality avarice, faith impiety. On this point too let us take a like example from a worthless creature. For just as we have said that a dumb animal does good when it is seen, so we perceive that the wolf does harm if it has seen someone first; for those whom the wolf has seen first are said to lose their voice. The basilisk too (this is a harmful serpent), if it sees any animal first, is said to kill it; and it is alleged that he is at once slain who has been seen first by a serpent of this kind. The serpent itself is also said to die if it has been forestalled by the sight of a man. So if there is so great a power either in the eyes of a serpent or in the eyes of a man that, if one sees the other first, he can kill him, is there no power in the eyes of the just man, who is filled with the grace of virtue, especially since faith works so much, that both she who touched the fringe of the Lord was healed, and he on whom the Lord Jesus fixed his gaze at once drew the grace of healing from his eyes (Matt. 9:22)?

7. But he who sees a just man ought to know what he sees. He does not see him in the body, not in his clothing, not in his patrimony, not in his countenance: but he sees within. He does not, I say, see him, unless he has seen his mind, unless he has attended to his speech, unless he has been able to grasp his meaning, has been able to take up wisdom from his discourse. Then, therefore, he will rejoice, when he has looked into these things, when he has come to know them. So then let us too, wherever we hear of a just man, hasten to see him, like that woman who heard that the Lord Jesus was reclining at table in the house of the Pharisee, and went in, and poured ointment over his feet (Luke 7:37 and 38). Let us be imitators of her, for who would doubt that in that woman there is a figure of the Church? Wherever, then, a just man sits, wherever he reclines, let us hasten to see him. It is a precious thing to see a just man, so that you may see him according to the image of God. What is outside profits nothing; what is within heals. Yet indeed, in him who is outside we often behold him who is within: so that, if we have seen a poor man, we honor in the poor man him after whose likeness he was made, of whom he says: You gave me to eat, for what you gave to one of these, you gave to me (Matt. 25:35 and 40).

8. Your hair is like a flock of shorn goats, which have been revealed from Mount Gilead. It says "hair" because the power of all the senses is in the head: The eyes indeed of the wise man are in his head (Eccles. 2). Deep, then, is the prudence of teachers, which can reveal those things that are obscure, and open up the depths of meanings.

9. (Verse 2.) Your teeth are like a flock of shorn goats, which have come up from the washing, all of which bear twins, and there is none barren among them. What is said outwardly of goats must be understood mystically of the flock of the Church. Do not let these animals seem worthless to you: you see that this flock pastures on the heights, bold on the mountain. And so, where there are precipices for others, there is no danger for goats; where there is danger for others, there is that flock's nourishment, there the food is sweeter, there the fruit more choice. They are watched over by their shepherds as they hang from a thorny rock; where the inroads of wolves cannot come, there fruitful trees supply them untouched fruit. One may see them distended with abundant milk, anxious over their tender offspring with a mother's tenderness. Therefore the Holy Spirit chose them, to compare with them the assembly of the venerable Church.

10. And that you may hear it mystically: the hair of the Word is the height and a certain eminence of just souls, since the sense of the wise man is in his head. For it is certain that wisdom lies in the height of human thought. And as goats are shorn so that they may put off what is superfluous, so too holy Church has a flock of shorn souls, that is, the virtues of many souls, in which flock you could find nothing senseless, nothing superfluous; since faith has made them wise, and spiritual grace has cleansed them from every stain of superfluous things.

11. Rightly, then, the souls of the just have been revealed, and revealed from Mount Gilead, that is, from the migration of testimony, because the heavenly testimony migrated from the Synagogue to the Church. On this mountain, therefore, grow incense, resin and the other fragrances which those Ishmaelite merchants, as you have it in the first book of the Old Testament, were carrying (Gen. 37:25). And so, just as goats, refreshed with good food and flourishing in the warmth of the sun, are washed in the river and, leaping for joy, come up clean from the river, so the souls of the just come up from the spiritual washing.

12. These truly are they who bear twins, in whom there is no barrenness of virtues, no sterility of merits. Well do they bear twins, because they double their senses; whence you have it written in Proverbs: And do you write these things for yourself threefold, for counsel and for knowledge (Prov. 22:20). He set down first a threefold writing, and added two things, counsel and knowledge. But knowledge is twofold, one of incorporeal things, the other of corporeal; whence you have also in what follows: Your two breasts are like two twin fawns of a roe, which feed among the lilies, until the day breathes and the shadows decline (Verse 5).

13. We have spoken of fruitfulness; let us speak of the teeth. For many who sail, and those who hasten on a journey by land, when they see some beautiful place, linger for the sake of delight; they feast their eyes and refresh their mind. Nor is it reckoned any delay to the journey, but a pleasure. So too it is our heart's desire to consider the most beautiful teeth of just souls; for Scripture has taught that the teeth of the just are most beautiful, speaking according to the letter of the patriarch Judah, but spiritually of Christ: His eyes are joyful from wine, and his teeth like milk (Gen. 49:12). In this it proclaimed, surely, not the functions of human flesh but the gifts of divine grace. The example teaches, therefore, that the teeth are not to be passed over where we have discussed the eyes.

14. Who, then, are the teeth of just souls, if not those which, receiving food that is shapeless and hard, or often cold, or hot beyond measure, now grind it, now warm it, now temper it, according to the quality of the nourishment? They grind what is hard, lest the harshness of the letter in the Old Testament and the rigidity of worldly understanding, unless it has been dissolved by the spiritual tooth, should block the passages of the saving books and choke the very vital organs, and a kind of gullet of the soul, through heedless gluttony. It is fitting, then, that you first divide the food that is taken, if it seems solid to you, and break it up; and, once it is softened, pour it without any harm to the soul into all its members by a natural distribution, so that its whole body may feast on the vital juice. Take nothing corpse-like, nothing dead, into your mouth, lest it be said: Their throat is an open sepulchre (Ps. 13 (14):3). But draw in the living word, so that it may work in the inmost parts of your mind. These teeth are whiter than milk, because they are the teeth of the just.

15. Finally, although all our fathers were baptized in Moses in the cloud and in the sea, yet it is not idly written: That all ate the same spiritual food, and all drank the same spiritual drink (1 Cor. 10:3 and 4); so that a certain greater brightness might come to these teeth of the saints, which we shall recognize to have been cleansed, after the crossing of the Red Sea, when the bitterness of the fountain of myrrh had been tempered by the grace of the wood (Exod. 15:25); then by the drink of twelve fountains; lastly by the watering of the rock that poured forth a spiritual wave: For the Rock was Christ (1 Cor. 10:3 and 4). Therefore they also ate manna (Num. 20:11), so that, washed so many times, they might eat the bread, as it is written, of angels (Ps. 77 (78):25). Now too, in the mysteries of the Gospel, you recognize that the baptized, although washed in the whole body, are nonetheless afterward cleansed by spiritual food and drink; for that is the true brightness of the teeth, where the melodious confession of a mind of good conscience resounds. That is the clean eye, which no beam of grave sins has weighed down, which no mote of light impurities has disturbed.

16. Your teeth are like a flock of shorn goats, which have come up from the washing, all of which bear twins, and there is none barren among them. This is no ordinary praise, first through the apt comparison with the shorn ones; for we know that goats both pasture on the heights without danger and take their food securely on steep places, and then, when they are shorn, are unburdened of what is superfluous. To their flock the Church is compared, having in herself many virtues of souls, which through the washing put off superfluous sins, which bring to Christ mystical faith and moral grace, which speak of the cross of the Lord Jesus. In these the Church is fair, whence God the Word says to her: Behold, you are fair, my nearest one; behold, you are fair; your eyes are like doves. You are all fair, my nearest one, and there is no reproach in you; because guilt has been sunk. Be present here from Lebanon, bride, be present here from Lebanon, and you will pass through, from the beginning of faith; because, renouncing the world, she has passed over the world, has passed through to Christ.

17. (Verse 3.) Your lips are like a scarlet cord. For in scarlet the appearance of fire, and the blood of the Lord's cross, glows red. Scarlet are the lips of the Lord, which spoke of his own passion. Indeed, in Exodus scarlet is placed for fire (Exod. 25:4). For the world does not consist of scarlet, but of four elements. But in scarlet a figure of fire is expressed, without whose heat penetrating heaven and air, seas and lands, all things would dissolve, as if their strength were exhausted. By the cord, then, we recognize the bond of persuasion; by the scarlet, either the ardor of desire that sparkles in the minds of the hearers, or a sign of the passion.

18. (Verse 4.) Your neck is like the tower of David, which was built in Thalphioth; a thousand shields hang on it, all the darts of the mighty. For the neck is raised toward God and fit for the yoke of Christ, a neck that is bowed toward the earth by no bending of allurements; as is the royal tower of Christ, on which Nebuchadnezzar could not impose his yoke. For that David, strong of hand, built that tower, and built it above the heights of the walls, that it might serve at once for defense and for beauty: for defense, because it sees the enemy beforehand and drives him off; for beauty, because it towers not only among low things but also among lofty ones. Yet it serves for defense or for beauty only if it has in itself the doctrines of the word like certain necklaces of medallions; if it has also the darts of the mighty prophets, which are aimed, by certain strong arms of faith, against every height that exalts itself.

19. Do not, then, lead your soul down into the dust of death, to which the Lord has even given a natural height and vigor, by which she may rouse herself and rise up. Hence also the saying is fittingly taken as spoken of this union of soul and body, because, in the natural mystery of our life, the soul, joined to the body, as it were creeps on the ground and cleaves to the pavement, partly because of this earthly dwelling, partly because this body is from the earth. So both the lodging of our region and the very matter of our body agree with this saying. And therefore the Apostle beautifully longs to be freed from the body of this death; because we are shut in a kind of prison, and, set in a kind of hovel of luxury, we are wrapped in the darkness of sins.

20. Let us, then, walk according to the will of God, so that we may be said to cleave to God. For he who lives according to the appetite of the body is flesh; he who lives according to the precepts of God is spirit. Let not our soul, then, become flesh, that is, so that we are called flesh; as were those too who perished in the flood, of whom it was said: Because they are flesh (Gen. 6:3). But rather let our flesh, obedient to the helm of the soul, become soul, and deserve to be called by this name; as the family of the patriarch Jacob was called, and the holy posterity of his line; for thus it is written: These are the sons of Bilhah, whom Laban gave to Rachel his daughter: she bore these to Jacob, seven souls. And in the same place: But all the souls who entered with Jacob into Egypt, who came out of his thighs, besides the wives of his sons, were sixty-six (Gen. 46:25 and 26). And at the beginning of Exodus we read: Now all the souls from Jacob were five and seventy (Exod. 1:5).

21. Those, then, who dwelt with Joseph and came out of Egypt are souls; but those who, though they were angels of God, that is, imitating the grace of the angels by the uprightness of their life (For those who (Matt. 22:30) do not take wives, and who do not marry, will be like the angels of God in heaven); those, then, who, though they seemed to be angels, were captured by womanly beauty, these are flesh, as the Lord God said: My spirit shall not remain in men forever, because they are flesh (Gen. 6:3). And rightly those who are free of lust are compared to angels; for they are not flesh, because they are not in the flesh but in the spirit: such as were those who followed the Apostle as their teacher, to whom he says: But you are not in the flesh, but in the Spirit (Rom. 8:9). But those who are captured by bodily lust for women are flesh: and would that they were only flesh, and not also neighing horses! Because, he says, they neighed after the wife of their neighbor (Jer. 5:8).

22. (Verse 7.) You are all fair, my nearest one, and there is no reproach in you. Who, indeed, can reckon any beauty greater than the beauty of her who is loved by the king, approved by the judge, dedicated to the Lord, consecrated to God? Always a bride, always unwedded; so that neither does love have an end, nor modesty a loss. This assuredly is true beauty, which lacks nothing, which alone deserves to hear from the Lord: You are all fair, my nearest one, and there is no reproach in you.

23. (Verse 8.) Come here from Lebanon, come here from Lebanon: you will pass and pass through, from the beginning of faith, from the head of Senir and Hermon, from the dens of lions, from the mountains of leopards. By these signs it is shown that the perfect and irreproachable beauty of the virginal soul, consecrated to the divine altars, not bent toward mortal things amid the encounters and dens of spiritual beasts, but intent on the mysteries of God, has won the beloved, whose breasts are full of gladness; for wine gladdens the heart of man.

24. And the fragrance, he says, of your garments is like the fragrance of Lebanon. Come from Lebanon, bride, come from Lebanon: you will pass and pass through, from the beginning of faith: you will pass into the world to contend, you will pass through to Christ to triumph over the world. You have heard that he has separated you from the lions and the leopards, that is, from the assaults of the spiritual forces of wickedness; you have heard that the beauty of your virtues pleases him.

25. Be present here from Lebanon, bride, be present here from Lebanon: you will pass and pass through, from the beginning of faith, from the head of Senir and Hermon, from the caves of lions, from the mountains of leopards; that is, go out from the body, and strip yourself wholly. For you cannot be present with me unless you first sojourn away from the body; since those who are in the flesh sojourn away from the kingdom of God. Be present, he says, be present. Well did he repeat it, because, whether present or absent, you ought to be present to the Lord your God and to please him: be present when present, be present when absent, even if you are still in the body; for all are present to me whose faith is with me. He is present to me who has gone out of the world; he is present to me who thinks of me, gazes on me, hopes in me, to whom I am his portion; he is present to me who has been absent from himself: he is with me who is not within himself, since he who is in the flesh is not in the spirit; he is with me who goes out from himself; he is near me who has been outside himself; he is whole to me who has lost his soul for my sake. And therefore, Be present, be present, bride; you will pass and pass through, from the beginning of faith. She who has come to Christ passes and passes through the others: she passes by the merit of faith and the brightness of works, which shines like Senir and Hermon, that is, the way of the lamp; she passes when the temptations of the world have been conquered and the spiritual forces of wickedness overcome, seeking the crown of the lawful contest.

26. (Verse 9.) You have wounded my heart, my sister, bride, you have wounded my heart with one of your eyes, and with one hair of your neck. For these eyes are the eyes of the mind, namely of the inner man, not these eyes which perform the service of sight. For there is an eye both of the mind and of the flesh; but that eye is blind which does not see the things that are divine, which is vainly puffed up by the mind of the flesh. There is also another eye, the sense of Christ, by which the Church sees Christ, as he himself says to the Bride: You have captured our heart with one of your eyes. Rightly is Christ seen with one eye, because he is not seen with the carnal eye; or because the Church, having two eyes, the moral and the mystical, sees Christ more with the eye of faith; for the mystical eye is keener, the moral sweeter.

27. And perhaps these are the eyes with which Paul saw eternal things, when he began not to see temporal things. Indeed, he who did not see Christ before he lost his eyes saw him after he had lost the sight of his eyes; for he saw, who said: Who are you, Lord (Acts 9:5)? He certainly saw Christ, whom he even confessed as Lord. And below: Lord, he says, what do you wish me to do? Did he not, then, see him whose command he was awaiting? With what eyes, then, did Paul begin to see more, if not with those which he himself showed us, saying: I will pray with the spirit, I will pray also with the mind (1 Cor. 14:15)? Indeed, that you may know that he saw by praying: It came to pass, he says, when I returned to Jerusalem, while I was praying in the temple, I was seized with fear; I saw him saying to me: Hasten, go out quickly from Jerusalem; for they will not receive your testimony about me (Acts 22:17 and 18).

28. These eyes, then, fail for the word of God, and say: When will you comfort me? With these eyes the prophets were called seers, because through revelation they discerned with the mind the things that were hidden. But because the eye of the mind and the eye of the flesh are one eye, and a man is sustained by consolation only when flesh and mind do not desire different things but desire one thing and seek one thing, therefore they are intent on him who said: I and the Father are one (John 10:30). These eyes also confess that they are one, because they are engaged in one and the same desire and office.

29. (Verse 10.) Your breasts are more beautiful than wine, and the fragrance of your ointments is above all spices. And below: The fragrance of your garments is like that of Lebanon. See what progress you grant us, virgin! For your first fragrance is above all the spices which were put into the burial of the Savior, and it breathes forth that the dead motions of the body and the delights of the members have perished. Your second fragrance, like the fragrance of Lebanon, exhales the integrity of the Lord's body, the flower of virginal chastity. Let your works, then, build the honeycomb. The fragrance of the garments, too, is the fragrance of our actions. Bring your hands, then, to your nostrils, and explore the fragrance of your actions with an unwearied and ever-watchful eagerness of mind. The fragrance of your right hand will soothe you, and your members will breathe the fragrance of the resurrection, your fingers will drip with myrrh, that is, your spiritual deeds will be fragrant with the grace of true faith. You take pleasure, then, virgin, from your inner body, and you yourself are sweet to yourself, you yourself are pleasant to yourself, you yourself (which often happens to sinners) do not begin to displease yourself.

30. (Verse 11.) Your lips, bride, are a dripping honeycomb. Let your works, then, build the honeycomb; for virginity is worthy to be compared to bees, so laborious, so modest, so continent: the bee feeds on dew, knows no mating, makes honey. The virgin's dew, too, is the divine speech, because the words of God descend like dew. The modesty of the virgin is an undefiled nature; the offspring of the virgin is the fruit of the lips, free of bitterness, fertile in sweetness: the labor is in common, common is the fruit. How I would wish you, daughter, to be an imitator of this little bee, whose food is a flower, whose offspring is gathered by the mouth and formed by the mouth. Imitate her, daughter; let your words put forward no veil of guile, have no wrapping of fraud, so that they may both have purity and be full of gravity. Let an eternal posterity of your merits, too, be brought forth for you by your mouth; and gather not for yourself alone, but for many as well; for how do you know when your soul may be required of you? Lest, leaving behind storehouses heaped with grain, which will be of use neither to your life nor to your merits, you be snatched away to where you cannot carry your treasure. Be rich, then, but for the poor, so that those who are sharers of your nature may also be sharers of your means.

31. Your lips, bride, are a dripping honeycomb. Teach us, Solomon, what the honeycomb is. For you have said: Good words are a honeycomb of honey (Prov. 16:24). And truly good is the honeycomb which the Church eats, fragrant with honey gathered by the spiritual abundance of many prophets, as of bees. This is the honey of which he says: I have eaten my bread with my honey (Song 5:1). The lips of the preacher drip honey when the members of a fallen soul, bruised by hard falls or ruins, are restored. Your lips drip a honeycomb, bride; honey and milk are under your tongue; for the mouth of the just drips wisdom; from the mouth of the just proceed the honeys of sweetness and mercy; in the mouth of the just there is no guile, no deceit, no bitterness of sin. The Church hears the words of the just, the people of God hears the words of the wise, delights in the sweetness of his address, is soothed by the pleasantness of moral discourse, saying: How sweet are your words to my throat, above honey and the honeycomb to my mouth (Ps. 118 (119):103)! For the honey of bees delights for an hour, but its taste soon vanishes, and often unhealthy inner parts are harmed by it; but the honeys of moral words, even if they sting, do not harm.

32. Yet know to whom these things are to be entrusted; for it is written: Say nothing in the ear of a fool, lest at some time he mock your prudent words (Prov. 23:9). For the fool, who cannot perceive the sweetness of your speech, will vomit it up and reject it. How can the words of God be sweet in your throat, in which there is the bitterness of wickedness? How can there be honey and milk under your tongue, when your tongue frames guile, so that you conceive one thing in your breast and put forward another in public speech, to deceive the unwary? And this though the apostle Peter tells you to put away all malice and cast off the paint of pretense, saying: As newborn infants, rational and without guile, desire milk (1 Pet. 2:2). For, showing us what it is to have honey and milk under the tongue, he teaches that we should know how not to curse those who curse us, but to return a blessing; that we should not know how to hate anything but the way of iniquity, so that with resisting affection we may beware of doing what we detest.

33. (Verse 12.) An enclosed garden is my sister, bride, an enclosed garden, a sealed fountain. Your shoots, etc. Because in gardens of this kind the wave of a pure fountain reflects the image of God impressed by the seals, and the streams are not troubled, spattered with mud from the wallowing-places of spiritual beasts. Hence that modesty, hedged in by the spirit as by a wall, is shut in, lest it lie open to plunder. And so, like a garden inaccessible to thieves, it is fragrant of the vine, it breathes of the olive, it gleams with the rose; so that in the vine religion, in the olive peace, in the rose the modesty of consecrated virginity may grow. This is the fragrance which the patriarch Jacob breathed forth, when he deserved to hear: Behold, the fragrance of my son is like the fragrance of a full field (Gen. 27:27). For although the field of the holy patriarch was full of almost all fruits, yet that one produced crops by a greater labor of virtue, this one flowers. And so gird yourself, virgin, and if you wish a garden of this kind to breathe for you, shut it with the prophetic precepts: Set a guard on your mouth, and a door of enclosure about your lips (Ps. 140 (141):3).

34. An enclosed garden is my sister, bride, an enclosed garden, a sealed fountain; your shoots are a paradise of pomegranates, with the fruit of apples, henna. The Bride is praised because she is a garden, having in herself the fragrance of that full field of which Isaac says: The fragrance of my son is as the fragrance of a full field (Gen. 27:27). The good soul, then, breathes forth the fragrances of justice. And perhaps the field is the patriarch, and the garden is the soul of some lesser person, as it were a portion of the field: and An enclosed garden, lest it be invaded by beasts; and A sealed fountain, she who has washed away her own sins by the integrity of the seal and the perseverance of faith. For he who has received from the Church has something to refer to the grace of virginity; because, set in the paradise of delight, she gathers spiritual fruits without labor, so that the souls of the patriarchs, by a kind of rustic labor of their soul, bring her their fruits, whereby she may gather perpetual sweetness: she who is rightly called a sealed fountain, because the image of the invisible God is expressed in her.

35. An enclosed garden is my sister, bride, an enclosed garden, a sealed fountain. All the teachers agree that in paradise there is both the tree of life, rooted, and the tree of knowledge, by which one may discern good and evil, and the other trees, full of vigor, full of quickening, breathing and rational. From this it is gathered that the earthly paradise itself can be seen to be, not in any soil, but in our ruling part, by which the soul is animated and quickened through the virtues and the infusion of the Holy Spirit. Indeed, Solomon, in the spirit, clearly declared that paradise is in man. And because he expresses the mysteries either of the soul and the Word or of Christ and the Church, therefore he says of the virgin soul, or of the Church, which he wished to present as a chaste virgin to Christ: An enclosed paradise, my sister, bride, an enclosed paradise, a sealed fountain. "Paradise" is the Greek word, in Latin it is called "garden". Indeed, Susanna was in a paradise, and so it is read in Latin; and Adam was in paradise, and so we read. Let it not trouble you, then, that some Latin manuscripts have "garden", others "paradise". There, then, is the chaste wife, where the virgin also is. Although the virgin, set apart, has her own bars and seals, yet both are in paradise, so that, against the heats of the body and the burning of the flesh, she may be cooled by the shady bowers of the virtues.

36. So paradise is in our ruling part, growing wooded with the plantings of very many opinions, in which God set, in the chief place, the tree of life, that is, the root of piety. For this is the substance of our life, if we render due worship to our Lord and God. He also set there a seedbed of the knowledge of good and evil; for man alone among the other earthly living creatures has the knowledge of good and evil. There are also other diverse plantings there, whose fruits are the virtues. But because the disposition of man, capable of knowledge, was known by God to incline more quickly toward cunning than toward the height of prudence, and since the quality of his own work could not be hidden from that judge who set fixed bounds in our soul, he willed, as the provident author of our salvation, to banish cunning from paradise, and to pour in the zeal for life and the discipline of piety. And therefore he commanded man that of every tree which is in paradise he should eat, but of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil he should not eat. But because every creature is subject to passion, delight therefore crept into human affections as by the gliding of a serpent.

37. And so not without reason did holy Moses figure delight under the likeness of a serpent; for it lies prone on its belly like a serpent, not walking on feet nor raised on any legs, but slippery with a winding curve, as it were, of its whole body. Earth is its food, as it is the serpent's, since it knows no heavenly food; for it feeds on bodily things, and is changed into various forms of desires, and bends itself into tortuous windings. It has poison in its teeth, by which every luxurious man disembowels himself, the glutton slays himself, the gourmand destroys himself. How many have wines burst, drunkenness dissolved, indigestion distended! Now I understand for what reason the Lord God breathed into the face of man: for there are all the senses, there the seat and the allurement of delight, in the eyes, ears, nostrils and mouth; so that he might make our senses stronger against delight. This, then, like the serpent, poured cunning into us; for it is not delight, but labor and long meditation, with the grace of God, that give perfect prudence. Yet because the inheritance of the human race has been entangled in the frauds of the serpent, let us follow the cunning of the serpent in this: that we do not throw our head into dangers, but guard it whole above all else; and our head is Christ. Let this remain unharmed, so that the poisons of the serpent cannot hurt us: for wisdom is good with an inheritance, that is, with faith; since there is an inheritance of those who believe in the Lord.

38. But if the first man, who, set in paradise, used to speak with God, could fall so easily, created from virgin earth, which had been formed and created at the word of God by a fresh birth, not yet clotted with parricidal blood and the blood of others, not yet polluted with crimes and disgrace, while our flesh was not yet condemned by the curse of a guilty inheritance: how much more easily afterward has the way, slippery toward sin, brought a greater precipice upon the human race, since the worse has succeeded the more tolerable through the turns of generation! For if the magnet stone has so great a power of nature that it draws iron to itself and transfuses itself into its form, so that, when many who wish to test it have brought several iron rings to that stone, it holds them all in like manner; then, if you bring another ring to the ring to which the stone has clung, and again set one after another in order, although the power of nature passes from that stone into all of them in order, yet it binds the first with a stronger link, the later ones with a looser: how much more has the condition and nature of the human race slipped from a purer state into a worse, the more wicked the generation it has reached! For if nature is diminished through those kinds of things which are not capable of guilt, how much more is its power dulled through minds and limbs polluted with the stain of crimes! Hence, because innocence had been abolished by the victory of malice, there was none who did good, there was not even one. The Lord came, then, to restore the grace of nature, nay rather to increase it; so that where sin abounded, grace might abound the more.

39. It is clear, then, that God is the author of man, and that God is one, not many gods, but one who made the world, and one world, not many worlds, as the philosophers say. First, then, he created the world, then the inhabitant of the world, to whom the whole world should be a homeland: for if today, wherever a wise man goes, he is everywhere a citizen, everywhere understands things to be his own, nowhere judges himself a foreigner, nowhere a guest, how much more was that first man an inhabitant of the whole world, and, as it is called in Greek, a cosmopolites (citizen of the world), God's newly made companion in converse, a constant fellow citizen of the saints! Planted together with the virtues, set over all the earthly living creatures, those of the sea and those that fly, he thought the whole world his own possession; and the Lord protected him as his own work, and did not abandon him, as a good parent and author. Indeed, he cherished the one he had created so far as to redeem him when disowned, to receive him back when banished, to raise him up when dead by the passion of his only-begotten Son. God, then, is the author of man; and the good worker loves his work, and the good father does not forsake him whom, like a rich householder, he has also redeemed with the wealth of his own inheritance.

40. Let us beware, then, lest that woman, that is, the passion of our senses, herself deceived and deluded by delight, make effeminate this man, that is, νοῦν (the mind), and, hemming him in, draw him into her laws and judgment. Let us flee delight as a serpent: it has many arts, and most of all in man. For other living creatures are caught by greed for food; man, the more senses of eyes and ears he has, the greater the dangers. And so beware lest the vigor of your mind, bent by a kind of union with bodily pleasure, be softened and dissolved into all its embraces, and open its fountain, which ought to be closed and hedged in by the zeal of attention and the consideration of reason: For an enclosed garden, a sealed fountain. For when the sense of the mind is relaxed, bodily delights, exceedingly pernicious, pour themselves forth and flow into an appetite full of grave danger; delights which the considered watch of a lively mind, had it remained, would have reined in.

41. (Verses 13–14.) Your shoots, he says, are a paradise of pomegranates with the fruit of apples, henna with nard, nard and saffron, calamus and cinnamon, with all the trees of Lebanon, myrrh and aloes with all the first ointments. The gifts of the soul are praised which were sent by the Bridegroom, endowed with which she was coming (and to God pious souls are good fragrances): myrrh, aloes, saffron and the rest, with which the grace of gardens breathes and the stench of sins is abolished. And so, secure in so great a commendation, she asks the north wind, a heavy wind, to be still, lest it shake off the flowers; and the south wind to blow, that is, that winter, as it were, may pass and the mildness of a softer breeze bring spring. She invites the Bridegroom into her garden; the bridegroom comes down and, delighted by the variety of its fruits, rejoices that he has found stronger food and has found sweeter food too. For there is, as it were, a kind of bread of the Word, and a honey: one speech more forceful, another persuasive. There is also one faith more fervent, like wine; another more clear, as is the juice of milk. This food Christ feasts on in us, this cup he drinks, and by the inebriation of his drink he summons us to make our going forth from lower things to better and best.

42. Hearing these things, the soul drank in the inebriation of heavenly mysteries, and, as though made drowsy by wine, and as if placed in ecstasy or stupor, she says: I sleep, and my heart keeps watch (Song 5:2). Then, struck by the light of the present Word, she is roused by the Word. This is the fourth progress of the soul. For first, impatient with love and not bearing the delays of the Word, she asked that she might be granted kisses, and she was granted to see the one she desired. Second, brought into the chamber of the king, while she was exchanging mutual speech, she rested in his shadow, and suddenly the Word departed from her in the middle of the conversation; yet he was not long absent from her as she sought him, but came leaping over the mountains and bounding across the hills. And not long after, like a young roe or a fawn of the harts, while he was speaking to his beloved, he leapt away and left her. Third, when she did not find him whom she sought on her bed and by night, in the city and the marketplace, in the streets, and at length, through her prayers, grace called her back as she sought, so much so that she was even called nearer by the bridegroom. Fourth, when she herself was already sleeping, she is roused by him, although she was keeping watch in her heart, so that she at once heard the voice of the one knocking: but she suffered delay while she rose (because she could not grasp the swiftness of the Word), and while she was opening the door, the Word passed by, and she herself went out at his Word; and, seeking him through wounds, but wounds of love, she at last barely found him, and held him, so that she might not lose him afterward. In sum, I have touched on these things in a brief discourse; now let us examine them one by one.

43. (Verse 15.) A fountain of gardens, a well of living waters, which flow with a rush from Lebanon. You have these in Solomon, because his Proverbs are moral, Ecclesiastes natural, in which he as it were despises the vanities of this world; his Song of Songs is mystical. You have it also in the prophet: Sow for yourselves unto justice, gather the vintage unto the fruit of life, light for yourselves the light of knowledge (Hos. 10:12); for this is the light of knowledge, to have the perfection of love. And therefore it is said: Fear not (John 12:15); for love casts out fear.

44. But that we may know that Solomon interpreted in this way these three wells which Jacob dug, so as to refer them to moral, natural and mystical doctrine, he placed these wells in each of his books, which he wrote on moral and natural or mystical matters. For in Proverbs too, when he was saying that the appearance of worldly allurement must be shunned, he says: Drink water from your own vessels, and from the springs of your own wells, and let waters overflow for you from your own fountain (Prov. 5:15). And below: Let the fountain of your water be your own, and rejoice with the wife who is from your youth (ibid. 18); because true wisdom is a remedy for us against the temptations of the world. Moral doctrine too it is that washes away with its watering, and wipes off with the stream of its fountain, the image of worldly pleasure smeared with a kind of harlot's paints.

45. Of natural things too you have the well itself in Ecclesiastes: I made myself pools of water, to water from them a sprouting wood (Eccles. 2:6). Nor let it trouble you that he put pools in place of a well, since Moses too spoke of the well of breadth; because he who has risen above this world with a pious mind is released from all anxiety and straits. Not without reason, then, does Ecclesiastes have pools, he who saw that there is no abundance under the sun: but if anyone wishes to abound, let him abound in Christ.

46. Of mystical things there remains for us the well too, which we also find in the Song of Songs, where Scripture says: A fountain of gardens, a well of living water, and descending with a rush from Lebanon. For if you pursue the depth of the mysteries, mystical wisdom seems to you a well, as though set in the deep; but if you wish to draw the abundance of love, which is greater and richer than faith and hope, then it is a fountain for you. For love overflows, so that you can both draw it close at hand and water with its abundance your garden, overflowing with spiritual fruits. And because he who has love is himself a well of breadth, therefore he said that where love is, there a great rush descends from Lebanon.

47. But lest it trouble anyone that he called the same thing both a well and a fountain, let the Gospel too instruct you, in which it is written: That Jesus came to a city of Samaria which is called Sychar, near the estate which Jacob gave to his son Joseph (John 4:5 and 6). Now Jacob's fountain was there: And being wearied, he says, he was sitting thus upon the well. Hence we recognize that there too this well is referred to mystical doctrine, because there that Samaritan woman, that is, the keeper (and the keeper of the heavenly precepts), drew from that well the divine mysteries, learning that God is spirit, and is worshiped not in a place but in spirit; and that the Messiah has come, who is Christ. When she had heard these things, that woman, who bears the figure of the Church, came to know the sacraments of the Law and believed.

48. A fountain of gardens, a well of living waters, and a rush descending from Lebanon. These rushes the Church has drawn down from Lebanon; by this rush sins are washed away; by this rush of the pure fountain of the Holy Spirit the Bride was present from Lebanon, and from the beginning of faith passed over the world, and passed through to the kingdom. To some spiritual grace is a fountain, to others a well, according to our capacity: to some an enclosed garden, a sealed fountain; to others the fountain of gardens, which is counted in the dowry of the Church; to others a rush descending from Lebanon, and a great rush which never fails. For the breasts do not fail from the rock, nor the snow from Lebanon, nor the water which is borne by a strong wind to the virgin Jerusalem.

49. The rush descended from Lebanon when, the apostles and many believers being gathered together, suddenly there came a sound from heaven, as though a spirit were borne along with great force, and all were filled with the Holy Spirit, who gave diversities of tongues (Acts 2:1, 3). Good is the rush that knows not how to harm and knows how to fill. If anyone, then, wishes to merit this rush of grace coming upon him from heaven, let him too go down with his eyes into the courses of waters. He who has first poured out this rush will merit that one. She went down with her eyes into these channels of waters who in the Gospel watered the feet of the Lord with tears (Luke 7:38), and therefore by the price of her faith she bought health for her soul and body, now no longer flowing with blood, but with spiritual grace.

50. So the prophet David went down; therefore he brought back grace out of sin. He went down into the channels of waters, that is, he filled them, and heaped up the streams of the running waters with his tears, or filled empty and void channels with his weeping alone. Or, since it shows certain meanings of moral expression, "he went down into the channels of waters" means he passed through them. And we could say "he passed over them" and "he stepped beyond them": but then the force of the wording is lessened, by which a greater force is expressed in an abundance that descends than in one that ascends. See, I ask you, what words take from usage; let prophetic speech not lose its rush, although usage itself has been accustomed to serve the sense of the writers with greater beauty.

51. A well of living waters; for a well, if you draw nothing from it, is easily corrupted by sluggish idleness and degenerate neglect; but when it is worked, it grows bright to the sight and sweet to drink. So too a heap of riches, sandy as a mere pile, is beautiful in use, but in idleness is held useless. Draw off something, then, from this well: For water quenches a burning fire, and almsgiving resists sins (Sir. 3:33). But standing water soon breeds worms: let not your treasure stand still, lest your fire stand. It will stand in you, unless you turn it aside by the works of your mercy.

52. (Verse 16.) Arise, north wind, and come, south wind, breathe upon my garden, and let my spices flow forth. You have heard, bride of Christ, that the beauty of your virtues pleases him; you have heard that he has preferred the spices of your garments, that is, the good fragrance of integrity, to all fragrances. You have heard that you are an enclosed garden, filled with the fruits of sweet fruit-trees. Ask, then, that the Holy Spirit may breathe upon you, breathe upon your bed, and heap up the fragrance of a pious mind and of spiritual grace. Rouse the Holy Spirit, saying: Arise, north wind, and come, south wind; breathe upon my garden, and let my spices flow forth. Let my brother come down into his garden, and eat the fruit of his fruit-trees. The garden of the Word is the affection of a soul in springtime bloom, and in the fruit-trees is the fruit of virtue. He comes, then; and whether you eat or drink, if you call upon Christ, he is present, saying: Come, eat my loaves, and drink my wine (Prov. 9:5).

53. Arise, she says, north wind, and come, south wind; breathe upon my garden, and let my spices flow forth. For from all parts of the world the fragrance of sacred religion has grown, with which the members of the beloved virgin were fragrant. Hence the Church too, keeping the height of the heavenly mysteries, drives away from herself the storms of the heavier wind, and invites the sweetness of springtime grace; and knowing that his garden cannot displease Christ, she calls her Bridegroom: Arise, she says, north wind, and come, south wind. Many take these words as though the north wind were cast away and the south wind invited. If they take them so, the icy harshness of unbelief is driven out from the Church, lest our flight be in winter or on the sabbath, and the springlike mildness of the south is invited.

54. Or surely: Arise, north wind, that is, rise, you who sleep, and arise from the dead (Eph. 5:14). People of the nations, you who slept long before, awake at last, and Christ will shine upon you. Finally, all are invited to the Church, both the people of the Synagogue and that of the Gentiles; but the Synagogue first, because the apostles from among the Jews believed first, and through them afterward the peoples of the nations were gathered. See, then, our sun coming toward the south, and afterward circling toward the north. Jerusalem, Jerusalem: he came indeed to her whom he even deigned to call; but this Jerusalem is earthly, which kills the prophets, that is, the Synagogue of the Jews: How often, he says, would I have gathered your children, as a hen her chicks, and you would not? Behold, your house will be left deserted (Matt. 23:37 and 38). He circled, then, toward the Gentiles; and in circling the Spirit of God circled, and turned back upon his circuits, so that God might be all in all.

Chapter V

1. (Verse 1.) Let my beloved come into my garden, and eat the fruit of his apple trees. From here Plato composed for himself that garden which in one place he called the garden of Jupiter, in another the garden of the mind; for he called Jupiter both God and mind. Into this garden, he said, the soul, which he names Venus, had entered, so that she might fill herself with the abundance and riches of the garden, in which, filled with drink, he might lie, with drink that poured forth nectar. This, then, he composed from the book of the Song of Songs, because the soul cleaving to God has entered into the garden of the mind, in which there was an abundance of diverse virtues and the flowers of discourse. But who does not know that it was from that paradise which, as we read in Genesis, had the tree of life and the tree of the knowledge of good and evil (Gen. 2:8), and the other trees, that he thought the abundance of virtues should be transferred and planted in the garden of the mind?

2. In the Song of Songs he signifies by this garden the garden of the soul, or the soul itself; for so it is written: An enclosed garden is the bride, my sister, an enclosed garden, a sealed fountain. And below the soul says: Arise, north wind, and come, south wind; blow through my garden, and let your ointments flow down; let my brother come down into his garden (Song 4:12 and 13). How much more beautiful is this: that the soul, adorned with the flowers of the virtues, is a garden, or has within herself a budding paradise, into which garden she invites the Word of God to come down, so that that soul, watered by the shower of the heavenly Word and by its abundance, may bear fruit! Now the Word of God is fed by the virtues of the soul whenever he finds the soul obedient to him and rich: he plucks her fruits and delights in them. And when the Word of God has come down into her, the ointments of healthful words flow down from her, and the breaths of diverse graces, giving forth their scent, are fragrant far and wide. Hence the Bridegroom says (now the Bridegroom of the soul is God the Word, to whom the soul is joined by a kind of lawful covenant of marriage):

3. (Verse 2.) I have entered into my garden, my sister, bride; I have gathered the vintage of my myrrh with my ointments, I have eaten my bread with my honey, I have drunk my wine with my milk. Eat, my nearest ones, and drink and be drunk, my brothers. I sleep, and my heart keeps watch. Let us learn what fruits or foods God feasts on, or in what he delights. He delights in this: if anyone mortifies his sin, blots out his guilt, buries and abolishes his iniquities; for myrrh is the burial of the dead, and dead are the sins which cannot have the sweetness of life. Certain wounds of transgressions are bathed with the ointments of divine speech, and are healed by the stronger food of the word as by bread, and by sweeter speech as by honey. That words are food, Solomon also teaches elsewhere, saying: Good words are honeycombs of honey (Prov. 16:24). In that garden, then, there are good words: one that restrains guilt, another that rebukes iniquity, another that makes insolence die and, as it were, buries it, when someone, rebuked, renounces his errors.

4. There is also a stronger word, which strengthens the heart of man with the more robust nourishment of heavenly Scripture. I have eaten the honeycomb with my honey, I have drunk my wine with my milk. For there is a persuasive word, sweet as honey, and yet pricking the conscience of the sinner in its very sweetness. There is also a word of more fervent spirit, which makes drunk like wine and gladdens the heart of man. There is, finally, a milky word, pure and white. These foods of sweet and useful words the Bridegroom tells his nearest ones to feast on: Eat, my nearest ones, and drink, and be drunk, my brothers. Now his nearest ones are those who follow him and are present at his wedding. Filled with this food and drink, and made drunk (for each one drinks (Prov. 5:15) water from his own vessels and from the springs of his own wells), the soul slept to the world and kept watch for God. And therefore, as what follows teaches, God the Word asked that her door be opened to him, so that he might fill her with his entrance.

5. From here, then, were taken over those Platonic banqueters, from here that nectar made of the prophetic wine and honey, from here that sleep, from here that everlasting life on which he said his gods feast; because Christ is life. And therefore the belly of her soul was filled with the seeds of such words, and she herself went out in the Word: and the soul that goes out from this servitude and lifts herself up from the body follows the Word. In another way, to repeat a little of what was said above (above, ch. 4): An enclosed garden is my sister, bride, an enclosed garden, a sealed fountain. By this it signifies that the mystery ought to remain sealed with you, so that it be not violated by the works of an evil life and by adultery against chastity; that it be not divulged to those for whom it is not fitting; that it be not scattered among the faithless by chattering talkativeness.

6. Good, then, must be the guarding of your faith, so that the unstained integrity of life and of silence may persevere. Hence also the Church, keeping the depth of the heavenly mysteries, drives back from herself the heavier storms of the wind and invites the sweetness of springtime grace. And knowing that her garden cannot displease Christ, she calls the Bridegroom himself, saying: Arise, north wind, and come, south wind: blow through my garden, and let my ointments flow (Song 4:16): let my brother come down into his garden, and eat the fruit of his fruit-bearing trees (Song 5:1); for she has good and fruitful trees, and her roots, which she had dipped in the watering of the sacred font, and which have sprung up into good fruits with the bud of a new fruitfulness, so that they are no longer cut down by the prophetic axe but are made fruitful with the abundance of the Gospel. Finally the Lord, delighted by their fertility, answers:

7. I have entered into my garden, my sister, bride: I have gathered the vintage of myrrh with my ointments, I have eaten my bread with my honey: I have drunk my drink with my milk. Why I have said bread and wine, the faithful understand. But this is not doubtful: that he himself eats and drinks in us, just as you have read that in us he said he was in prison (Matt. 25:36). Hence also the Church, seeing so great a grace, exhorts her sons, exhorts her nearest ones, to run together to the sacraments, saying: Eat, my nearest ones, and drink, and be drunk, my brothers. What we are to eat, what we are to drink, the Holy Spirit has expressed to you elsewhere through the prophet, saying: Taste, and see that the Lord is sweet; blessed is the man who hopes in him (Ps. 33 (34):9). In that sacrament is Christ, because it is the body of Christ: it is therefore not bodily food but spiritual. Hence the Apostle too says of its type: That our fathers ate spiritual food and drank spiritual drink (1 Cor. 10:3). For the body of God is a spiritual body; the body of Christ is the body of the divine Spirit; for Christ is spirit, as we read: The Spirit before our face is Christ the Lord (Lam. 4:20). And in the epistle of Peter we have: Christ died for us (1 Pet. 2:21). Finally, this food strengthens our heart, and this drink gladdens the heart of man, as the prophet has recalled (Ps. 103 (104):15).

8. An enclosed garden is my sister, bride, an enclosed garden, a sealed fountain: so that she may not easily open her mouth, nor unseal it for common talk; for it is not fitting, even concerning divine things themselves, unless you answer when you are addressed by the word of God. What have you to do with others? Speak to Christ alone, converse with Christ alone. For if it is written that women should keep silence in the Church (1 Cor. 14:34), how much more unfitting is it for a virgin's door to stand open, how unfitting for a widow's doors to stand open! Quickly does one who lies in wait for modesty creep in, quickly does he catch a word which you would wish to call back. If Eve's door had been shut, Adam would not have been deceived, nor would she, when questioned, have answered the serpent. Death entered through the window, that is, through Eve's door. Death enters through your door if you speak falsely, if shamefully, if wantonly, lastly, if you speak where you ought not. Let the doors of your lips, then, be shut, and let the porch of your voice remain barred, to be unbarred perhaps only when you have heard the voice of God, when you have heard the word of God. Then myrrh will drip for you, then the grace of baptism will breathe upon you, so that you may die with Christ to the elements of the world and rise again with Christ. Why, he says, as though still living, do you make decrees about this world? Do not touch, do not handle, do not taste, things which tend to corruption by their very use (Col. 2:20 ff.).

9. Let my brother come down into his garden, that he may eat the fruit of his fruit-bearing trees. What are these fruit-bearing trees? In Adam you were made a dry tree, but now through the grace of Christ you have sprouted as a fruit-bearing tree. The Lord Jesus accepted it willingly, and with heavenly condescension answered his Church: I have come down, he says, into my garden, I have gathered the vintage of myrrh with my ointments, I have eaten my bread with my honey, and I have drunk my wine with my milk.

10. Eat, he says, my brothers, and be drunk: I have gathered the vintage of myrrh with my ointments (Ps. 79 (80):9). What is this vintage? Know the vineyard, and you will recognize the vintage. A vineyard, he says, you have transplanted out of Egypt, that is, the people of God. You are the vineyard, you are the vintage: as a vineyard you were planted, as a vintage you have given fruit. I have gathered the vintage of myrrh with my ointments, that is, into the fragrance which you have received.

11. I have eaten my bread with my honey. You see that in this bread there is no bitterness, but all is sweetness. I have drunk my wine with my milk: do you see that such is the gladness that is polluted by the defilement of no sin? For as often as you drink, you receive the remission of sins and are made drunk with the spirit. Hence the Apostle too says: Do not be drunk with wine..... but be filled with the Holy Spirit (Eph. 5:18). For he who is made drunk with wine sways and staggers; he who is made drunk with the spirit is rooted in Christ. And therefore it is a glorious drunkenness, which works sobriety of mind.

12. Eat, he says, my friends, and drink, and be drunk. Good is the drunkenness which produces a certain going out of the mind toward better and joyful things, so that our spirit, unmindful of cares, is cheered by the wine of joy. Good is the drunkenness of the spiritual table; finally, the cup that makes drunk, how glorious it is (Ps. 22 (23):5)! And elsewhere you have: Make its streams drunk, multiply its generations (Ps. 64 (65):11); because the drunkenness of the earth, when it has been drenched with heavenly rain, is wont to stir up the seeds and multiply the fruits. And so, when the Word, which comes down from heaven like rain, has made drunk with divine preaching the veins of our earth, that is, of the soul and the mind, zeal for diverse virtues is roused, and the fruit of faith and of chaste devotion grows up, and rightly is it said to him: You have visited the earth, and made it drunk (Ps. 64 (65):10). For by taking up a body he visited it, to heal the sick; he made it drunk with spiritual grace, to soothe the anxious with joy.

13. I sleep, and my heart keeps watch. The sleep of the saints is a working sleep too, according to what is written: I sleep, and my heart keeps watch. And so holy Jacob, sleeping, saw divine mysteries which waking he had not seen: from heaven to earth an air that opened the way for the saints, the Lord looking down, and promising the possession of his land. And so, sleeping a brief sleep, he obtained what he afterwards acquired with great labor; for his inheritance is the sleep of the saints, free from all pleasures of the body, from every disturbance of the mind, bringing tranquillity to the mind and calm to the soul, so that, as though loosed from the bond of the body, she may relieve herself and cleave to Christ. This is the sleep of the life of the saints.

14. The voice of my beloved knocking: open to me, my sister. Even if you sleep, if only Christ knows the devotion of your soul, he comes and knocks at its door and says: Open to me, my sister. Rightly sister, because the marriage of the Word and the soul is spiritual; for souls do not know the covenants of wedlock and the practice of bodily union, but are like the angels of God in heaven. Open, he says, to me, but shut to strangers. Shut to the age, shut to the world. Do not go out yourself to those material things, and do not leave your own light and seek another's; for material light pours in a dark mist, so that the light of true glory is not seen. Open, then, to me: do not be narrowed, but enlarge yourself, and I will fill you. And because, having gone round the circle of the lands, I found more trouble and offence, and did not easily have where to rest, therefore do you open, that in you the Son of Man may lay his head, for whom there is no rest except upon the humble and meek.

15. Open to me, my sister, arise, my nearest one, my dove, my perfect one. Nearest by love, a dove by simplicity, perfect by virtue. For my head is full of dew; for as the dew of heaven takes away the droughts of the night, so the dew of our Lord Jesus Christ has dropped the moisture of eternal life upon the darkness of the night and of the world. This is the head which could not be dried up by the heat of the world; hence he says: For if they do these things in the moist, what will they do in the dry (Luke 23:31)? This head, then, drops dew for others and abounds for itself. And rightly does the head of Christ abound; for your head is Christ, who is always full, and is neither drained by his own generosity nor fails through long-continued largesse. Upon this head no iron comes up, which is the instrument of war, the badge of discord. Now look, and see what that dew is like: namely, of no common moisture.

16. But my locks are of the drops of the night. Do not, my nearest one, take these locks to be of bodily hair: those are not adornments but faults, the enticements of beauty, not the precepts of virtue. The Nazirite has other locks, upon which no iron comes up, which no one cuts off: locks which, not arranged with curling irons or set in order by art, but curling with the manifold grace of shining virtues, have shone out. Learn from the history what kind of locks the Nazirite has: as long as Samson kept them untouched, he could never be conquered. He lost his locks, and he lost the merit of his power (Judg. 16:17).

17. (Verse 3.) Having heard, then, the voice of the Word, do not ask how you may put on the tunic which you had put off at night; for it is shown, and often offered, through spiritual wickedness: how, I say, you may put it on. Forget it and do not know it. As though the Lord were already standing by, rise up troubled, free from bodily bonds; prepare your inner mind with prayers while you rise, so that from lowly things you may strive toward things above, and be eager to open the doors of your heart. While you stretch out your hands to Christ, your deeds will breathe forth a faithful fragrance.

18. Open to me, my sister. When you desire to meet one of the leading men, do you not first approach his house, then seek to be informed and instructed so as to learn the mind of the master of the household, and then implore that you may enter his house, lest anyone cast you off and shut you out? Knock, then, you too, at that heavenly palace; knock not with the hand of the body, but with a kind of right hand of your prayer. Not only the hand of the body knocks; the voice knocks too; for it is written: The voice of my brother knocks at the door. We knock with the finger too. Thomas, indeed, was found worthy to open the door of the resurrection with his finger; and to you too Jesus says: Put your finger here, and put it into my hands and my side, and do not be unbelieving, but faithful (John 20:27). Knock, then, with the finger if you cannot with the whole hand; knock at the door. Christ is the door, who says: If anyone enters through me, he will be saved (John 10:9).

19. When, then, you have knocked at this door, see how you enter, lest perhaps, even having entered, you be out of the king's sight. Many enter palaces and do not at once see this earthly king, but wait upon him often, that at some time they may be found worthy to see him. Nor do they presume upon an opportunity of seeing him, but are presented at his command and pour out their petition, so that they may be received with goodwill, careful about the first entrances of their speech, lest anything falter, lest anything give offence: how much more must God be asked that our prayer may enter the door of his mercy!

20. The voice of my brother knocks at the door. Learn also, O bride of Christ, to bar your door in the hours of night, lest anyone easily find it open. The Bridegroom himself wants it shut when he knocks. Our door is our mouth: it ought to be opened almost to Christ alone, and let it not be opened unless the Word of God has first knocked. The voice of my brother knocks at the door. Hear him knocking, hear him who desires to enter.

21. (Verse 2.) Open to me, my sister, bride, my dove, my perfect one; for my head is full of dew, and my hair of the drops of the night. Consider when God the Word knocks most at your door: when his head is filled with the dew of night. For he deigns to visit those who are set in tribulation and temptations, lest anyone perhaps give way, overcome by hardships. His head, then, is filled with dew or drops when his body labors. Then, therefore, we must keep watch, lest, when the Bridegroom comes, he withdraw shut out. For if you sleep and your heart does not keep watch, he departs before he knocks; if your heart keeps watch, he knocks and asks that the door be opened to him.

22. We have, then, a door of our soul; we have gates too, of which it is said: Lift up your gates, O princes, and be lifted up, eternal gates, and the King of glory shall enter (Ps. 23 (24):7). There is, then, a heaven too in those in whom there are eternal gates. If you will lift up these gates of your faith, the King of glory will enter in to you, bearing the triumph of his own passion. For justice too has gates; for of these also we read it written, the Lord Jesus saying through his prophet: Open to me the gates of justice (Ps. 117 (118):19). And below the prophet David says: Praise the Lord, Jerusalem, praise your God, Zion; for he has strengthened the bars of your gates (Ps. 147:12).

23. There is, then, a soul that has a door, there is one that has gates. To this door Christ comes and knocks; he knocks at the gates too. Open, then, to him: he wants to enter, he wants to find the Bride keeping watch. Do not, then, make the good lover wait: he quickly withdraws, and you will seem by the sleep of your body to have shut out him who knocks. For you shut him out when you are slothful, when lazy, when drowsy. By these bars Christ is shut out: if you are chaste, if you are sober, take care that you are not negligent. He does Christ a greater injury who drives him back as he comes.

24. Open to me, my sister, my nearest one: he knocks at the door even when you are asleep; if only you either wake up when roused, or open the door of your breast when called, he will enter. But if you flee the reading of the prophets, if you do not read at home, if you will not listen in church, are you not like one who, with gaze turned away, shuts his eyes so as not to see what he could see, who closes his eyes so as not to look, though he has the power of seeing? Or as many in a fury have laid hands upon their own eyes; so you too at first turn away, by a pretense that shuts its eyes rather than by an abrupt refusal. For when you come to church and declare yourself to be a Christian, you seem sound, you open the eyes with which you can see; but while you pretend not to hear what is read, you close them so as not to see for yourself, even though you seem to others to see. You also lay certain hands of faithlessness and intemperance upon the eyes of your soul, and bring upon your heart a blindness (which is more grievous) that is voluntary, so that seeing you do not see, and hearing you do not hear.

25. Do you think you are alone when you commit fornication, and do you not remember that the eyes of the Lord see the whole earth? Do you not hear him who says: Behold, the hour comes when you will be scattered, each to his own, and will leave me alone: but I am not alone, because the Father is with me (John 16:32). The Father, then, is present, the Son of God is present too, the ministers are present, the Cherubim and Seraphim are present, who say. Holy, holy, holy, the earth is full of your majesty (Isa. 6). For the world is full of powers, because it is full of wickednesses: the whole earth is full of remedies, because it is full of snares.

26. (Verse 3.) I have put off my tunic; how shall I put it on? For in this night of the world the garment of bodily life must first be put off by you; for the Lord put off the flesh from himself, so that for you he might triumph over the dominions and powers of this world. How shall I put it on? See, O soul devoted to God, what she says. So has she put off the acts of the body and earthly ways that she does not know how she could put them on again, even if she wished. How shall I put it on? That is, with what shame, with what modesty, lastly with what memory? For the habit of good has lost the practice of the old depravity.

27. The soul, hearing Open to me, having a head full of dew, that is, of worldly temptations, was suddenly troubled, and as though about to rise, she who was bidden to rise, she says, since she was fragrant with aloes and myrrh, the badge of burial: I have put off my tunic; how shall I put it on: I have washed my feet; how shall I soil them? For she fears lest she rise again into temptations, lest she return again to guilt and sin, and begin to soil her goings-out and her progress in the virtues with earthly footsteps.

28. Certainly in this way she also shows the perfection of her virtue, which has merited so great a love of Christ that he comes to her and knocks at her door, and comes with the Father, and sups with that same soul, and she with him, as John the apostle said in the Apocalypse (Rev. 3:20). For when she had heard above: Be present here from Lebanon, and perceived that she could not be present to Christ in the flesh, but would be present then if she were present in the spirit, she conformed herself to his will, so that she too might be conformed to the image of Christ; she no longer feels the cast-off skin of the flesh; now, as though a spirit, stripping herself of union with the body, now as though forgetful, and as one who, even if she wished, could not remember that union, she says: I have put off my tunic; how shall I put it on? For I have put off that tunic of skin which Adam and Eve received after their fault, the tunic of corruption, the tunic of passions: How shall I put it on? She does not seek to put it on, but signifies that it has been so cast away that it could no longer be a garment for her.

29. (Verse 3.) I have washed my feet; how shall I soil them? That is, I washed my footsteps while I was going out and lifting myself up from my dwelling together with the body, from that connection and familiarity of carnal embrace: how shall I soil them, so as to return into the enclosure of the body and that dark prison of its passions? So we bury the passions, if we do not undo the cross of this body; if we do not write again the bond of sin which has been blotted out on the cross of Christ; if we do not put on the garment of the old man which we have put off. For it is written in the Song of Songs: I have put off my tunic; how shall I put it on? I have washed my feet; how shall I soil them? The members of the body, then, have been mortified: why do its vices sprout? Therefore the Law did not prevail, because it did not mortify the flesh; therefore it passed away like a shadow, because it did not discolour; and therefore it overshadowed us from the sun of justice, because it heaped up crimes.

30. Sometimes to be naked is a sign of virtue, for it is written: I have put off my tunic; how shall I put it on? I have washed my feet; how shall I soil them? What tunic, then, she put off, and did not find how she might put it on, let us reveal from the Scriptures, if we can. For there is a certain bodily tunic; there are certain coverings woven of desires; and therefore it is sometimes better to be naked in body than veiled in heart. Hence Paul too admonishes us that to be stripped is much better than to be clothed over, saying elsewhere: Stripping off the old man with his deeds, put on the new, who is renewed unto knowledge, according to the image of him who created him. She, then, who has been stripped and has her feet washed does not know how she could not be soiled; for by grace she forgets what she had drawn in by nature.

31. I have washed my feet; how shall I soil them? You have learned in the Gospel that to wash the feet is a mystery of faith, a mark of humility, according to what is written: If I, the Lord and master, have washed your feet, how much more ought you to wash one another's feet (John 13:14)! But this pertains to humility. As regards the mystery, however, he who wishes to have a part with Christ ought to wash his feet: For if I do not wash, he says, your feet, you will have no part with me (ibid. 8). When this is said to Peter, what is judged of us? He, then, who has washed his feet has no need to wash them again; and therefore let him take care not to soil them.

32. And rightly does holy Church say: I have washed my feet; she does not say, how shall I wash them again, but how shall I soil them again? As though forgetful of the old stain, forgetful of the contagion, she admonishes us how in a bodily ministry we ought to wash away the spiritual footprint of our actions. And so, when once you have washed your feet in the watering of the eternal fountain and cleansed them by the sacrament of the mystery, beware again the filth of bodily desire, lest your muddy acts be soiled with earthly defilements. These are the feet which David washed in the spirit, who teaches you how you may be unable to soil them, saying: Our feet were standing in your courts, Jerusalem (Ps. 121 (122):2). Here, certainly, understand not the feet of the body, but of the mind; for how would an earthly man have the feet of his body in heaven? Jerusalem, as Paul has taught you, is in heaven; and he too has taught you how you can stand in heaven, when he says: But our way of life is in heaven (Phil. 3:20). A way of life in conduct, a way of life in deeds, a way of life in faith.

33. I have done judgment and justice; do not hand me over to those who slander me. Where there is justice, there is mercy. Mercy frees from sin. How then am I handed over to sinners? Like this is that saying in the Song of Songs: I have put off my tunic; how shall I put it on? I have washed my feet; how shall I soil them? I have put off the tunic of the sinner and the covering of the earthly man; why am I judged as a sinner and an earthly man? I have washed my feet, so that no lot of sin might cling to my footstep; why do you give power over me to those who transgress?

34. Jeremiah says in his Lamentations: Your iniquity has come to an end, daughter of Zion; he will not again drive you out: he has visited your iniquities, daughter of Edom, he has laid bare your sins (Lam. 4:22). Do you notice that iniquity could not come to an end without the visitation of God, nor correction be complete except through the grace of the Lord the Savior? But how iniquity comes to an end, hear the Church saying: I have put off my tunic; how shall I put it on? I have washed my feet; how shall I soil them? The garment of the old man, then, woven with the vices of error and laid aside in the washing of regeneration, she does not know how she could put on; for through zeal for correction forgetfulness of sins had grown into her. So great is the force of a completed amendment that she returns to a kind of age of spiritual childhood, which does not know the ways of error; even if she wished, she could not commit a crime, because she has lost the habit of knowing how to sin.

35. (Verse 4.) My brother put his hand in from the opening, and my belly was troubled over him. I rose up to open to my brother. My hands dripped myrrh, my fingers were full of myrrh. First, then, he puts in his hand as from the opening, when God is judged from his works; hence he says: If you do not believe me, believe the works (John 10:38). Then love increases, and, once conceived, grows deep within the inmost bowels. Then, his seeds having been poured into the intelligible womb in which is the receptacle of the Word, our soul desires to draw in his fullness dwelling bodily; for the womb of women is not troubled, except of those who are heavy with child. The soul, then, rises to open to the Word of God; but while she spreads herself out and opens, so that by receiving the Word she may mortify the works of this world, like him who said: We carry about in our body the mortification of the Lord Jesus (2 Cor. 4:10). So, while she opens, the Bridegroom passes; for he wishes always to be sought, and to be found more often. And if he finds the door shut, he knocks; and if through delay he has been shut out, he withdraws; but he quickly returns and knocks again, so that at least afterwards he may find his Bride prepared.

36. It can indeed also be taken in this way, My brother passed: just as we read that he penetrated the inmost marrow of the beloved, as it was said to Mary: And a sword shall pass through your own soul, that the thoughts of many hearts may be revealed (Luke 2:35). Then the Bride added that her soul went out in his word, which happens when the soul is a pilgrim away from the body and is present to God. Hearing this, the Bridegroom sent his working, the Word, as through a cleft, not yet face to face: he sent it as a hand. And my belly, she says, was troubled over him. Whoever lives so can say:

37. My brother put his hand in through the opening, and my belly was troubled toward him: I rose to open to my brother. It is good that our inward parts be troubled at the coming of the Lord. If Mary was troubled at the coming of the angel, how much more are we troubled at the coming of Christ! For as divine things flow in, bodily affection becomes a stranger, and that habit of the outer man fades away. You too, be troubled; you too, make haste. They are commanded to eat the lamb in haste. Rise, open: Christ is at the door, he knocks at the porch of your house: if you open, he will enter, and he will enter with the Father. Not only when he has entered does he leave a reward, but even before he enters he sends a reward ahead. Still the soul is troubled, still she gropes along the walls of her house, still she seeks the door where Christ is, still she loosens the bond of the flesh and the enclosure of the body, still Christ knocks outside.

38. What is our window, if not that through which we see the works of Christ, namely the eye of the soul and the gaze of the mind? And therefore, virgin, let Christ come in through your window, let Christ put in his hand: through your window let the love of the Word come to you, not the love of the body. If, then, the Word of God puts his hand in through your window, see how you prepare your windows, see how you must wipe them clean of all the dust of transgressions. Let the windows of a virgin have nothing foul, nothing adulterous. Away with antimony and the other trifles of affected beauty, away with the allurements of adulterous love. Like this is the closing of the ears, from which no weights are to be hung, in which no wounds are to be pierced, but which have one adornment: to hear what profits.

39. (Verse 6.) I opened, she says, to my brother; my brother passed. How he passed: that is, he penetrated the inner parts of the mind, as was said to Mary: And a sword shall pass through your own soul. For the living word of God, like a sharp sword, penetrating the bars of bodily thoughts, searches out the inner parts of the heart. Placed, then, on your bed and in the time of night, meditate always on Christ, and hope for his coming at every moment. If he seems to you to delay, rise up. He seems to delay when you sleep long; he seems to delay when you are idle from prayer; he seems to delay when you do not lift up your voice in psalms. Dedicate to Christ the firstfruits of your vigils; offer to Christ the firstfruits of your actions.

40. (Verse 5.) I rose to open to my brother; my hands dripped myrrh, my fingers were full of myrrh upon the handles of the bolt. Let us consider what this signifies. First, as I have said, God the Word is seen in his works as through a cleft, not fully and perfectly; then love increases and what was conceived grows up, and from his seeds, which the soul has received in a kind of intelligible womb, she desires to see the whole fullness of his divinity dwelling in him bodily, as we read. She rose so that she might see that Word of God more nearly, and in this very thing her advance is signified, that she rose through vigor and power; for the presence of the Word drew in the power of the soul, just as the presence of Mary, when she was heavy with child, instructed John, who was placed in the womb, so that he leapt forth from the womb and exulted, recognizing the presence of the Lord.

41. She rose to open, and her works and deeds were mortified to the world. For such must be the soul that is to receive the Word: she must die to the world and be buried together in Christ; for so is Christ found, and such a lodging does he seek for himself. Then the very instruments of her workings, that is, the hands and fingers by which Christ is grasped, are mortified: and these fingers we can consider as the outstanding works of our deeds. And so, when, as if from her embrace, she was already stretching out her intelligible hands and fingers to grasp the Word, the devout soul says that it had passed her, but had not yet passed through her. And this is the progress there is when the Word of God passes and passes through the soul, for it is written: And a sword shall pass through your own soul, that the thoughts of many hearts may be revealed (Luke 2:35). Here, then, there is still a passing, not a passing through; so that, as in Mary, there is perhaps a passing through in what follows, where the Lord Jesus is set as a seal in her midst.

42. Then at once there is another progress of the Word passing through, because the soul went out in his Word: that is, following his Word, she went out from the body, lifting herself from its dwelling and making herself a stranger to it, so that she might be present to God and be a citizen with the saints. For we cannot be at once members of the household of the flesh and of God. In this place, then, as I have said, the soul is signified to go out when she withdraws herself from the pleasures of the body. Accordingly it is written: Go out from Babylon, fleeing from the Chaldeans (Isa. 48:20). Not, certainly, that the Hebrew should flee the regions of Babylon, but its ways, as he is admonished by the prophet's address; since there are Hebrews who are in Babylon and yet show by their ways that they have gone out of Babylon. For those too of whom the prophet says that they sat upon the rivers of Babylon (Ps. 136 (137):1) sat indeed in the region of Babylon, but were not in its vices and confusion. For how were they in that confusion of vices, who wept, doing penance because they had fallen from the ark of devotion and faith and from the merits of their fathers' virtue? But the soul that goes out in the word seeks the Word; hence she too, while she was seeking, fell in with the watchmen who go about the city.

43. (Verse 7.) The watchmen who go about the city found me, they struck me and wounded me, and the watchmen of the walls took away my cloak from me. Rightly indeed she came as a bride with a cloak, with which she might veil her head when the bridegroom met her; like Rebecca, who, on learning that Isaac was coming to meet her, got down from the camel and covered herself with her cloak: so this soul too sent ahead the insignia of the wedding garment, lest perhaps she be cast out as one who has no wedding garment; or so that she might veil her head because of the angels. But they struck her, so that she might be tested further; for souls are exercised by temptations. They took away her cloak, inquiring whether she brought the true beauty of naked virtue; or because one ought to enter that heavenly city without a covering, bearing with oneself no coverings of paint. There are also those who inquire lest any soul carry with her the cast-off spoils of fleshly allurement and bodily concupiscence. She is stripped of her cloak when her conscience is laid open. But there is one who is well stripped, who may imitate him who says: For the prince of this world comes, and in me he finds nothing (John 14:30); because surely in him alone did he find nothing, who did not commit sin.

44. Blessed too is she in whom he finds nothing grave or much, but finds in her the garment of faith and the discipline of wisdom. And so without loss to herself (for even if one wishes, he cannot take away true wisdom), even though the adversary clamors against her, yet, where the true integrity of a blameless way of life shines out, she has passed the watchmen, and, joined with the daughters of that heavenly city, she seeks the Word; and by seeking she arouses in herself love for him, and recognizes where she should seek the Word. It is not only an injury not to have found him whom you seek, but often it is also a wound to have sought where one ought not: to have sought in the houses of men who falsely take to themselves the name of teachers, to have sought more boldly than modestly. Let us, then, take care by her example, lest the watchmen who go about the city find us.

45. They found me, she says, the watchmen who go about the city: they struck and wounded me, and the watchmen of the walls took away my cloak from me. The Church is wounded not in herself, daughters, but in us. Let us take care, then, that our fall become not a wound of the Church, that no one take away our cloak, that is, the garment of prudence, the badge of patience, by which the ambition for softer clothing is put off: for those indeed who are clothed in softer garments are in the houses of kings (Matt. 11:8). But to us Christ has given a cloak, with which he himself clothed his apostles and his body; which indeed he bids you give, so that, if anyone asks your tunic of you, you let him have your cloak also (Matt. 5:40): that is, that you hand over the badge of your philosophy, and clothe round, as with the garment of your prudence, him who before was naked.

46. There, then, daughters, let us seek Christ, where the Church seeks him: on the mountains of good fragrance, which, by the loftiness of their exalted deeds, breathe forth the sweet fragrance of life from the summits of their merits. She who seeks Christ must not be common, must not be in the market place, nor in the streets, with a plaintive voice, a slippery step, easy of access, cheap in appearance. The Apostle denies you earthly company, and teaches that you must fly away to heaven on spiritual wings, almost beyond the bounds of nature. Mind, he says, the things that are above, not those upon the earth (Col. 3:2). But because this was impossible for those shut up in this enclosure, as it were, of the body, and because the soul is said to fly back to higher things when bodies have died, being bound while we live by a certain law of our nature, therefore he added: For you are dead, and your life is hidden with Christ in God (ibid. 3). If it is hidden with Christ in God, let it not appear to the world; for Christ died to the world, he lives to God. See now how Christ loves to be desired, does not love idle talk. That virgin opened her doors to the Word of God; but he passed, she says, and my soul went out in his word (Verse 6). She went out from the world, she went out from the age, she remained in Christ. I sought him, she says, and did not find him. For Christ loves to be sought long.

47. The watchmen of the walls found her. Perhaps there are other watchmen too, whom we ought rather to understand. For there is a city which does not have the gates of its walls shut, of which it is said: And its gates shall not be shut by day (Rev. 21:25); for there will be no more night in it; the nations will bring glory and honor into it. That, then, is the city Jerusalem which is in heaven, within which you are kept as though already perfect and spotless; for nothing common enters into it. Chastity is not common, modesty is not common, which is written in the book of life.

48. If, then, we have found the city, let us enter into it, let us see its light, let us see its walls, let us see the foundations of its walls, let us see also the watchmen of the walls. But how shall we enter into it? In this city is life, and there is one way that leads to life. The way, then, is Christ; therefore let us follow Christ. But the city itself is in heaven; how then shall we ascend to heaven? The Evangelist teaches us, who says: And he led me out in the spirit to a great and high mountain, and showed me the holy city Jerusalem coming down from heaven (Rev. 21:10). Let us ascend, then, in spirit, because the flesh cannot ascend to it. Let us meanwhile ascend to heaven, so that afterwards that city may come down to us from heaven, in which the light is like a most precious stone, like a stone of jasper and of crystal; it has a great and high wall.

49. You have learned the light, you have learned the wall; learn the gates, learn the watchmen. It has, he says, twelve gates, and at the gates twelve angels, on which are written the names of the twelve tribes of the sons of Israel (ibid. 12). In the gates are contained the names of the patriarchs, in the wall those of the apostles; for the foundations of the city are the apostles, and the cornerstone is Christ, in whom the whole structure rises. God outside, God within, God is everywhere: For the city, he says, has the majesty of God (ibid. 11). You too, then, holy virgins, and all of you who are just and bear the spotless chastity of the soul, are fellow citizens with the saints and members of the household of God. But you will possess that nobility of your homeland only if you seek Christ within the enclosures of that city, having entered by faith and by precious deeds, made bright by the light of the patriarchs, founded upon the apostles, moving among the angels.

50. How, then, are these watchmen angels, who take away the cloak of a chaste soul? The cloak of virgins is one thing, that of a young girl who roams the market place is another. She who seeks Christ in the market place has lost even the cloak she had. For prudence is possessed not in the market place, not in the streets, but in the Church. And perhaps, so that we may be reconciled with them too, and teach that God is merciful to all, since they too sometimes find Christ, if only they seek him constantly, this cloak is the garment of the body. He, then, who has sought Christ on his bed, if only he has sought him as he did who said: Thus have I remembered you upon my bed (Ps. 62 (63):7); if he has sought in the nights, according to what is written: In the nights lift up your hands to the holy places (Ps. 133 (134):2); if he has sought in the city, in the market place and in the streets, in the city of our God: in the market place perhaps, in which that judge of divine law sits, in the streets from which were gathered those who came together to the Lord's supper; he can, while he seeks long, meet the angels who guard the city of God. Indeed, from the heavenly nature of the watchmen we can understand a heavenly city, a heavenly market place of everlasting justice, not common streets, but perhaps those in which that fountain is wont to overflow, of which it is written: Let the waters of your fountain overflow for you, and let your waters be spread abroad in your streets (Prov. 5:16). He, then, who seeks Christ thus reaches the angels.

51. But if the angels are reached by good merits, why is he who has reached them wounded? There is a good sword too, and the wound of that sword is good. The word of God wounds, but does not make a festering sore. There is a wound of good love, there are wounds of love; and therefore she said: I am wounded by love (Song 2:5). She who is perfect is wounded by love. Good, then, are the wounds of the Word, good are the wounds of one who loves: For more useful are the wounds of a friend than the willing kisses of an enemy (Prov. 27:6). Rebecca is wounded by love, who left her parents and went over to her bridegroom. Rachel is wounded by love, who was jealous of her sister and loved her husband; for she, still barren, envied her sister because she abounded in sons, since she bore the type of the Church, to which it is said: Rejoice, barren one, who do not bear; break forth and cry, you who are not in labor (Isa. 54:1).

52. The watchmen, then, found her and wounded her, and took away her cloak; that is, they removed the wrapping of bodily action, so that the naked simplicity of the mind might seek Christ; because no one clothed in the garment of philosophy, that is, in the habit of worldly wisdom, can see Christ. And rightly is the garment of philosophy taken from her, lest anyone despoil her through philosophy. Rightly is the cloak taken from her who draws near to Christ, so that, being about to see God, she may walk with a clean heart: Blessed indeed are the clean of heart, for they shall see God (Matt. 5:8). And so, when she had cleansed her heart, she found the Word, she saw God. Seek him, then, virgin; nay rather, let us all seek him; for the soul has no sex. But perhaps it received a feminine name for this reason: that the more violent heat of the body drives her, while she herself soothes the impulse of the flesh and of her love with a certain soft and gentle reason. Therefore we ought to invite God with prayers and supplications, that he may deign to breathe upon us like a good south wind, and breathe on us the breeze of the heavenly Word, which is wont not to disturb the fruit-bearing trees with a heavy wind, but to stir them with a gentle breath and a soft blowing.

53. They took my cloak from me. A good garment is the eternity of life: this cloak, of which the first man had been stripped, the watchmen of the walls wished to take away from the Bride; but the soul devoted to God, by holding long him whom she had sought and by not letting go her beloved, clothed herself with the precious wrapping of divine love. Blessed, then, are those who are clothed with such a cloak and have merited a garment of this kind by the observance of the law; because they have not forgotten the law, but have done the things that were of the law.

54. (Verse 8.) I adjure you, daughters of Jerusalem, if you find my beloved, that you tell him that I languish with love. But what is this languor of the soul? The moral lesson of an ancient history, the one concerning holy Moses, makes it our duty to remember: he asked God to show himself to him, so that he might see him face to face. The holy prophet of the Lord certainly knew that he could not see the invisible God face to face. But holy devotion goes beyond measure, and he thought that even this was possible for God: to make what is incorporeal be grasped by bodily eyes. This error is not blameworthy; it is even a pleasing and insatiable desire, in that he longed to hold his Lord as it were in his hand and to see him with the gaze of his eyes. For he knew that man was made to the image and likeness of God; for when, chosen by the Lord God to free the people, he was filled with the spirit of wisdom, he had seen that angel, and his face in glory; then he was terrified by his flashing light, and he saw the bush burning and not burnt up. By his sight he caught the brightness, and wondering at it he drew near, provoked by desire and by grace to consider that brightness in the bush more carefully. And so, if he received such ardor, if he underwent such desire when he had seen the angel in the flame of fire from the bush, that, though seized with fear he did not dare to look closely, yet he wished to look: how much more did he desire to see the face of the Lord bodily, saying within himself: That face, full of light, full of grace, full of power, full of divinity, what shall I say that it is? I cannot say or think more of God; for when a man has finished, then he begins; and when he has ended, then he will be at a loss, as it is written, because the eternal majesty of God is incomprehensible (Sir. 18:6).

55. The result of his petition was indeed vain, but the affection of the servant was commendable; because, advancing by devotion beyond his nature, he reckoned from the angels of the Lord how much more he should desire of the Lord's face, with which he longed to satisfy his desire. He knew that in man himself there would be another glory after death, another brightness: For as star differs from star in brightness, so also is the resurrection of the dead; which, though it is sown in corruption, yet will rise in incorruption, will rise in glory, will rise in power, will rise a spiritual body (1 Cor. 15:41–44). He, then, who could know these things rightly presumed to desire to see the face of the Lord, since he would see it once he had met the death of the body. Certainly he had already taken on such a form, and the Lord already approved him as such, that the heavenly minister and executor of the oracle did not differ from the angels. And therefore, since he knew That the angels of the little ones daily see the face of the Father who is in heaven (Matt. 18:10), he thought that he could and should already see it, as though forgetful of the body and with the flesh laid aside; like him who, while still placed in the body, when he was caught up into paradise, said that he did not know whether he had been caught up to the third heaven in the body or out of the body (2 Cor. 12:3). For the saints, even when they bear a body, are yet not in the flesh but in the spirit; since those who are in carnal desire and way of life cannot please God. But the saints, in their way of life, are not in the flesh, but in heaven.

56. From these things, then, let us grasp the affection of the languishing Bride. As one innocent, she desired to see the face of God, as it were with a certain face of conscience; she longed to open to him the face of her inner mind and to be known more fully. Impatient love, knocking day and night at a harlot's doors, if the desires of possession are put off too long, faints from the very waiting while it hopes: and in this there is surely not the end of love, but its increase. And whatever is desirable, if it does not fall to the one who desires it, he faints toward it, and he who desires lays down, as it were, his very soul; if, however, hope rises nearer, hope close at hand gives strength; but if the beloved is absent, by the very fact that she desires him who is absent, she who has longed for him suffers a fainting of her soul. And so, the farther off that which is desired, the more does he who desires faint. This, then, is to faint: for each one to pass with all his zeal into that which he loves; he thinks of it, cleaves to it, sounds it forth, that which he has taken up to love; into it he is poured over by a certain fainting of the soul. So it is when a mother awaits the presence of her son, as Tobit's wife awaited her son on his travels: fainting from longing and set in distress, she seemed as it were undone (Tob. 10:3 ff.). For what else do her words signify but a certain fainting? But the more the affection is wearied, the more love grows; and the longer he who is desired is away, the more the desires of the one who waits are set on fire by a certain greater force of love. The flesh faints, but desire is fed and increased.

57. And so the holy soul does not know how to desire anything other than the Bridegroom, who is Christ Jesus: she longs for him, she desires him, she strains toward him with all her strength, she cherishes him in the bosom of her mind, she opens and pours herself out to him, and she fears this alone, that she might lose him. And so, the greater the desire with which the soul has been roused, longing to cleave to God her Savior, the more she faints. This fainting, then, brings about a diminishing indeed of frailty, but a taking on of virtue. She longed, she desired, she fainted, she was dissolved with her whole affection, so as to become wholly his whom she desired, as David himself also says: I pour out my prayer in his sight, and I declare my tribulation before him, when my spirit fails from me (Ps. 141 (142):3 and 4). For his spirit failed; nay rather, the spirit fails from him who denies himself so as to cleave to Christ. And so, as though impatient of love, she ran about and sought the Word of God everywhere, because she was wounded, because she was naked; and she sought the help of someone by whom Christ might be asked to come. See her burning, see her desiring: I adjure, she says, you, daughters of Jerusalem, etc.; then, joined with the daughters of that heavenly city, she seeks the Word, and by seeking him and contemplating his beauty she arouses in herself love for him.

58. (Verse 10.) My brother, she says, is white and ruddy, chosen out of thousands. For it is fitting, virgin, that you should know fully him whom you love, and recognize in him the whole mystery both of his innate divinity and of the incorporation he took on. He is rightly white, then, because he is the splendor of the Father; ruddy, because he is the offspring of a virgin. The color of both natures shines and glows in him. Remember, however, that the insignia of divinity in him are more ancient than the sacraments of the body; because he did not begin from the Virgin, but he who already was came into the Virgin.

59. My brother is white and ruddy. White with eternal brightness, ruddy with the appearance of the human color which he took on in the sacrament of the incarnation. And rightly does that very thing which is ruddy smell sweet; because the flesh of Christ is without sin: the faithless, handling it, defiled their hands; the saints, venerating it, became fragrant with the fragrance of piety.

60. Chosen out of thousands. Ten thousands, says Jacob, of Ephraim, and thousands of Manasseh: that is, that he may rule over both Jews and Gentiles, and from both peoples acquire for himself the fullness of the Church. And therefore holy Jacob placed his right hand upon Ephraim, because, as you read, the Church says in the Song of Songs: My brother is white and ruddy, chosen out of ten thousands. Indeed, David too, the ancestor of the maiden Mary, from whose line Christ was born through the Virgin's childbearing, they proclaimed among ten thousands; but Saul among thousands, though they ought rather to have given the first place to the regard due to the king.

61. (Verse 11.) His head is gold of Cephas. This signifies a stable and eminent wisdom. The body of Christ is the Church; its head is like gold, because precious is the wisdom of the saints: that is, just and prudent men.

62. His locks are like fir trees, black as a raven. Of this he said elsewhere: Your hair is like a flock of shorn ewes. Hair, for this reason: because the power of all the senses is in the head: The eyes indeed of the wise man are in his head (Eccles. 2:14). Deep, then, is the prudence of teachers, which can reveal what is obscure and open up the depths of the meanings. And disputants of this kind are the locks of the Church, like the young of ravens to which the Lord gives food: as he gave it to holy Jacob, and fed him from his youth. These, lofty and deep in the richness of their doctrine, the Lord feeds with heavenly sacraments.

63. (Verse 12.) His eyes too are like doves. The eyes are men adorned, namely, with spiritual senses, who are keen to see the mysteries and ready to penetrate the secrets of divine Scripture, shining with rational milk, in whom there is no spotted confusion of guile, but the spotless sincerity of a simple affection. Therefore he recalled these doves as washed in milk in the abundance of waters, saying:

64. His eyes are like doves upon the abundance of waters, washed in milk, of him who sits upon the fullness. The Lord baptizes in milk, that is, in sincerity. And these are the ones who are truly baptized in milk, who believe without guile and bring a pure faith, so that they may put on a spotless grace. Therefore the Bride ascends white to Christ, because she has been baptized in milk; therefore the Powers marvel at her, saying: Who is this who ascends made white (Song 8:5)? A little before she said, I am black (Song 1): now she is seen made white, and she ascends to heaven, and, leaning on the Word of God, she now penetrates the heights.

65. And not without reason is there an abundance of waters there, where Christ is, from which the human mind may desire to be filled. These waters the hart thirsts for, and when it has drunk them it cannot thirst. These waters the Prophet thirsted for when he said: My soul has thirsted for you (Ps. 41 (42):3). Christ, then, sits upon the abundance of waters and upon the fullness; and therefore he who baptizes in water says: And we have all received of his fullness (John 1:16). Hence too "the eye of punishments" is not foreign to the Church; because, though John baptized in Aenon, he baptized near Salim, where there was an abundance of waters, and twelve fountains, and seventy palm trees. The Church has these fountains: that is, in the Old Testament the twelve patriarchs, in the New the twelve apostles; and therefore it is said: In the churches bless the Lord God from the fountains of Israel (Ps. 67 (68):27). Whoever attains the most holy mysteries is first bathed in these fountains; for these fountains, flowing from the eternal fountain, have streamed over the whole world. Where these fountains are, there is the ascent of souls. Indeed, they have interpreted Salim as "he himself ascending"; for he truly ascends who lays aside his own sins. By this word, then, the practice of purifying sanctification is expressed.

66. (Verse 13.) His cheeks are like bowls of spice, bringing forth perfumes. We now understand the cheeks and the rest to be the virtues of the soul and the diversities of teachers: who either supply spiritual nourishment to the mind by diligent dispensing, or by the preaching of the Lord's cross bind the hearer as with a kind of line of the Word, or are bashful in their modesty and most pleasing in the flower of youth. Even if they are held back from touch by shame, yet let the fragrance of Christ breathe in them; and as the ointment comes down upon the cheeks as from the priestly head, so in them too let the beauty of doctrine shine forth.

67. His lips dripping full myrrh. For by myrrh are declared the ointment of the passion and the grace of the resurrection, which pours a revived fragrance of life into the inmost parts of the dead that are raised. Let the doors of your lips, then, be shut, and let the porch of your voice remain barred, to be unbarred perhaps only when you have heard the Word of God. Then myrrh will drip for you, then the grace of baptism will breathe upon you; so that you may die together to the elements of the world, and rise again with Christ.

68. His lips dripping full myrrh. But we think ourselves idle if we seem to devote ourselves only to the word; and we esteem those who work more highly than those who practice the study of knowing the truth; for many say: Look at the man and his works: as though he who devotes himself to the word does not work, when this is a work more than the rest are. For if justice is a work, if temperance is a work, if fortitude is a work, surely wisdom too is a work; for these are held to be the four principal virtues. For if Christ works inasmuch as he is justice, surely he works inasmuch as he is the Word. And he was working when he was in the beginning with the Father. Indeed, all things were made through him, so that you may know that he is the worker of all things, and that our work is Christ Jesus. For inasmuch as he is the Word, for those who seek the Word, the Word is a great work. Hence, when Martha was hurrying about the serving, while Mary was listening to the Word of the Lord, she who listened deserved to be preferred to her who served; for to Martha, who said: Lord, do you not care that my sister has left me to serve alone? Tell her, then, to help me, the Lord answered and said: Martha, Martha, Mary has chosen the good part, which shall not be taken away from her (Luke 10:40–42). Thus it is defined by the authority of the divine sentence that to know the Word is a greater work than to serve.

69. But perhaps someone may say that it was said by the Apostle: That the kingdom of God is not in speech, but in power (1 Cor. 4:20). I do not deny that it is written, but I know in what speech: namely, the speech which one who is puffed up has poured out, speech which cannot profit the hearers, which is without the demonstration of the spirit and of power. This speech Paul does not deign to know; for he wishes rather to commend the power of speech. Indeed, the Apostle wished his speech to be such that it came in weakness, so as to make others stronger; in fear and trembling, so that those who feared might fear nothing but the Lord Jesus, and those who trembled might keep his peace and tranquillity. Hear, then, what kind of speech the Apostle had: And my speech, he says, and my preaching were not in the persuasion of the words of human wisdom, but in the demonstration of the spirit and of power (1 Cor. 2:4); because faith is made firm not in the forensic speech of wisdom, but in the demonstration of the spirit, in the power of God.

70. Therefore in the speech of the saints there is power, but in this forensic and philosophical speech there is the vanity of the world. And that there is power in the speech of the saints, let this prophet too teach you, who says: The Lord will give the word to those who preach the Gospel with much power (Ps. 67 (68):12); that is, that they may be able to preach the Gospel with much power. It is proved, then, that there is power in the preaching of the Gospel; and the preaching of the Gospel is the speech of the saints; so no doubt remains that there is power in holy speech. And this is the myrrh dripping from the lips of the Bridegroom: namely, that by which vices are mortified.

71. (Verse 14.) His hands are turned, golden, full of hyacinths. See what hands made man, and what man they made! That man, surely, Whom we have put on according to Christ, stripping off the old man with his deeds, and putting on the new, who is renewed unto knowledge according to the image of him who created him: where there is not slave and free, but Christ is all and in all (Col. 3:9–11). We have put on Christ, then, as it is said elsewhere too: You have put on Christ (Gal. 3:27). We have received the Holy Spirit, who not only forgives our sins, but also makes us his priests to forgive the sins of others. Therefore the Prophet says: You have formed me and laid your hand upon me (Ps. 138 (139):5): he formed me through clay, he laid his hand on me through spiritual grace; although many take this psalm as spoken in the person of the Savior. Hear that the hand of the Lord is also called spirit: Job cries out: The divine Spirit who made me (Job 33:4). These, then, are the hands that prepared man: Christ and the Spirit.

72. Therefore the Lord Jesus is also the author of our body, he who first made man to the image, and afterwards formed him from clay, and willed to save what he had made, to save what he had fashioned, so that he might make the whole man safe. These are the turned hands of God, that is, perfect: golden because of wisdom, who is Christ; full of hyacinths because of the Holy Spirit and the fullness of his gifts of grace.

73. (Verse 14.) His belly is of ivory, studded with sapphires. The Apostle: Even if, he says, he was crucified out of weakness: behold the belly, that is, the weakness of the flesh; yet he lives by the power of God (2 Cor. 13:4): behold the studding of sapphire. For the sapphire, because it bears the appearance of the clear sky, signifies the works of divinity. But ivory [the edition prints "onir", evidently corrupt; the verse and the sense call for ivory], because of its chastity, brightness and lastingness, signifies that flesh of the Savior of which the same Apostle says: That even if we have known Christ according to the flesh, yet now we know him no longer (2 Cor. 5:16).

74. (Verse 15.) His legs are marble columns, founded upon bases of gold. They signify that the Apostles are columns of the Church, who are founded in holy fear. For as Peter, James, John and Barnabas seemed to be columns of the Church, so too whoever has conquered this world becomes a column of God, which he confirms who says: I have confirmed its columns (Ps. 74 (75):4). So too the golden base is fear full of discipline, because it is the beginning of wisdom. In the fear of the wise, then, the apostolic preaching, the column of Christ, is made firm as upon a golden base. Therefore for eloquence and for apostolic speech the tribunal is the fear of the just man, and a golden base full of prudence; and the good statue, as the likeness of truth, is the speech of the saints.

75. And see that the fear of the saints is like a golden base. Read Isaiah: see after how many things he placed fear, so as to make fear blameless and good: The spirit, he says, of wisdom and understanding, the spirit of counsel and of power, the spirit of knowledge and of piety, the spirit of the fear of the Lord (Isa. 11:2). After how many things he has placed fear, so that it might have something it can follow! It is formed through wisdom, instructed through understanding, directed by counsel, strengthened by power, governed by knowledge, adorned by piety. Take these things away from the fear of the Lord, and fear is irrational and foolish, one of those: Fightings without, fears within (2 Cor. 7:5); by which Paul too would have been afflicted, had he not had the Lord to console him.

76. (Verse 15.) His appearance is as of Lebanon, chosen as the cedars. He dwells on high and looks upon lowly things, because his appearance is like the cedar of Lebanon, which thrusts its foliage into the clouds, its root into the earth; for his beginning is from heaven, and his later parts on earth have brought forth fruits close to heaven. This kind of tree never loses its greenness; in winter as in summer it nourishes its foliage, and it is not changed to a different color: this tree alone the wind never strips of its honor; it alone is never stripped of its old garment nor clothed in new flower. So the apostolic grace too knows no failing, and flowers with its own perpetual loveliness. The soul, then, that thrives with flourishing members knows no corruption; she always sustains with patient magnanimity the summits of justice and of the other virtues; and therefore she does not waste away or fail, because there is nothing in her ruinous or slack, nothing unstable, nothing slippery, nothing that could be poured out of her by a fault of speech.

77. (Verse 16.) His throat is most pleasant, and he is wholly desire. The judgments of the Lord are true, justified in themselves, more to be desired than gold and very precious stone (Ps. 18 (19):10 and 11). What is severe in others is sweet in Christ, is pleasant in Christ, because he himself is pleasant: Finally, taste and see that the Lord is pleasant (Ps. 33 (34):9). Sweet are the judgments to one who confesses, because he himself says: I am, I am he who blots out your iniquities, and I will not remember them: but do you remember, and let us be judged. Tell your iniquities, that you may be justified (Isa. 43:25 and 26). Sweet are the judgments to one who does penance, because he himself has said: There will be joy over one sinner doing penance more than over ninety-nine just who need no penance (Luke 15:7). If, then, the judgments of the Lord are sweet, let us labor to gather the fruits of their pleasantness. Do you wish to know how sweet are the judgments of the Lord? He who believes in him is not judged (John 3:18). Sweet, then, are the judgments to those who believe; but those who do not believe have not been condemned by the judgment of Christ, who came not to judge the world but to save and redeem it; rather they have undergone the judgment of their own impiety, since they would not believe in the remission of sins. For they cannot share in the benefit of him whom they refused to know. Those, then, who have not believed in him are seen to be unworthy of his judgment. For recognize what his judgment is, as he himself says: And this is the judgment, that the light has come into this world (ibid. 19). Sweet, then, because it is light; sweet the judgment, because mercy goes before it; for so it is written: Mercy and judgment I will sing to you, O Lord (Ps. 100 (101):1). Sweet too, and most pleasant, is the throat from which this mercy and this judgment go forth.

78. (Verse 16.) And he is wholly desirable. Beautiful in form beyond the sons of men (Ps. 44 (45):3). For he was man according to the flesh, but beyond man according to his divine working. Indeed, when he touched the leper, he seemed a man; but he was beyond man when he cleansed him. And when he wept for Lazarus dead, he wept as a man; but he was above man when he bids the dead man come out with his feet bound. He seemed a man when he hung on the cross; but he was above man when, opening the tombs, he raised the dead. Nor let the poison of Apollinaris flatter itself because it is read thus: And found in appearance as a man (Phil. 2:7); for the man Jesus is not denied, since elsewhere Paul himself says of him: The mediator of God and men, the man Christ Jesus (1 Tim. 2:5); rather it is confirmed. For it is the use and custom of Scripture to signify in this way, as we also read in the Gospel: And we saw his glory, glory as of the only-begotten from the Father (John 1). As, then, he is said to be as the only-begotten, and it is not denied that he is the only-begotten Son of God, so too he is said to be as a man, and it is not denied that the perfection of man was in him.

79. Though he was, then, in the form of a servant, humbled even to death, he was nonetheless in the glory of God. What harm, then, did servitude do him? He is read to have been made a servant because he is read to have been made of the Virgin and created in the flesh; for every creature serves, as the prophet says: For all things serve you. This is the glory of Christ: that he took on servitude in his body, so as to restore freedom to all; he bore our sins, so as to take away the sin of the world; he was made a servant, sin, a curse, so that you might cease to be the servant of sin, so that he might free you from the curse of the divine sentence. He, then, took on your curse: Cursed indeed is everyone who hangs on a tree; he was made a curse on the cross, so that you might be blessed in the kingdom of God; he was dishonored and made cheap, and esteemed as nothing; he said, I have labored in vain; through him Paul merited to say, I have not labored in vain (Phil. 2:16); so that he might confer on his little servants the fruit of good work and the glory of the preaching of the Gospel, through which all might be freed from the burden of labor.

80. (Verse 17.) Such is my beloved, and my friend, daughters of Jerusalem. So the Church, or the holy soul, joined with the daughters of that heavenly city, seeks the Word, and by seeking arouses in herself love for him; while they inquire and say: Where has your beloved gone, O most beautiful among women? Where has your beloved turned aside, that we may seek him with you?

Chapter VI

1. (Verse 1.) My beloved, she says, has gone down into his garden, to the bed of spice, to pasture in the gardens and to gather lilies. She recognizes where she is to seek the Word: she knows that he lingers among the prayers of the saints and that he cleaves to them, and she understands that he pastures his Church, or the souls of his just ones, among the lilies. This mystery he showed you in the Gospel, when on the sabbath he led his disciples through the sown fields. Moses led the people of the Jews through the desert; Christ leads through the sown fields, Christ leads through the lilies; for through his passion the desert blossoms like the lily. Let us follow, then, so that on the day of that great sabbath, in which there is great rest, we may gather fruits. And do not fear that the Pharisees may accuse those who gather in the sown fields: even if they accuse, Christ excuses, and he makes the souls that he wills, those that follow him, like that David who, above the Law, ate the bread of proposition, conceiving even then in his mind the prophetic sacraments of the new grace.

2. (Verse 2.) I am my beloved's, and my beloved is mine, who pastures among the lilies. It seems an easy and common saying, but it belongs to few. For rare enough is he who can say: I am my beloved's, and my beloved is mine. What is it to say: I am yours? For he says it who cleaves to God with all his senses, who knows not how to think of anything else; he uses this saying who can say to the Lord: Show us the Father, and it is enough for us (John 14:8). Does the man greedy for money, for honor, for power use this saying? For many it is not enough to know God, and indeed for most. So many peoples, so many nations, so many rich men think it poverty to serve the Lord; and he who is above all is to them meagre and narrow: for them the Son of God, in whom are all things, is not enough. Thus that rich man in the Gospel, to whom it was said: If you will be perfect, sell all that you have, and give to the poor (Matt. 19:21), judged that God was not sufficient for him. Thus he was even made sad, as though he were bidden to leave what was worth more and to choose what was worth less. He, then, says: I am yours, who can say: Behold, we have left all things, and have followed you (ibid. 27).

3. This is the voice of the apostles, and yet not of all the apostles; for Judas too was an apostle, and he reclined among the apostles at the banquet of Christ. He too used to say: I am yours; but with his voice, not with his heart. Satan came and entered into him, and began to say: He is not yours, Jesus, but he is mine. Indeed he thinks the things that are mine, he turns over in his breast the things that are mine: he feasts with you, and he feeds with me; from you he received bread, from me money; he drinks with you, and sells your blood to me; he is your apostle, and my hireling. The worldly man cannot say: I am yours; for he has many masters. Lust comes, and says: You are mine; because you desire the things that are of the body; in the love of that young girl you sold yourself to me; in lying with that harlot I counted out the price for you. Avarice comes, and says: The silver and gold that you have is the price of your servitude; the possession that you hold is the purchase of your rights, it is the sale of your liberty. Luxury comes, and says: You are mine; the banquet of one day is the price of your life; that outlay on feasts is the auction of your person, it is the sum of your contract. And what is worse, you were bought at a dear price, you are cheaper than your food: your table for one day is more precious than your life for all time; amid cups I redeemed you, amid feasts I acquired you. Ambition comes, and says to you: Plainly you are mine. Do you not know that I made you command others for this reason, that you yourself might serve me? Do you not know that I conferred power on you for this reason, that I might subject you to my power? Or are you unaware that it was said to the Lord the Savior himself by the prince of this world, when he had shown him all the kingdoms of the world: All these things I will give you, if you will fall down and worship me (Matt. 4:9)? He, then, who wishes to have others subject to him is himself subjected first. All the vices come, and each says: You are mine. How cheap a slave is he whom so many compete for! How then can you, who are of this kind, say to Christ: I am yours? He will answer you: Not everyone who says to me, Lord, Lord, shall enter into the kingdom of heaven (Matt. 7:21): not everyone who says to me: I am yours, is mine.

4. You are plainly mine, if your conscience does not refute your voice, if your mind or your works do not disprove your word. I do not deny that he is mine who does not deny himself, or at least who for my sake denies himself to himself. I do not want to have a little slave serving very many masters. For how is he mine, if he says to me in word: I am yours, and denies it by his works, and by his deeds adjudges and binds himself to the devil? He is not mine whom lust inflames, for chastity is mine; he is not mine whom the care of despoiling the lesser drives on, for generosity is mine; he is not mine whom the breeze of fickleness unsettles, for tranquillity is mine. He is not mine who is drunken with the surfeit of wine, unclean with the filth of ambition, greedy for worldly glory, drunk to the point of peril, who cannot keep the track of sober moderation. I am peace, I know not how to quarrel: why should I have him of whom the devil may come and say: He is mine; for he has bowed his neck to me, I find more of what is mine in him: he claims your name for himself, and my gift. No one, then, is Christ's but he who is a stranger to crime: no one is Christ's but he who can always show himself to be Christ's little servant.

5. I am my beloved's, and my beloved is mine, who pastures among the lilies. Among the beloved things of her beloved she seems to say: My secret is mine, my secret is mine. For in the ecstasy of her love she had understood certain hidden mysteries of the faith, which she judged were not to be divulged even to the daughters of Jerusalem themselves. It is good to hide the mystery of the king; for he sins against God who thinks that the mysteries entrusted to him are to be published to the unworthy. There is danger, therefore, not only in saying false things, but even in saying true things, if one imparts them to those to whom one ought not. This fault is fourfold: of flattery, or of avarice, or of boasting, or of incautious talkativeness; for while someone wishes to flatter the one to whom he speaks, he pours out the mystery; some too, from zeal for gain, pursue the wages of betrayal, so as to sell by speaking what should be covered by silence; others, that they may seem to know more and may boast of their knowledge, lay open what they ought to conceal; very many, while they speak without judgment, let slip a word that they cannot call back. Therefore a man is praised who is serious and not idle. There was a man who hid his words, like the apostle Paul, who knows what he should say to each, and when he ought to say what.

6. (Verse 3.) You are well pleasing, my sister, beautiful as Jerusalem, a wonder like ranks set in order. The truth of love is proved by its perpetuity. And so the Bride is praised by the Bridegroom because she has sought him so well and so constantly. And therefore she is now not only called sister, but is also named well pleasing, as it were pleasing to him; because she has pleased the Father: and beautiful as Jerusalem, a wonder like ranks set in order; because she holds all the mysteries of the eternal city, and is a wonder to all who see her; because she is full, as equity is, and perfect: and having borrowed radiance from the light of the Word, while she always strives toward it, she is also terrible, carried up by a certain order to the summit of the virtues.

7. You are beautiful, he says, my friend, sweet and comely, etc. And truly beautiful is she who strives toward him of whom it is said: Beautiful in form above the sons of men (Ps. 44 (45):3). Hence let us too direct our mind toward him as much as we can, and let us be in him. Let us hold in our mind that which is beautiful, comely, good; so that by his illumination and radiance our soul may become beautiful, and our mind clear. For if our eyes, when they are covered with some mist, feed on the greenness of the fields, and by the sight of a grove or of a shady hill drive away all the unevenness of an ailing gaze, and the pupils themselves and the eyeballs seem to be colored with a certain wholesome beauty: how much more does this eye of the mind, when it gazes on that highest good, and dwells in it, and feeds on it, grow bright and shine forth! Beautiful as Jerusalem. This, then, is not the beauty of a perishable body, which will perish either by disease or by old age, but a beauty subject to no mishaps, never to die to the good repute of her merits; since she is worthy to be compared no longer to human beings, but to the heavenly ones, whose life she lives on earth. And therefore the Bridegroom says to the Bride, as to one now perfect:

8. (Verse 4.) Turn away your eyes from me, for they have made me fly away. Do not strain toward me with excessive devotion and faith. You have gone beyond the possibility of nature and of your own condition, for it is hard to gaze straight at light inaccessible. Turn away, he says, your eyes from me: because she cannot bear the fullness of his divinity and the splendor of the true light.

9. We can, however, also take it thus: Turn away your eyes from me; even if you are perfect, other souls must still be redeemed by me, others supported. For you lift me up by seeing me; but I came down for this reason, that I might lift up all. Even if I have risen, and hold the seat of the Father, yet I will not leave you orphans, as though bereft of a father's protection, but I will strengthen you by my presence. You have this written in the Gospel: Behold, I am with you even to the consummation of the world (Matt. 28:20). Turn away your eyes from me, then, because you lift me up. For the more one strives toward the Lord, the more he lifts up the Lord, and is himself lifted up. Hence he too says: I will exalt you, O Lord, for you have upheld me (Ps. 29 (30):1). For the saint exalts the Lord, the sinner humbles him. He wishes, then, that she turn away her eyes, lest he, gazing on her, because she can now follow to the higher things, be lifted up and abandon the other souls. Therefore also in the Gospel he showed his glory not to all the disciples, but to the more perfect (Matt. 17:1). Picture now some teacher who wishes to open an obscure matter to his hearers: how, even if he himself is powerful in speech and in knowledge, he yet condescends to the ignorance of those who do not understand, and uses simple, plainer and familiar speech, so that he can be understood. If, then, someone among the hearers is quicker of understanding, one who can follow easily, he lifts him up and rouses him. Seeing him, the teacher calls him back, so that he may rather allow the teacher to linger over humbler and plainer things, whereby the others too may be able to follow.

10. (Verses 5–7.) The Bride is praised also because she is faithful, because she is powerful in word, because she is fruitful with diverse fruits, because she is one dove, having the unity of the spirit, in which there is peace, which makes both one; and because she is not composed of diverse elements of a divided and mutually warring nature. For what is so diverse as fire and water, air and earth, of which the frame of our body consists? But every blessed soul is simple, imitating him who says: That all may be one, as you too, Father, are in me, and I in you, so they too may be one in us (John 17:21); for this is the consummation and perfection, whence he also added: That they may be one, as we too are one: I in them, and you in me, that they may be perfected into one (ibid. 23). This, then, is the one dove, and perfect, which is simple and spiritual, and is not troubled by the passions of this body, in which there are fightings without, fears within. Indeed Scripture teaches us that by this word of unity concord and peace are signified, saying: And the multitude of believers had one heart and one soul, and there was no separation at all among them (Acts 4:32).

11. Nor is the soul idly praised for fruitfulness: not only because she is fruitful in virtues, but also because she has nothing evil in her; for that is comely and beautiful in which there is nothing evil. For what is good is comely; but what is uncomely, that is evil. Beautiful is the fruitfulness of good works; therefore barrenness is contrary to beauty; since in him who is deprived of beauty or comeliness there is every evil: and what is evil, that is barren and unfruitful. What clearer proof of this is there than the earth? For the earth that is good is fertile and fruitful; but that which is bad is lean and barren; and that which is fertile is also comely. For what is more beautiful than a full field, when the crop waves, when the fruits glow red, or when garlands of grapes hang down, or the olive tree bends laden with berries, or the summits of the mountains and the low places of the valleys are clothed with green grass?

12. For God is the author of all good things: and the things that are his are surely all good, and nowhere is there evil there; and if our mind abides in him, it knows no evil. The soul, then, that does not abide in God is herself the author of her own evils, and so she sins: The soul, however, that sins, she shall die; for, loosed from the golden chains of the virtues, she is borne headlong down the precipice, and slips to lower things. But blessed is the soul which no hostile battles of the body overcome. This soul, like a sparrow when the snare is broken, flies away. For bodily pleasures are the baits of evil things: whoever strives toward these entangles his soul in a snare. But he who abstains from its baits, and comes out of its darkness, his soul shines like the dawn, the soul of which it is said:

13. (Verse 9.) Who is this who looks forth like the dawn, beautiful as the moon? For she looks forth as from a free house, and does not say: Darkness covers me, and walls surround me, and who knows whether the Most High sees (Sir. 23:26)? But rather she seeks the light, as in the upper parts of her house, that is, of her body: and set above the world, she gazes on divine things and lifts herself up to eternal things, that she may be present to God; now carrying the light of her works around the whole world, as the moon carries her disc.

14. (Verse 10.) I went down into the garden of nuts, to see in the birth of the torrent, and to look whether the vine had flowered, and the pomegranates had budded. The Word of God, then, is invited into the garden of nuts, in which is the fruit of prophetic reading and the priestly grace, which is bitter in temptations, hard in labors, fruitful in the inner virtues. Hence also the rod of Aaron, of nut wood, blossomed, no longer by its own nature, but by a hidden power. Let him go down, then, into the garden, that he may gather the vintage of faith, take in the fragrances, find heavenly pasture, feast on the sweetness of our honey.

15. For while she is praised by the Bridegroom, she modestly shrinks from being praised in his presence; then, called back by the bridegroom's love, she says: I went down into the garden of nuts to see in the birth of the torrent. For where is the Church, if not where the priestly rod and grace flourish? There she is often, that she may be proved in bitternesses and in temptations. By the nut we understand bitternesses, by the torrent temptations, but bearable ones, for it is written: Our soul has swum across the torrent (Ps. 123 (124):5). And so she goes down into the place of bitterness, where the vine flowers, and the diverse and manifold fruit, in the appearance of pomegranates, which, as with a single covering for the whole body, is fortified by faith and love.

16. In that bitterness, then, the soul did not know herself, For the corruptible body weighs down the soul, and the earthly dwelling is quickly bowed down (Wisd. 9). Yet she ought always to know herself. But Peter was tempted, and Peter too did not know himself; for had he known himself, he would not have denied his Author. But Christ knew him. Indeed he knew him, because he also looked back at him; for the Lord knows those who are his: and as his own, with the reins of his mercy, like a good driver, he called him back from his fall: our driver, then, is Christ. Hence it also follows:

17. (Verse 11.) It made me the chariot of Aminadab: because the soul is joined to the body, and, like a chariot of snorting horses, seeks a charioteer to be her driver. For Aminadab was the father of Nahshon, as we read in Numbers (Num. 1:7), who was prince of the people of Judah, whose figure is referred to Christ, who, the true prince of the people, mounting the soul of the just man as a charioteer mounts a chariot, steers her with the reins of the Word, lest she be swept by the fury of violent horses over steep places and precipices. For her four affections are like four horses: anger, desire, pleasure, fear: when she begins to be driven by these in their fury, she in no way knows herself. For the corruptible body weighs down the soul, and like a chariot of irrational animals sweeps her away against her will, as she rushes forward, as with a kind of headlong force, while her cares roll on, until the passions of the body we have mentioned grow gentle by the power of the Word.

18. This is the providence of the Word, as of a good charioteer: that the mortal body joined to that soul, which in itself is not subject to death, may not make the driving of her difficult. First, then, let him tame these swift motions of the body, and bridle them with the bond of reason; then let him take care that they do not entangle themselves like horses with uneven motion, so that the wicked one discolors the good, or the slow one hinders it, or the unruly one unsettles it. For the horse of malice snorts, and by tossing itself about harms the chariot, weighs on its yoke-fellow. This one the good charioteer soothes, and sends out onto the plain of truth, and he avoids the windings of deceit. Safe is the course to the higher things, perilous the descent to the lower. Thence, as having served their time well, those who have borne the yoke of the Word are led even to the manger of the Lord, in which the food is not hay, but the bread that came down from heaven. Of this chariot are the wheels of which the Prophet said: And the spirit of life was in the wheels (Ezek. 1:20); because the chariot of the soul, smooth and round, rolls on without any stumbling.

19. It made me the chariot of Aminadab. The soul, then, is a chariot, which bears a good driver. If the soul is a chariot, she has horses, either good or bad. The good horses are the virtues of the soul; the bad horses are the passions of the body. The good driver, then, restrains and calls back the bad horses, and spurs on the good. The good horses are four: prudence, temperance, fortitude, justice; the bad horses are wrathfulness, concupiscence, fear, iniquity. Sometimes the horses themselves disagree among themselves, and either wrathfulness pulls ahead, or fear: and they hinder one another, and slow the course. But the good horses fly, and lift themselves up from the earth to the higher things, and raise the soul; above all if they have the sweet yoke and the light burden of him who says: Take my yoke upon you; for my yoke is sweet, and my burden light (Matt. 11:29).

20. He is the driver who knows how to steer his own horses, so that the course of all may be even: prudence holds back the swifter, justice admonishes the more sluggish with her own whip; temperance makes them gentler, fortitude hardier. He knows how to yoke together the discordant, lest perhaps they tear his chariot apart. And so one may see, in a spectacle for the understanding, each soul swept up to heaven in the highest contest, the horses hastening to be the first to reach the prize of Christ, that the palm may be laid first upon their necks. These are the horses subject to the yoke of faith, bound by the bond of love, by the reins of justice, by the restraints of sobriety.

21. Beautifully, then, she says: It made me the chariot of Aminadab, that is, of the father of the people: and he who is the father of the people is also the father of Nahshon, that is, of serpents. Now call to mind who hung like a serpent on the cross for the salvation of all, and you will understand that that soul is peaceful whose protector is God the Father, and whose charioteer is Christ; for this name too is written in our books: Father, father, charioteer of Israel (2 Kings 2:12). This charioteer, then, says:

22. (Verse 12.) Turn, O Shulammite, turn. Well does he speak both as a charioteer and as to a chariot: Turn, O Shulammite, that is, the peaceful one; for the soul that is peaceful quickly turns herself back and corrects herself, even if she sinned before: and Christ mounts her the more, and deigns to drive her, he to whom it is said: Mount your horses, and your riding is salvation. And elsewhere: You sent your horses to Tarshish (Hab. 3:8). These are the horses of Christ. Christ, then, mounts his horses: the Word of God mounts pious souls. When our soul is peaceful, so that it may be said to her: Turn; turn, O Shulammite, which means the peaceful one: then she will receive Christ into herself as a seal, that is, the image of God, that she may be after the image; since As is the heavenly, such also are the heavenly, and we must bear the image of the heavenly (1 Cor. 15:48), that is, peace. And that we may know this is true, you have it in the same Song of Songs, said at the very end to the soul now perfect, which the Lord Jesus may say to me too: Set me as a seal on your heart, as a seal on your arm (Song 8:6); that peace may shine in my heart, and Christ in my works, that wisdom may be formed in me, and justice, and redemption.

Chapter VII

1. (Verse 1.) How beautiful your steps have become in sandals, O daughter of Aminadab! The curves of your thighs are like necklaces, the work of a craftsman. There is no doubt that here too the steps signify the progress of the Church, or of the soul. How beautiful are the feet of those who bring the good news of peace, who bring the good news of good things (Isa. 52:7)! Surely he calls beautiful the advances of the preaching and the discourse of the Gospel, as it is said elsewhere: Pass over the rivers (Isa. 47:2), that is, run across the streams and slippery places of this world with a steady pace of the mind. That this is said of the soul's pace David plainly shows, when he declares that his soul would pass over the torrent of iniquities (Ps. 123 (124):5).

2. But what does it mean when it says that the steps of the Church are beautiful in sandals? We read what was said to Moses: Loose the sandal of your feet (Exod. 3:5): by which he seems to have been admonished not to be held bound by bodily fetters. So in the Song of Songs it signifies a beautiful loveliness of the soul, which uses the flesh as a sandal, and in that very sandal suffers no hindrance, but excels in the beauty of her gait. The soul therefore shoes herself with the grace of the Church, so that she may pass through the course and the passage of this life with beauty. This is done if she does not soil her sandal with the mud of the body, does not sink it in the whirlpool of vices: if she chastises her flesh, so that it may not hold her back in the race and be weighed down by the burden of thick fat. Modesty is a good sandal of the soul; a good step is in the footprint of chastity. But wisdom is the garment of the soul, and so it is written: Honor her, and she will embrace you (Prov. 4:8). Let us therefore use the body as a sandal for the works of a lower virtue: for service, not for command; for compliance, not for delight; for obedience, not for dissent; and let us set our footprint in the way of wisdom, lest some force of the torrent shut in our steps.

3. Therefore it was said to Moses: Loose the sandal (Exod. 3:5). It was said also to Joshua son of Nun (Josh. 5:16). But to Christ it was not said; rather it was written, when the Baptist said: After me comes a man, whose sandals I am not worthy to carry (Matt. 3:11); because those are rightly admonished to loose their sandal who could not be without sin. But he not only did not loose his sandal, but even loosed the sandals of others; because he not only kept his own body free from sins, but also gave pardon for the sins of all.

4. Therefore the Church, in imitation of Christ, is beautiful, and in her sandals washed clean of every fault. And perhaps when she speaks wisdom among the perfect, she is beautiful in her upper members; but when men also of a lower rank or learning follow the Word, do not forget the order of the faith, and keep the precepts of the priest, she is beautiful in her sandals. Often the clergy has erred, the priest has changed his opinion, the rich have sided with the earthly king of this world; the people have kept their own faith.

5. Hence we may also rightly say of the Lord Jesus that, even in those things that are bodily, the Word has beautiful steps, when moral matters are discussed. And perhaps the apostles are sent with bare feet for this reason, that their discourse may not be overshadowed but may shine forth. And so the Church is the daughter of Aminadab, that is, of one who is willing, or well pleasing; because one who is willing, or well pleasing, gathered her, and she is beautiful in sandals. And rightly it is added:

6. The curves of your thighs are like necklaces, the work of the hands of a craftsman; so that the ornaments of the Church's posterity might be sung. For by the thigh we recognize the emblem of generation, according to that saying: Gird your sword upon your thigh, O most mighty (Ps. 44 (45):4): by which it is signified that the Son of God, when he had emptied himself, girded with the divinity of the Word and shod with human generation, would come forth from the Virgin to give salvation to all. Now the "curves" are said to be precious ornaments that are customarily hung on the necks of matrons. So great an advance of the Church, then, is signified, that it is compared to the most precious ornaments, and to the necklaces of those who triumph; for these are the ornaments of warriors. Hence Symmachus too said epitrachelia, that is, things worn around the neck. Whether, then, it is the generation of Christ from the Virgin or the propagation of the Church, it has adorned the necks of the faithful, in appearance indeed as with necklaces adorned by the hand of a craftsman, but in truth with spiritual insignia of true virtue. In short, this whole description of the members of the Church is full of beauty and praise. For also:

7. (Verse 2.) Her navel like a turned bowl is proclaimed, not lacking mixed wine; because, being turned in all doctrine, it does not fail in the fullness of knowledge and in spiritual drink. And her belly is not only fattened with a heap of wheat, that is, with the stronger foods of the heavenly mystery, but is also filled, as with certain lilies, with the sweetness of moral teachings. Hence she herself also, as a well-deserving queen, is crowned with the blood of Christ, as it is written: And the adornment of her head as purple. The blood of Christ is purple, which dyes the souls of the saints, resplendent not only in color but also in power; because it makes kings, and better kings, to whom it gives an eternal kingdom.

8. Your navel like a turned bowl, never in need of cups. Fine is the navel and belly of the soul that ascends to Christ; and therefore she is praised by the voice of the Bridegroom, saying: Your navel like a turned bowl, not lacking mixed wine; your belly a heap of wheat fenced among lilies. For it is turned in all doctrine, not lacking spiritual drink, in fullness and in the knowledge of heavenly secrets. The belly of the soul too is mystical, as the navel is: by this belly she takes not only strong food, by which hearts are strengthened, but also sweet and flowery food, by which they are delighted.

9. (Verse 4.) Your eyes are pools in Heshbon, at the gates of the daughter of many. Your nostrils are like the tower of Lebanon. What are the pools in Heshbon, if not the abundance in rational thoughts which is at the gates of the Church, to whom a multitude of teaching is rightly accorded? For the daughter of many is the posterity and the effect of very many teachings, whose nostrils are the fragrance of sacrifices, surpassing all flowers.

10. Your nose is like the tower of Lebanon: it is lofty beyond measure, and therefore it looks out toward the face of Damascus, scenting, that is, the Gentile people, and the faith of that people, by whose grace that people would wipe away the stench of its sins. The face of Damascus, then, is the faith of the Gentiles, overshadowed by no covering, veiled by no garment, bare and free, intent on heaven more than on the earth. This the nostrils of the Church watch and look out upon, gathering what is sweet in it, both of the breath of fragrances and of grace.

11. And rightly, Her nostrils are like the tower of Lebanon; because in the sacrifices is the sweet fragrance of the Church, in which the victim of good fragrance is the remission of sins. Take, then, these nostrils, O man, that you may distinguish the flowery from the foul-smelling, and then the Lord will give you life. For when he has noticed what you ask of him, that he turn away the eyes of your mind from vanity, he works together with your soul, so that, if she is captivated by some outward beauty, or is not bent because of hardness or weakness, he may bend her with the yoke of the Word and govern her with his reins, so that by the will of God she may be led away from vices and receive the fragrance of eternal life.

12. (Verse 8.) I will go up into the palm tree, and I will lay hold of its height. Hence know that he has ascended this one too, and has brought her to the place of the palm tree, when he also says to her: How beautiful and sweet you have become, O love, in your delights! Your stature has become like a palm tree (Song 7:6). And she herself says: I said, I will go up to the palm tree. But love itself is also a palm, for it is the fullness of victory; for love is the fullness of the Law. Let us run, then, that we may lay hold; let us run, that we may conquer: he who has conquered goes up into the palm tree and eats its fruits; he who has conquered no longer runs, but sits, as it is written: He who conquers, I will grant him to sit with me on my seat, as I also conquered and sat with my Father on his seat (Rev. 3:21). Hence the philosophers portrayed in their books those chariot contests of souls, and yet they could not reach the palm; because their souls did not know the sweetness and the height of the Word. I will go up, she says, into the palm tree, by conquering the world; that I may hold the height of the Word, which this soul knew, in whom was the turning of the Word; for thus she says:

13. (Verse 10.) I am my brother's, and his turning is toward me. This sense she has repeated three times, in different ways, in the Song of Songs. At the beginning she says: My brother is mine, and I am his: he who pastures in the lilies, until the day breathes and the shadows decline (Song 2:16). Then she says: I am my brother's, and my brother is mine, he who pastures among the lilies (Song 6:2). At the end she says: I am my brother's, and his turning is toward me. The first is for the instruction of the soul; and therefore she also put first: My brother is mine; for as he showed himself, she too took up the desire of cleaving to God. What follows is according to progress; the third according to perfection. In the first, as at the stage of instruction, the soul still sees shadows, not yet stirred by the revelation of the Word drawing near; and therefore the day of the Gospel was not yet shining for her: in the second, without the confusion of shadows, she plucks holy fragrances: in the third, now perfect, she provides rest in herself for the Word, so that he may turn toward her, and lay down his head, and rest; and she invites him to her field, saying:

14. (Verse 11.) Come, my brother, let us go out into the field, let us rest in the villages. Above she was inviting him to the garden; here, to the field, which has not only the grace of flowers but also wheat and barley, that is, the firm support of solid virtues, so that he may look upon its fruits. Let us rest, she says, in the villages, to which Adam, when he had been cast out of paradise, had been banished: in these he rested, but he worked the earth.

15. For what reason she wishes him to go out into the field, the meaning is plain: that as a good shepherd he may pasture his flock, relieve the weary, and call back the wandering. For even if this soul has kept things new and old, yet there are still some who are like lambs, who must be nourished with the juice of milk. Therefore, as one who is perfect, she intercedes not for herself but for others: that he may go out from the bosom of the Father, that he may come forth outside as a bridegroom from his bridal chamber; that, going forth, he may run his way, to win the weak; that he may not linger on that secret throne of the Father and in that light, where feeble horses cannot climb; but that he may be taken up and brought into the house and the secret place of the Bride. Let him be outside for her, that he may be within for us: let him be in our midst, even if he is not seen by us. Hence she says: Who will give you, brother, sucking the breasts of my mother? Finding you outside, I will kiss you (Song 8:1). Good is the soul that is outside, so that the Word may be within: she is outside the body, so that the Word may dwell in us.

16. I will take you up, she says, and I will bring you in (ibid. 2). Rightly is the Word of God taken up and brought in; because he knocks at the soul, that the door may be opened to him: and unless he finds the door open to him, he does not enter. But if anyone opens the gate, he enters and dines. So the Bride takes up the Word, that by taking him up she may be taught. Hence not undeservedly she ascends to this one, to the higher dwellings, and always receives an advance in virtue.

17. Come, my brother, let us go out into the field, let us rest in the villages, let us rise early in the vineyards, let us see whether the vine has flowered. Many fields have fruits; but that one is better which abounds both in fruits and in flowers. The field of the Church, then, is fertile in various riches. Here you may see the buds of virginity blooming with flower; there, as in the open spaces of a wood, widowhood strong in its gravity; elsewhere, as the rich crop of the Church, the harvest of marriage filling the granaries of the world, and, as it were, the winepresses of the Lord Jesus overflowing with the produce of the wedded vineyard, in which the fruit of faithful marriage abounds.

18. The Bride, having obtained the abundance of her desire, speaks to the Bridegroom: Come, my brother, let us go out into the field, let us rest in the villages, let us rise early to the vineyards. You notice how the Bride invites the Word of God to come to the earth and take away the sins of the world. This field was formerly deserted, unkempt with the briars of our sins, bristling with thorns. The village was the one in which Adam, banished, was binding the heirs of his posterity in perpetual exile. Thither, then, the Church leads Christ, to set Adam free. Then, when the exiles had been released, the field of this world began to have fit cultivators: and that which was formerly barren became fertile through the planting of the eternal vine. Yet not even so does she, glad in her spiritual branches, wander off, but she calls Christ to these vineyards, where there are those who sing psalms and pray, where by night and by day an innocent fruit endures.

19. (Verse 12.) There, she says, I will give my breasts; there the mandrakes have given fragrance. Many distinguish a certain sex among mandrakes, so that they think there are both males and females, but the females of a heavy odor. It signifies, then, that the Gentiles, who formerly stank, since they were weaker through a certain emasculated feebleness of unbelief, began to bear fruits of good fragrance after they believed in the coming of the Lord. We read also that holy Rachel received mandrakes from her sister Leah, so that she would grant her to sleep with holy Jacob that night. Reuben the firstborn had brought the mandrakes to his mother Leah, who, with her bleary eyes, took on the figure of the Synagogue; because she could not see the grace of Christ, the gaze of her weak mind being dulled. By this it is declared that the fruits which the Synagogue had formerly received from the firstborn Son of God, she yielded to the Church. But because, through the union of that night which she had asked for, Leah conceived and bore an heir of her posterity, she fulfilled the mystery; since, when the apostles believed, the remnant of the Jews was saved through the election of grace; and so the Church says:

20. (Verse 13.) Things new and old, my brother, I have kept for you. Who will give you as a brother, sucking the breasts of my mother? Finding you outside, I will kiss you, and indeed they will not despise me. I will take you up and bring you into the house of my mother, and into the secret place of her who conceived me; there you will teach me. Having, then, instruction in the new and the old Scriptures, and feeling that she is not to be despised, she holds the Word not only by prayers in the secret of the heart, but also by the voices of the psalm-singing choir, as if she kisses him with certain kisses of grace.

21. Things new and old, my brother, I have kept for you; that is to say: I hold all the commandments of the New and of the Old Testament. The Church alone can say this: no other congregation says it; the Synagogue does not say it, holding neither the new according to the letter, nor the old according to the spirit. The Manichaean heresy does not say, The old I have kept for you, for it does not accept the prophets. Rightly is she called made white, who shines with the grace of both Testaments.

22. I have adjured you, daughters of Jerusalem, that you rouse and rouse again love, until it wills. She still seeks one who will rouse it, and she asks that it be roused for her by the daughters of Jerusalem, for whose sake, that is, for the sake of faithful souls, she desires the Bridegroom to be provoked into a richer love of her. And so those Powers who guard the gates of heaven say.

Chapter VIII

1. (Verse 5.) Who is this who ascends made white, leaning on her brother? How so? She who has placed her head upon the left hand of wisdom, so as to open her hands to the poor to help the needy; so as to use riches that were shared out or left to her, not seized and plundered; she who, desirous of glory, acquired it also by good works, and did not court it with the empty boasting of worldly rank: for this is to set one's head, and as it were the ruling part of the senses, above the hand of wisdom. That soul, I say, ascends with merits growing white, out of this desert of this life, as most have it, toward that ever-flourishing place of eternal joy.

2. These are the Powers which also say in the book of Isaiah: Who is this who comes from Edom, the redness of his garments from Bozrah, so beautiful in his robe (Isa. 63:1)? These, I say, are they who marvel that any soul can ascend out of this rugged and rocky desert without the stain of great vices; and therefore they rejoice that one has been found who has not polluted the garments of natural innocence with the ink of worldly folly, but rather has cleansed them with the whiteness of spiritual wisdom and grace. The daughters of Jerusalem marvel, the holy souls of the patriarchs and prophets and of the righteous of old, or the heavenly powers, saying: Who is this who ascends whitened, leaning on her brother? That is, the Church shines with such solemnities, and she who before was dark by day now shines in the nights and glows again. The Lord himself too, delighted by so great a gift of those who sing psalms, says:

3. (Verse 6.) Set me as a seal upon your heart, as a signet upon your arm; for love is strong as death, your jealousy hard as the underworld. Its wings are wings of fire, and flames. Much water will not be able to shut out love, and rivers will not overwhelm it. Because the Church has seen such great devotion, she thinks the peoples now fit, we who can carry his seal in our heart and on our arm. For God the Father has sealed him, and he who has received his testimony has set his seal that God is true; and therefore those who work for the food that endures to eternal life are sealed after the image and likeness of Christ, who is the image of the invisible God. As, then, God is true, so you too seal truth in your thought and in your work; that your mouth may not speak a lie, that your hands may not work the works of men, which belong to this deceitful world, but those which are of God: that they may give generously to the poor, lift up the weak, honor the dead with the tombs of burial. By these works love is sought, so that no one can be torn from Christ even by the danger of death; whence he says: Who will separate us from Christ? Tribulation, or distress, or persecution (Rom. 8:35)? And below: For I am confident that neither death, nor life, nor angels (ibid.).

4. Under that aspect, too, the jealousy of love is hard, and its wings are wings of fire. It has wings like a dove, for there are the feathers of a dove covered with silver, with which he who loves flies away, saying: Lo, I have gone far off in flight, and remained in solitude (Ps. 54 (55):8). But the wings of love are wings of fire, with which it inflames the ardor of love. With this heat the Lord made his angels fervent spirits, and his ministers a burning fire, but one that does not burn up. We read also of the wing of the temple, upon which the devil, in tempting him, made the Savior go up, which was on the summit of the temple (Matt. 4:5). There are, then, pinnacles of piety, there are summits of love, which are accustomed to kindle the heat of grace in human hearts; so that much water cannot extinguish and shut out love, and no rivers of worldly storms can hem it in. We have said that love shuts out fear; we did not say that it shuts out every disturbance; for he who loves God has a deep tranquility of a confirmed mind.

5. (Verse 7.) Many waters, he says, will not be able to shut out love, and rivers will not flood it. The many waters are those of diverse passions, and the rivers are those of worldly desires stirred up by bodily motions, which nevertheless cannot overthrow the wall of love. And therefore he who is founded in love says: Our soul has passed through the torrent (Ps. 123 (124):5). Could the water of the sea shut out the love of Moses? And, so that according to the letter the course of the psalms may support you: surely, loving God, he believed the way through the seas to be safe for him; but those who did not love God, sunk beneath the waves, suffered the end their sacrilege deserved.

6. (Verse 5.) Who is this who ascends, leaning on her brother? Above they said, Who is this who looks forth like the dawn, beautiful as the moon, chosen as the sun? Here something more has been found to be added, because she had ascended leaning on the Word of God; for the more perfect recline upon Christ, as John too reclined on the breast of Christ. So, then, she too either leaned upon Christ or reclined herself upon him: or certainly, since we are speaking of a wedding, as one already given into the right hand of Christ, she was being led into the bridal chamber for the Bridegroom. And because there is now the bond of love, the bridegroom caresses her and says:

7. Under the apple tree I raised you up; there your mother was in labor with you, there she who bore you was in labor with you. Good is the soul which rests under a fruitful tree, and one above all of good fragrance. For if Nathanael, a good man in whom there was no guile, was seen under the fig tree, surely good is the soul which has been raised up by her Bridegroom under the apple tree. For it is more to be raised up than to be seen: more still to be raised up by the bridegroom. For although Nathanael was seen under the tree, yet his soul was not a bride; because he came to Christ secretly, because he feared the Jews. She was not beautiful as the moon, chosen as the sun, who was in the shadow; because she who weds as a bride by day confesses publicly. Therefore this one is under the apple tree, that one under the fig; because this one spread the fragrance of her confession farther, that one had the sweetness of the purity of innocence, but did not have the fragrance of the spirit.

8. There, he says, your mother was in labor with you, there she who bore you was in labor with you; for there we are born, there we are born again. And those are labored over in whom the image of Christ is formed; whence Paul too says: My little children, with whom I am in labor until Christ be formed in you (Gal. 4:19). For he has been in labor who receives in the womb the spirit of salvation and pours it into others. Whence, since Christ was already formed in her, he says:

9. (Verse 6.) Set me as a seal on your heart, as a signet on your arm. The seal of Christ is on the forehead, the seal is in the heart: on the forehead, that we may always confess; in the heart, that we may always love; the seal on the arm, that we may always work. Let his image, then, shine in our confession, let it shine in the reading, let it shine in works and deeds; that, if it can be, his whole likeness may be expressed in us. Let him be our head, because the head of man is Christ; let him be our eye, that through him we may see the Father; let him be our voice, through which we may speak to the Father; let him be our right hand, through which we may offer our sacrifice to God the Father. He is also our seal, which is the emblem of love and of perfection; because the loving Father sealed the Son, as we read: Him has God the Father sealed (John 6:27). Our love, therefore, is Christ. Good love, when he offered himself to death for the beloved: good love, which forgave sin.

10. And therefore let our soul put on love, and love of such a kind as is strong as death; because as death is the end of sins, so too is love; since he who loves the Lord ceases to sin: Love indeed thinks no evil, nor rejoices over iniquity, but bears all things (1 Cor. 13:5). For he who does not seek his own, how will he seek what belongs to others? There is also that strong death through the washing, through which every sin is buried and guilt is forgiven. Such was the love which that woman of the Gospel brought, of whom the Lord says: Her many sins are forgiven, because she loved much (Luke 7:47). There is also that strong death of the holy martyrs, which abolishes former guilt; and therefore it is strong, a death to which love is not unequal, love which is made equal to the passion of the martyrs, so as to take away the desert of offenses.

11. Jealousy too is as the underworld, since he who has the jealousy of God does not spare even his own for Christ. Thus love has death, and love has jealousy, and love has wings of fire. Indeed, Christ, loving Moses, appeared to him in fire. And Jeremiah, having in himself the gift of divine love, said: And there was a fire burning in my bones, and I was loosed on every side, and I cannot bear it (Jer. 20:9). Good, then, is love, having wings of burning fire, which flies through the breasts and hearts of the saints and burns up whatever is material and earthly, but whatever is pure it proves; and whatever it has touched it makes better by its fire.

12. This fire the Lord Jesus sent upon the earth, and faith shone out, devotion was kindled, love was illumined, justice shone again. With this fire he inflamed the hearts of his apostles, as Cleopas testifies, saying: Was not our heart burning within us, when he opened the Scriptures (Luke 24:32)? The wings of fire in flame, then, are the divine Scriptures. Indeed, he opened the Scriptures, and fire went forth and pierced the hearts of the hearers. And they are true wings of fire, because: The words of the Lord are chaste words, silver tried by fire (Ps. 11 (12):7). When Paul too was taken up by Christ, he saw a light shining round about over him and over those who were with him; he fell from fear, and rose up more approved. Indeed, he who had come as a persecutor was made an apostle. The Holy Spirit too came down and filled the whole house in which very many were sitting, and there appeared divided tongues as of fire. Good wings, the wings of love, truly wings that flew through the mouths of the apostles: and wings of fire, because they spoke a purified word.

13. With these wings Enoch flew away, caught up to heaven; with these wings Elijah flew away, carried to the heights by a fiery chariot and fiery horses. With these wings the Lord God led the people of the fathers by the pillar of fire. These wings the Seraphim had when he took coals of fire from the altar, and touched the mouth of the prophet, and took away his iniquities, and purged his sins. By the fire of these wings the sons of Levi were purified, and the peoples of the nations were baptized, as John testifies, saying of the Lord Jesus: He will baptize you in spirit and fire (Matt. 3:11). Rightly did David wish his reins and his heart to be burned, because he knew that the fiery wings of love are not to be feared. Rightly did the Hebrew children in the burning furnace not feel the burning of the fire, because the flame of love cooled them. And that we may know more fully that perfect love has wings, you heard a little while ago him who said: How often have I wished to gather your children, as a hen gathers her chicks under her wings (Matt. 23:37).

14. Let us take up, then, these wings, which like flames direct us to higher things. Let each one strip his soul of its baser wrappings and, like gold in the fire, prove her wiped clean of mud. For thus the soul is purified, like the finest gold. And the beauty of the soul is sincere virtue and comeliness, a truer knowledge of things above; that she may see that good on which all things depend, while it itself depends on nothing. By it, therefore, she lives and receives understanding. For that highest good is the fountain of life, the desire for which is kindled in us, to draw near to which and to be mingled with which is pleasure: which, to him who does not see it, is an object of desire; and to him who sees, it is life; and therefore he despises all other things, is soothed by this one thing alone, and delights in it. This is what supplies all substance: but itself, remaining in itself, gives to others, and receives nothing into itself from others; of which the Prophet says: I said to the Lord: You are my God, for you have no need of my goods (Ps. 15 (16):2).

15. Its wings are wings of fire. All who desire to return to paradise must be proved by fire; for not idly was it written that, when Adam and Eve had been cast out of their abode in paradise, God placed at the exit of paradise a fiery, turning sword. All must pass through the flames, whether it be John the Evangelist, whom the Lord so loved that he said of him to Peter: If I wish him to remain, what is it to you? You follow me (John 21:22). Of his death some have doubted; of his passing through fire we cannot doubt, because he is in paradise and is not separated from Christ. Or whether it be Peter, who received the keys of the kingdom of heaven, who walked upon the sea, he must say: We have passed through fire and water, and you have brought us out into refreshment (Ps. 65 (66):12). But for John the fiery sword will quickly be turned, because no iniquity is found in him whom equity loved. If there was in him anything of human fault, divine love burned it away: For its wings are like wings of fire. He who has had the fire of love here will not be able to fear the fire of the sword there. To Peter himself, who so many times offered his death for Christ, he says: Pass, recline. But he says: He has tried us with fire, as he himself has tried silver.

16. Its wings are wings of fire. Therefore, since the means of flying has been given to us, let each one stir up in himself the grace of God, and, forgetting what is behind, reaching for what is before, press on to the goal. Far from the honors of public service, far from the heats of the world; lest, as the fables tell, the wax melted by the heat of the sun, with the feather slipping away, should leave flights like Icarus's without support. For although the gravity of the words stands against it, yet with poetic wit they wished to declare that flights through the sky are safe for the maturity of the prudent; but that youthful lightness, liable to the desires of the world, with its feathers flowing away, and the framework of its merits loosened through forgetfulness of the truth, falls back to earth with greater ruin.

17. Flight is not easy for all; difficult too is the course of human life for living creatures in discord upon the earth. But if the order of our action is in accord for you, the prophet will see in us too that one wheel upon the earth, joined to the four living creatures. Ezekiel will see it again, then, for he still sees and flourishes and will flourish (Ezek. 1:15 and 16): he will see, I say, a wheel in the middle of a wheel, gliding upon the earth without stumbling. For the wheel upon the earth is the life of the body fitted to the virtue of the soul and formed, in a coherent course, to the precept of the Gospel. And the wheel in the middle of the wheel is as it were life within life, in that the life of the saints is not out of tune with itself; but such as it was in the earlier age, such let it be also in the following one: or that in this life of the body the practice of eternal life may revolve. When these things have come into accord, then let the divine voice resound, then above the likeness of the throne there will appear a likeness as the appearance of a man. This man is the Word, because The Word was made flesh (John 1:14): this man is the charioteer of our living creatures, he is the driver of our ways, who, according to the reckoning of our merits, often mounts either a chariot, or a mountain, or a ship.

18. Set me as a seal on your heart, as a signet on your arm, you who have kept for me things new and old. You are my seal, you are after my image and likeness. In you shines the image of justice, the image of wisdom, the image of virtue. And because the image of God is in your heart, let it be also in your works: let the likeness of the Gospel be in your deeds, so that in your conduct you may keep my precepts. The likeness of the Gospel will be in you if to him who strikes your cheek you offer the other, if you love your enemy, if you take up your cross and follow me. For this reason I carried the cross for you, that you might not hesitate to carry it for my sake. The daughters of Jerusalem heard this, that the Lord God was now joining the Church to himself: and because, considering the greatness of the Word, they judged themselves unequal to so great a marriage, lest perhaps they might not be able to bear the weight of so great a union, they excuse themselves, saying:

19. (Verse 8.) Our sister is little, and has no breasts. For thus those who wish to defer a marriage are accustomed to excuse themselves, pleading the weakness of an immature age and asserting that they do not have breasts, which signify the marriageable time of life. This is wont to be the token common to all virgins who are to marry: that when their breasts have begun to stand out, then they are judged fit for union. Troubled, then, that the Bridegroom in the eagerness of love is pressing on the marriage, they say:

20. What shall we do for our sister, on the day when he shall speak in her, or, as Symmachus has it, when he shall speak to her? That is, at the celebration of a betrothal there is usually a conversation, and the confirmation of the marriage. What then shall we do, they say in their trouble, since the spiritual union presses? They cannot beg off from so great a marriage; for there is no one who does not think blessed the union either of soul and spirit or of Christ and the Church. But because the fullness of the Word, or of the Holy Spirit, quivers and shines, and there is nothing that can be made equal to them, therefore they desire to defer it, so that by that delay either the soul or the Church may become more perfect. They say, therefore:

21. (Verse 9.) If she is a wall, let us build upon it silver shelters; and if she is a door, let us carve upon it cedar boards. The soul of the saint is a wall. The Church too has her walls, she who, now more perfect, says: I am a fortified city (Isa. 27:3). This is the wall which has twelve apostolic gates, through which an entrance into the Church lies open to the people of the nations. But a wall, although it encloses the circuit of the whole city, is nevertheless more strongly fortified when it has had shelters prepared, in which the defenders of the city may have a safe support for watching and defending. But because this city is rational, and all its hope is in the Word of God, not iron but silver bulwarks are required for it, accustomed to repel the assaults of the enemy by heavenly words rather than by bodily pleasures.

22. Supported by this protection, shining with this splendor, she is judged fitter for union with Christ. And because Christ is the door, who says: If anyone enters by me, he will be saved (John 10:9); the church too is called a door, because through her an approach to salvation lies open to the peoples. But lest she be corrupted by the moths or worms of the heretics, the daughters of Jerusalem, or the angels, or the souls of the righteous, say: Let us build upon her cedar boards, that is, the good fragrance of sublime faith. For sweet is the fragrance of this wood, which neither worm nor moth corrupts. Therefore the use of this wood is chosen for raising the gables of roofs and for forming the letters of the alphabet, by which childhood is instructed for the study of liberal learning. This wood, then, is lofty for grace, light for a burden, sweet for fragrance, useful as an instrument of knowledge, fit for the ministry of eternal understanding. But just as Christ, loving his Bride, was pressing on toward the solemnity of the spiritual union, so too the Church, now captivated by the beauty of the Word, was hastening to the wedding. And therefore, impatient of the delays and the postponement which the daughters of Jerusalem were eager to weave, she says:

23. (Verse 10.) I am a wall, and my breasts are towers. That is, do not doubt whether I am a wall; for they had said: If she is a wall; I, she says, am a wall, and I do not have small breasts, but my breasts are like towers. How do you say that I do not have breasts? I have senses like towers of wisdom, in which there is abundance, as it is written: And abundance in your towers (Ps. 121 (122):7). By these breasts, that is, by her senses, she judged herself fit for so great a marriage. But the daughters of Jerusalem could not yet judge so, because they did not see the abundance of her senses. And she added:

24. (Verse 11.) I was in his eyes as one finding peace; that is, you deliberate about my senses, when I have found the peace of God, which surpasses every mind, and guards hearts and senses in Christ Jesus. Such, she says, was I in the eyes of the bridegroom, as one who has peace; for it is written: Those who seek peace rightly shall rejoice.

25. (Verse 12.) I, she says, am a fortified city, I am a besieged city (Isa. 27:3). With these walls she is fortified; with these, though besieged, she is defended. And truly the soul is a wall, which is set before as a screen in the camp. Whence she herself also says in the Song of Songs: I am a wall, and my breasts are like towers (Verse 10). Good is the wall which the Lord has painted, as he himself says: On my hands I have painted your walls, and you are always in my sight (Isa. 49:16). Good is the soul which has God as her watchman, and is in his hands; like the prophetic soul, which is commended into the hands of God as spirit (Ps. 30 (31):6), and which is in the sight of God: For the eyes of the Lord are upon the righteous; as he too says: I was in his eyes as one finding peace. She has good towers who has both the word concerning things intelligible and discipline concerning things moral. As the beloved and his beloved hastened, then, the spiritual union was celebrated, sought by mutual consent. And therefore, as if singing a wedding song, the Spirit exulted in the prophet, saying:

26. (Verse 11.) Solomon had a vineyard in Beelamon; he gave his vineyard to those who keep it. The Spirit cries out, then: The congregation of the peoples has been planted, but founded on the root of the eternal vine, and with a meek heart it has placed spiritual hearts under the yoke of the Word. And it has been planted in a multitude of nations. For that this is how Beelamon is to be understood, Symmachus, Aquila and the other translations have taught in the Greek tongue. The old union, which could not bear fruit, has been repudiated; the vineyard has been given to new and faithful husbandmen, who could not only produce fruit but also keep it. One sheep, then, went astray, but, called back, it filled the spaces of the whole world. Error had led away one sheep, but the grace of the Lord gathered a multitude of peoples. Man went astray, but in the Church there is now a wall, and a strong wall. Adam went astray; David is a wall, who did not forget the commandments of God. Kept, then, and fenced round with a spiritual defense, this vineyard gives a thousand fruits to Christ, and two hundred fruits to the keepers; and therefore the Church says:

27. (Verse 12.) My vine is in my sight: a thousand for Solomon, and two hundred for those who keep the fruit. The perfection and fullness of Christ is the portion of the little ones. You have this mystery in Genesis, where Joseph gave five portions to his younger brother Benjamin, single ones to the other brothers (Gen. 43:34). To the Lord, then, is assigned the portion and prerogative of the five senses, which he himself surely grants to the one whom he loves, as he loved Paul too, to whom he gave the primacy of wisdom for calling the Gentiles. Here let fear depart from sacred virgins, to whom the Church first grants such great defenses, she who, anxious for the success of her tender offspring, herself grows up like a wall with breasts abounding in the manner of towers: until, the onset of the enemy's siege having been broken, she gains peace for her strong youth by the protection of her motherly strength. Whence the Prophet too says: Let there be peace in your strength, and abundance in your towers (Ps. 121 (122):7).

28. (Verse 13.) You who sit in the gardens, friends attending to your voice, make your voice known to me. And therefore this soul, having the grace of her breasts, enters into the gardens, and finding there the Bridegroom sitting and discoursing with his friends, says: You who sit in the gardens, make your voice known to me: to me, she says, not to the friends.

29. You who sit in the gardens, friends attending to your voice, make your voice known to me. For she was delighted that Christ was sitting in the gardens, and that the friends placed in the gardens were attending to his voice: but because those friends were of the heavenly ones, Archangels or Dominions and Thrones (for men had been expelled from paradise because of disobedience to the heavenly commands, and therefore the Church could not yet hear his voice, which she longed to hear), therefore she says: Make your voice known to me. Whence we too, if we wish him to sit within us, let us be gardens enclosed and fortified, let us bear the flowers of the virtues, the sweetness of grace, so that we may be able to hear the Lord Jesus discoursing with the angels. But because it was to come about that, when the Church had come to her fullness, she would be tried by various persecutions; therefore, while she was delighting in the grace of the Word, she suddenly perceives the snares of the persecutors: and as one who feared more for the Bridegroom than for herself, or because Christ is sought more by the persecutors in us, therefore she says:

30. (Verse 14.) Flee, my brother, and be like a young roe or a fawn of the harts, upon the mountains of spices. He flees for the sake of the weak, who could not bear heavier trials. Therefore it is written that we should flee from cities to cities: and if they persecute us in this city, let us flee into another. For the sake of the weak, then, as we have said, let him flee the persecutors: or let him flee from the lowest things and pass over to the mountains of spices, he who in return for martyrdom can yield the fragrance of the blessed resurrection. The mountains of spices are the saints. To them Christ flees for refuge; because His foundations are on the holy mountains (Ps. 86 (87):1). To them, then, he flees for refuge, who are his stable foundations. In us he flees, in them he stands firm in a faithful station. A mountain of spices, then, is Paul, who can say: We are the good fragrance of Christ to God (2 Cor. 2:15). A mountain of spices is David, the fragrance of whose prayer ascended to the Lord, and therefore he said: Let my prayer be directed as incense in your sight (Ps. 140 (141):2).

31. Symmachus, however, and Aquila have interpreted it that Christ is saying to the Church: You who sit in the gardens, that is, you now sit in the gardens, you are worthy of the paradise above; and therefore Make your voice known to me, to which the friends attend: I too desire to hear it. The Church began to be in the gardens after Christ was taken in the gardens.

32. Flee, my brother, and become like a hart or a fawn of the hart, onto the mountains of spices. For, hated by serpentine snakes, and ever fleeing from dogs, and hostile to the serpents that creep on the ground, he knows not how to dwell except in the loftiness of the virtues, knows not how to abide except with such daughters of the Church as can say: For we are the good fragrance of Christ to God (2 Cor. 2:15). But to some it is a fragrance of death unto death, in those who perish: to others a fragrance of life unto life, in those, surely, who with living faith hope for the fragrance of the Lord's resurrection. These are the mountains of spices, who received the body of the Lord Jesus and bound it in linen cloths with spices. For whoever believed that Jesus died, and was buried, and rose again, these have raised the lofty peak of true faith with the summits of the virtues. Where, then, is Christ sought? In the breast, surely, of a prudent priest?

33. Flee, my brother. She urges the Bridegroom to flee, because she herself will now be able to follow him as he flees earthly things. She says that he should be like a little doe which escapes from the nets; for she herself too wishes to flee and to fly away above the world. Let us flee, then, to our truest homeland. There is our homeland, and the Father by whom we were created, where Jerusalem is, the city which is the mother of all. But what is this flight? Not, surely, of the feet, which belong to the body; for these, wherever they run, run on the earth, and pass from ground to ground. Nor let us flee in ships or chariots, or on horses, which are entangled and fall; but let us flee with the mind and the eyes, or with the inner feet; let us accustom our eyes to see what is clear and bright, to behold the face of continence and temperance and all the virtues, in which there is nothing rough, nothing dark and crooked: and let each one look upon himself and his conscience, and cleanse that eye, that it may have nothing sordid. For what is seen ought not to be out of tune with him who sees; since God has willed us to be conformed to the image of his Son.

34. Known to us, then, is that good, and it is not far from each one of us: For in him we live, are present, and move; for we are also his offspring (Acts 17:28), as the Apostle wished to admonish the Gentiles. For this is the good which we seek, and the only good; for no one is good but God alone. This is the eye which beholds that great and true beauty. Only a sound and strong eye looks upon the sun, nor can any but a good soul see the good: let him, then, become good who wishes to see the Lord, and that which is good. Let us be like this good, and let us do, according to it, the things that are good. This is the good which is above all operation, above every mind and understanding; it is that which always remains, giving life and being to all; because Christ is the fountain of the life of all, of whom the prophet says: In his shadow we shall live. For now our life is hidden in Christ: but when Christ our life appears, we too shall appear with him in glory.

35. Let us not fear death, then; for it is rest for the body, but for the soul either liberty or release. Nor let us fear him who can kill the flesh but cannot kill the soul; because we do not fear him who can take away a garment, we do not fear him who can steal what is ours but cannot steal us. Let us, then, be souls, if we wish to be Hebrews, of those who are companions of Jacob, that is, his imitators. Let us be souls, and our members are garments: the garments indeed are to be kept, that they be not torn, that they grow not old: but he who uses them ought rather to keep and guard himself.
