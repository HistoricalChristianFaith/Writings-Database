Letter to Constantia

No public-domain English translation of this work is available. The fragments preserved in the acts of the Second Council of Nicaea (Greek text in the Patrologia Graeca, vol. 20, cols. 1545ff.) were translated by Cyril Mango in his sourcebook The Art of the Byzantine Empire 312–1453: Sources and Documents (Sources and Documents in the History of Art; Englewood Cliffs, NJ: Prentice-Hall, 1972), pp. 16–18, an anthology of translated texts on Byzantine art; it was reprinted by the University of Toronto Press for the Medieval Academy of America (Medieval Academy Reprints for Teaching 16, 1986; ISBN 978-0-8020-6627-5) and is available from the publisher. Constantia, sister of Constantine the Great, had asked Eusebius to send her an image of Christ. In his reply he refuses: Christ’s divine form cannot be depicted at all, and even the form of a servant that he took on was transfigured and swallowed up in the glory of his divinity, so that no painter’s dead colours could capture it; images of the kind are forbidden by the commandment against likenesses and are not found in the churches, and he tells how he once took away from a woman a picture said to show Paul and the Saviour, so that no one should seem to carry God about in an image as the pagans do. The letter has not survived as a whole: passages from it were cited by the iconoclast synod of Hieria in 754 and so were quoted again, to be refuted, in the acts of the Second Council of Nicaea in 787, and further fragments are found in the writings of the Patriarch Nikephoros. (Noted by Roger Pearse, 14 June 2021, who quotes Mango’s version.)
