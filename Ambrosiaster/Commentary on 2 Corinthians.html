Migne, Patrologia Latina 17 (Paris, 1845), cols. 275–338, verified against the page images, collated in full with the Maurist edition (Paris, 1690) and checked against A. Migne's misprints where the Maurist edition he reprints has the right letter are corrected; at two places a slip is corrected with Ballerini's edition (Milan, 1877) (7:7 nervum, 11:21 illi), and Migne's misprinted Scripture references and verse labels are corrected. Prologue

The Apostle writes a second letter to the Corinthians, and this is its principal reason. Since in the first he had chiefly rebuked their teachers on account of the sins of certain persons, and they had been much grieved, he now consoles them, setting his own example before them and teaching that they ought not to take it ill that they were rebuked for the salvation of others, since he himself, for the salvation of others, is daily exposed to dangers and to death.

Chapter I

(2 Cor. 1:1) Paul, an apostle of Jesus Christ. Now secure about them, he confidently proclaims himself an apostle of the Lord. For in the first letter he says that he was called an apostle, though not approved by those who had been led away from his tradition. Through the will of God. To prove his apostleship valid, he says that he was made an apostle by the will of the Lord Christ: for so the Lord says to the Jews: The things that are pleasing to him, that is, to God the Father, I do always (John 8:29). And Timothy the brother. He writes this together with the one through whom he sent the first letter to them, that is, with Timothy; to show them that he had heard from him of their good will, by which they submitted themselves to be rebuked. To the church which is at Corinth with all the saints who are in Achaia. Now he joins them to the saints of the other churches, so that they may know that they have made progress.

(2 Cor. 1:2) Grace to you and peace from God our Father, and the Lord Jesus Christ. Since the gift of God and of Christ is one, he therefore wishes them to be sharers in the grace of God, which is also Christ's.

(2 Cor. 1:3) Blessed be God and the Father of our Lord Jesus Christ. Always, in every letter, when he is about to speak of God the Father and of his gift, he hands down the order of the mystery. The Father of mercies and God of all consolation. He began from the very reason; for he gives much refreshment to those who had been grieved because of the rebuke. For hearing that the Lord is the father not only of all origin but also of mercies, they were lifted up by hope, and made certain that they had been rebuked for this purpose: that, once converted, they might find the mercy of God. But why did he say "father of mercies," unless so that it might be recognized that through mercy he regenerates and reforms the penitent once more, so that there is not pardon alone but the restoration of their former state? And so he says "of mercies" on account of the various sins: to console those who were grieved because of their offenses, that is, not to deny hope to the converted.

(2 Cor. 1:4) Who consoles us in all our affliction. This is what he said: the God of all consolation, who is present in every affliction; that we too may be able to console those who are in every affliction, through the exhortation with which we are exhorted by God. He introduced two kinds of consolation: one by which those are consoled who suffer affliction unjustly for the name of Christ, so that they are set free; the other, that of those who, grieved because of their sins, receive consolation, while hope is promised to them, as they amend, by those who, consoled by the Lord, were rescued from affliction.

(2 Cor. 1:5) For as the sufferings of Christ abound in us, so through Christ our consolation also abounds. It is plain that he for whom we suffer is himself present to us, consoling us through deliverance, rescuing us from affliction by the intervention of his majesty.

(2 Cor. 1:6–7) But whether we suffer affliction for your exhortation and salvation, or whether we obtain exhortation for your exhortation, which works through the patience of the same sufferings which we ourselves also suffer; so that our hope for you may be certain; because we know that as you are sharers of the sufferings, so you will also be of the consolation. He speaks in general terms: that, just as they suffered persecutions from the faithless for the sake of believers, subjected to injuries and beatings, so again they were delivered by the help of God for the consolation of believers, lest these, having taken offense, fall away from the faith; because the injuries of the apostles were temptations for believers, as it is written in the prophet Zechariah: I will strike the shepherd, and the sheep of the flock will be scattered (Zech. 13:7). Although the faithful believe the things to come, yet amid the very beginnings afflictions cause offense to the newly converted: for they think the promise empty if they see the preacher overpowered by force. But if the believer is already firm, he suffers together with his teacher, trusting in the hope to come; so that he may be consoled together with him with whom he also suffers.

(2 Cor. 1:8) For we do not want you to be ignorant, brothers, of the affliction which befell us in Asia; that we were weighed down beyond measure, beyond our strength, so that we despaired even of living. He points out the sufferings of the afflictions inflicted on them almost to the point of death for this reason: to show what evils they were suffering for the sake of their salvation, so that they might not take it too hard if their errors were rebuked by those who endured such harsh things for them. For what physician would not reprove his patient for treating himself too carelessly, lest the care of his medicine remain without effect?

(2 Cor. 1:9) Indeed, we ourselves received in ourselves the answer of death; so that we should not be trusting in ourselves, but in God, who raises the dead. He indicates that so great an insolence of iniquity had risen up against the preachers of the faith that they had death before their eyes; and so, rescued from that very affliction, they say that they were raised up again. For they had been so afflicted that they despaired of the present life. But since God does not deny his protection to those placed in need, above all to his own, he rescued them as they despaired of themselves but trusted in God. For they professed that under that excessive affliction they would have failed, had God not been present.

(2 Cor. 1:10–11) Who delivered us from such great deaths, and delivers: in whom we have hoped that he will also deliver, your prayer for us also coming to our aid; so that the gift bestowed on us through the faces of many may be celebrated through many in thanksgiving for us. He says this because the grace of God consoled the apostles for the sake of many, that is, of all believers, for whose sake afflictions were also heaped upon them: and therefore, because it is for the sake of all, let all give thanks to God when the trial ceases; or, when it arises, let them make supplication together.

(2 Cor. 1:12) For our glory is this: the testimony of our conscience. He says this because they relied on their conscience, which was pure of all pretense; wherefore they did not distrust the help of God. That in simplicity and sincerity, not in carnal wisdom, but in the grace of God, we have conducted ourselves in this world, and more abundantly toward you. This is the glory of conscience of which he spoke: simplicity and sincerity. And because these belong to the teaching of God, he added: Not in carnal wisdom, but in the grace of God; to show that he had offered a free conscience not to human wisdom but to the preaching of the Gospel. He rubs at things past; for this is what it signifies: that in the first letter he reproved a painted preaching fitted to human understanding (1 Cor. 2:4), whose preachers he accuses of carnal wisdom in a twofold way: because they both preached according to the understanding of the world, so as not easily to offend men, and did this for the sake of gain, intent on their own carnal profits. Hence the Apostle never wished to accept support from the Corinthians, lest he give these men an occasion, on the ground that he was like them in this matter. Hence it is that he says: More abundantly toward you we have conducted ourselves simply; because, though he accepted from others, he would not accept from them, lest he lose the authority to rebuke them.

(2 Cor. 1:13) For we write to you no other things than what you have read and know. He says that he writes the things which they not only saw in his letters but also had manifest in his works; to prove that he fulfills by his deeds the things he says, deeds by which the mind and purpose of each person is learned.

(2 Cor. 1:14) And I hope that you will know to the end, as you have also known not in part, that we are your glory, as you also are ours, in the day of our Lord Jesus Christ. He hopes that they are making progress, because they had already begun to be made better, having come to know the Apostle's affection toward them, and they boasted in him as sons in a most dear father. Hence the Apostle too testifies that his glory is seen in obedient sons; and that it ought to be seen at the time when it profits, that is, in the day of God's judgment. In saying this he admonishes them to persevere.

(2 Cor. 1:15–16) And in this confidence I had wished to come to you first, so that you might have a second grace; and to pass through you into Macedonia, and again to come from Macedonia to you, and to be brought on my way by you into Judea. This confidence comes from their amended life; for he indicates that he now even longs for those whom before it was unfitting to see. They would, then, have had full grace from his coming; because a letter is an image, but presence is the truth. So it is not idle that he had wished to go and did not go; for he wants it understood that there are some among them because of whom he did not fulfill his wish, so that, once effort had been applied, they might be purified.

(2 Cor. 1:17) Planning this, then, did I use levity? Or the things I plan, do I plan according to the flesh, so that with me there is Yes, yes, no, no? Since he said: I had wished to come to you, and did not go, therefore did I use levity, he says, since one who says something and does not do it seems to use levity? So, lest they perhaps think this of the Apostle, he clears it away: that he did not do this lightly, but with counsel, in not fulfilling what he had arranged. Or the things I plan, he says, do I plan according to the flesh? For one who plans according to the flesh fails to fulfill what he arranges when he either defers to more important persons, or else is overcome by gains or by display, which the Apostle always despised. But the spiritual man fails to fulfill what he arranged when he considers something more providently for the salvation of the soul; just as the Apostle too did not fulfill what he had wished for this reason, that through this very thing they might be made better, knowing that he had put off coming because some among them had not yet purified themselves from their sins. So that there is, he says, with me Yes, yes; no, no. This signifies that he did nothing other than what he knew ought to be done; because advantage is to be put before will. This is the spiritual understanding; but the carnal understanding is changed in such a way that it puts will before advantage.

(2 Cor. 1:18) But God is faithful, that our word with you is not Yes and no. This is what he also says to the Galatians: If I build up again these same things which I destroyed, I make myself a transgressor (Gal. 2:18). Therefore he says that the preaching of God is faithful in him: for flatterers do the other thing. For often, so as not to offend men, they pass over the things that are true.

(2 Cor. 1:19) For the Son of God, Jesus Christ, who is in you, who was preached by us, by me and Silvanus and Timothy, was not Yes and no: but in him was Yes. As far as fit preachers are concerned, this is how it ought to be: that with them there is Yes, yes, no, no; so that they do nothing other than what they know to be advantageous. For since we often wish something other than what is advantageous, the Apostle, lest will overcome advantage, therefore set advantage before his will, so as not to go to them. For in Christ Jesus there was not Yes and No, but in him was Yes; because he never willed anything other than what is advantageous. For his will is always joined with advantage: nor was he changed, so as to do unwillingly, like a man, what is advantageous; or so as to waver about anything and change his will.

(2 Cor. 1:20) For as many as are the promises of God, they are in him: and through him the amen to God for glory through us. It is plain that truth is always in God, that is, the amen, made manifest through Christ, afterwards preached through the apostles to the glory of God, the powers of signs bearing witness that the things God promised through Christ our Lord are true. For it is the glory of God when all things are said to be from him, and through him, and in him.

(2 Cor. 1:21–22) But he who confirms us with you is Christ the Lord; and he who anointed us is God: who also sealed us, and gave the pledge, the Spirit, in our hearts. He says that Christ confirms the Gentiles in the faith promised to the Jews; because he it is who made both one (Eph. 2:14), that is, circumcision and uncircumcision, making peace in one new man. The Lord, therefore, confirms, and God, who anointed us, that is, who gave us royal honor, as the apostle Peter says: For we are, he says (1 Pet. 2:9), a royal race through a spiritual anointing, the type of which was in the kings of the Jews. Who also sealed us, giving us his Spirit as a pledge; so that we may not doubt of his promises. For if he has entrusted his Spirit to us while we are still mortal, there is no doubt that he will add to us, once we are immortal, unto glory. Yet he speaks of one work of the Father and of the Son; because he says that both Christ and God confirm. For whom the Son confirms, the Father also confirms: and when the Father gives the Spirit, the Son also gives; because the Holy Spirit is of both, as he says to the Romans: But if anyone does not have the Spirit of Christ, he is not his (Rom. 8:9). Since, therefore, he has spoken of the perfection of man, he made mention of the Trinity in this place; for the whole sum of perfection consists in the Trinity.

But I call upon God as witness upon my soul, that it was to spare you that I have not yet come to Corinth. Now he makes plain for what reason, when he had wished to go to them, he put it off: so as to go at the time when he would find almost all of them already amended. For he is now speaking to those who seemed to want to correct themselves but were not taking pains to carry it out. Since, therefore, it was necessary to rebuke them, he turned his journey toward others, so as not to grieve many; and this was to spare them. And to commend this to their minds, he gives God as witness; lest they should think themselves unworthy and despised by him: so that, having learned this, they might amend themselves in order to deserve his presence.

Not that we lord it over your faith, but we are helpers of your joy; for by faith you stand. Since faith is a matter not of necessity but of will, he therefore says: Not that we lord it over your faith; for lordship is a cause of necessity. But we are helpers, he says, of your joy; that is, because just as there is sorrow in an evil deed, so there is joy in amendment. Of this joy we are helpers, he says; because to those who are willing to amend themselves they offer admonitions, so that what they had begun to will they may be able to fulfill.

Chapter II

(2 Cor. 2:1) But I decided this with myself, not to come to you again in sorrow. It is clear that he did not wish to go for this reason: lest perhaps, in rebuking a few, he should grieve many, being himself grieved as well; for all the members suffer together in the grief of one.

(2 Cor. 2:2) For if I grieve you, who then is it that gladdens me, except the one who is grieved by me? He says this because he does not wish to grieve them: but since they had given themselves to sins, it was necessary for them to be grieved, against the Apostle's will; yet in such a way that, having become obedient for the future, they might gladden those who were grieved with them; so that he who had grieved them by rebuking them might be gladdened by them while they are being corrected.

(2 Cor. 2:3) And I wrote this very thing for this reason, lest, when I had come, I should have sorrow from those from whom I ought to have rejoiced: being certain that my joy is the joy of you all. He says that he wrote these things for this reason: so that, coming afterwards, he would have nothing to grieve him, the vices having been removed, but might rejoice with them as with most dear sons; so that, since he was being afflicted with tribulations by unbelievers, he might at least rejoice in those who had believed. For the joy of the Apostle is the purification of the people; for he rejoices for this reason, because there is no blame in those with whom he must rejoice together.

(2 Cor. 2:4) For out of much affliction and anguish of heart I wrote to you with many tears, not that you might be grieved, but that you might know what love I have more abundantly toward you. It is plain that when someone rebukes another with such feeling that he grieves over his sins more than the other does, he certainly does not rebuke him in order to grieve him, but to show him with what love he loves him. For one who does not rebuke with this feeling grieves his brother; for he who does not grieve with his brother insults him.

(2 Cor. 2:5) But if anyone has grieved me, he has not grieved me, but in part; so that I may not burden you all. All these whom he here calls in part are saints; for there are two parts in the people, as I mentioned in the first letter. These, then, who are of the part of the saints, are the ones whom he indicates to have been grieved at the error of their brother, as he was himself. But in saying this he burdened the one who sins, that is, the one who had sinned. For he becomes more guilty in whose offense many are afflicted: and therefore he says:

(2 Cor. 2:6–7) Sufficient for such a one is this reproof, which is made by many. He will certainly suffer great pain who sees that his offense is abhorred by many. So that, on the contrary, you should rather forgive and console him; lest perhaps one such as this be swallowed up by more abundant sorrow. He commands that help be given to a man afflicted on account of his own sin; because repentance, if it comes from a true heart, that is, if one who is rebuked grieves in his heart at once, immediately bears fruit. Accordingly it is found in the Book of Kingdoms that Ahab, when the prophet had reproved him, being moved in heart, at once obtained pardon (1 Kings 21:29). And David, when he was rebuked in the matter of Uriah the Hittite, because he acknowledged that he had sinned, had his offense blotted out (2 Sam. 12:13). So too the Apostle orders that the man who had committed incest, having his father's wife, and who had been reproved and cast out, should, now that he grieves, be called back; lest, seeing himself, in his grief, spurned for a long time, he should despair of himself and give his mind to enjoying the world, as one who no longer had a place with God. For this is to be swallowed up by greater sorrow: to despair of oneself and turn to committing sins, weighed down by which one is swallowed up by the second death. For this is true repentance: to cease from sin from now on; for so a man proves that he grieves over himself, if he desists for the future.

(2 Cor. 2:8–9) Wherefore I beseech you to confirm your love toward him: for to this end I wrote, to test whether you are obedient in all things. As far as can be understood, he finds that they have become obedient in other matters; for they had amended the things that pertain to ecclesiastical order. Hence he wishes them to be obedient in this too: to confirm their brother in love, receiving him into communion.

(2 Cor. 2:10) But to whom you have forgiven anything, I also. It is plain that what he commands to be done he himself does: nor can he refuse this, which he writes even beseeching that it be done, though he had the power to command. But in the first letter he had denounced the man's crime as grievous, so that all would abhor him: now, when he wants him to be received back, he pleads, lest, with their minds still embittered against him, it be hard for them to have the communion of the Church with him. For there was not as much foresight in them as in the Apostle, that they should at once understand that what the Apostle said must be done, at least in this man's case: therefore he beseeches them to forgive him, indicating that God had pardoned him; because the Apostle did nothing without the Spirit of God. For what I have forgiven, if I have forgiven anything, I have forgiven for your sake in the person of Christ. By saying this he presses them; because if the master, at his disciples' request, forgave the sin of the one they wished, how much more ought the disciples to obey their master? And to show that what he forgave was ratified before God for the one to whom he forgave it, he says that he forgave what he forgave in the person of Christ: that is, that Christ, whose embassy he was discharging, had accepted it; so that the act of the Apostle is the act of Christ, as he said: Whatever you loose on earth will be loosed also in heaven (Matt. 18:18). If, therefore, Christ pardoned this man, for whom they asked through the Apostle, how much more had he already been pardoned, whom the Apostle himself even exhorts them to forgive?

(2 Cor. 2:11) Lest we be possessed by Satan; for we are not ignorant of his wiles. He says this, as I mentioned above, because consolation ought to follow upon a brother who weeps over his offense; lest, grieved for a long time and spurned by the love of the Church, he begin to despair of himself, and the subtle devil, who is always lying in ambush, seeing that his mind has become abject, should approach and suggest to him that he at least enjoy present things, since he has been cast down from the hope of a future reward; and so the brother perish, possessed by the devil, the brother to whom repentance had been given for this purpose, that, being converted, he might reform himself, as the prophet Ezekiel says: I do not want the death of the one who dies, so much as that he turn back and live (Ezek. 18:23). And again: Let him be converted, he says, and I will replant them (Jer. 24:6).

(2 Cor. 2:12–13) But when I had come to Troas for the Gospel of Christ, and a door had been opened to me in the Lord, I had no rest for my spirit, because I did not find Titus my brother: but having bidden them farewell, I set out for Macedonia. He indicates that he went to Troas to preach the Gospel of Christ to them, and that when he had begun to preach, there were those who received the word of faith: but because the support of Titus was lacking, the labor was unbearable on account of those who were raising an uproar. For although some of them had opened their hearts to receive the word of God, yet the impudence of the unbelievers was by no means slight, rising up against the Apostle out of jealousy toward the believers: and these two tasks could not be accomplished by one man, namely both to instruct the faithful and to fight off the unbelieving. And so, having bidden farewell to those who had received him, he set out for Macedonia.

(2 Cor. 2:14) But thanks be to God, who always triumphs through us in Christ Jesus. For God to triumph through the apostles in Christ is this: to make them victors in the faith of Christ; so that, faithlessness having been trampled down, faith may have its trophy, while the faithless are made faithful, and the malevolent make no headway in persecuting believers.

(2 Cor. 2:15) And to manifest the odor of his knowledge through us in every place; for we are the good odor of Christ to God among those who are being saved, and among those who are perishing. The odor of the knowledge of God is in Christ, and through Christ. And he said "odor" for this reason: because, just as certain things, though they are not seen, are nevertheless recognized by their odor, and you understand that something you do not see is in a certain place, so too God, since he is invisible, willed to be understood through Christ; so that the preaching of Christ (which indeed reaches the ears invisibly, as odor reaches the nostrils) might make known God the Father as Creator, and his only-begotten Son; and that the apostles, discharging the embassy for this (as the Lord says to God, the Son to the Father: As you sent me into this world, so I also send them into this world) (John 17:18), might manifest the odor of the knowledge of God and of Christ through signs and wonders. That this preaching about God and Christ is true is proved by the testimony of power: and this is signified by odor for this reason, because, though God is not seen, he is understood through this power, which works invisibly, to be in the apostles, so that the truth of their teaching may be made manifest. Whoever, therefore, rightly asserts Christ is a good odor to God, worthy of praise in one who believes, and not blameworthy in one who does not believe: but whoever does not rightly assert Christ is a bad odor to God, both among those who trust and among those who distrust; for he will be guilty on behalf of both. For he who distrusts seems not to believe for this reason, because what he hears is perverse; and he who believes believes wrongly. The Apostle, therefore, has spoken according to the pattern of the Law: that just as in the Law he who offered victims with a good vow was a good odor to God and acceptable, so now too the power of preaching renders the odor of teaching fragrant to God. For this reason God kept the apostles safe by his help, so that they might pour the words of his knowledge into open ears, whether of Jews or of Gentiles, adding to these the mystery of the birth of the Lord Jesus according to the will of God and his Father, in the unity of the saving faith, for the salvation of believers and the perdition of those who distrust.

(2 Cor. 2:16) To some indeed we are the odor of death unto death. He says this because to unbelievers the preaching of the cross of Christ is an odor of death; for when they hear the word of God, they receive it as a pestilence from which death arises: and it must be done to them according to their faith. But to others the odor of life unto life. It is true, because to those who trust, the word of God, once heard, is the messenger of eternal salvation, and it will befall them according to their faith. Whether, then, among those who perish or among those who are saved, the apostles were the good odor of Christ to God; because they preached sincerely and without flattery. For those who did not believe were putting a noose around themselves, while the apostles remained free, as the Lord says to the prophet Ezekiel: Preach; if they hear you, you will gain their souls: but if not, let them see to it themselves; you, however, will be free of their perdition (Ezek. 3:19).

(2 Cor. 2:17) And for these things who is fit? For we are not like most, adulterating the word of God: but out of sincerity, as from God, before God, in Christ we speak, This verse touches the false apostles, who with corrupted teaching adulterated the words of God through evil interpretation, and, taking away the divine sense, put a human one in its place. Some of them, having zeal for the Jewish tradition, did not teach rightly about Christ either; therefore he calls them less fit: but the apostles, he says, preached sincerely, as it was given from God, before God; that is, they spoke in Christ as God gave it, with him as witness, seeking not their own glory but God's. This is to speak in Christ: to preach his honor and power.

Chapter III

(2 Cor. 3:1) We are beginning again to commend ourselves. Since he is marking out the false apostles, pointing to their crooked inventions, while testifying that he himself is a true preacher, he seems to be commending himself again, as in the first epistle. And lest he should seem to do this by some cunning, for the sake of earthly gain, he added: Do we need, as some do, letters of recommendation to you, or from you? In saying this he clears his own intention, and still marks out the false apostles, who, as he shows, go round the churches for the sake of their own honor and gain, to take salvation away, not to bestow it. Of their number were those to whom the Lord says: You devour the houses of widows and orphans, praying long prayers; therefore you will receive the greater judgment (Matt. 23:14). For these were seizing both their property and their souls; but the Apostle did not touch their property, and by the truth of his doctrine he was saving their souls. Therefore he commended himself not carnally but spiritually, for the advancement of their salvation, so that they might believe of him what he was; for he who thinks ill of a good man sins: and on this account this commendation profits them rather.

(2 Cor. 3:2) You are our epistle, written in our hearts. The epistle of the Apostle is the token of salvation. Rightly, then, he says that the salvation of the Corinthians was in the heart of the Apostle and of those who were with him; for they were always thinking about their salvation. Since, then, they are in the heart of the Apostle and of those who were with him, they are an epistle written in their hearts; for when anyone is always mindful of someone, that person is written in his mind.

(2 Cor. 3:3) Which is known and read by all men: made manifest that you are an epistle of Christ, ministered by us. It is plain that when they are called Christians, they are an epistle of Christ, indicating the salvation which God gave in Christ to all men, the apostles doing the writing; for while they teach, they write. Written not with ink, but with the Spirit of the living God. Since the things that are promised are eternal, for that reason they are said to be written with the Spirit of God, who always is; but temporal things with ink, which fades and loses its memory. Not on tablets of stone, but on carnal tablets of the heart. Now he strikes at the old law, which, first given on tablets of stone, was abolished when the tablets were broken by Moses beneath the mountain. But the new law is written in the mind (Exod. 32:19), that is, in the heart, not with a pen, but by the Spirit; because faith is an eternal thing, it is written by the Spirit, so that it may remain: but the old precepts will cease when the world fails. As, then, law differs from law, so the stewards of that law differ from the stewards of the law of faith.

(2 Cor. 3:4) But such confidence we have through Christ toward God. How great the dignity of the apostles is, he shows when he says: But such confidence we have toward God through Christ. This is to show that the men of old did not have this confidence toward God; because the ministration was lesser.

(2 Cor. 3:5–6) Not that we are sufficient to think anything of ourselves, as though it were from ourselves: but our sufficiency is from God, who also made us fit ministers of the new testament, not by the letter, but by the spirit. For the letter kills, but the spirit gives life. Although he sets forth the apostolic dignity, he nevertheless breaks out in praise of God, ascribing this not to human merits but to the grace of him who deigned, for the sake of human life, to ordain a saving preaching, which would save those whom the old law held guilty, remission of sins being given through Jesus Christ our Lord. For the letters were given to Moses for this purpose, that it might kill those who despised the Law, according to the right of nature. But the spirit, that is, the law of faith, which is not written but is held in the mind, gives life to those guilty of death; provided, however, that they turn themselves, so that, being justified, they may sin no more thereafter. Rightly, then, the Law had been given, that there might be fear of sinning: but because the human race is frail, the mercy of God was given to the apostles in their preaching, so that, their sins being forgiven, those who believe in Christ might escape death. This is the preaching of the new testament, which God had promised through the prophets.

(2 Cor. 3:7–8) But if the ministration of death, formed in letters on stones, was in glory, so that the sons of Israel could not gaze on the face of Moses because of the glory of his countenance, which is done away: how shall not the ministration of the Spirit be rather in glory? It is manifest that the grace of the law of faith is greater than that of the law of Moses; for although the law of Moses was given for this purpose, to be of profit, yet, being despised, it became a law of death. Therefore, because it could not provide for sinners, the law of faith came to save them, not only pardoning but also justifying them. There is, then, a great difference between law and law; for at that time, stained with sin, they could not gaze on the face of Moses as he came down from the mountain with the law received on the tablets; because his face had been made glorious, so that it could not be looked upon by sinners, who were worthy of death (Exod. 34:30). This glory is now done away through the law of the Spirit, since, having received the remission of sins and been made righteous, they are worthy to behold the glory of God, and are able to, as Peter and John, and James saw the glory of Christ on the mountain (Matt. 17:2).

(2 Cor. 3:9) For if there is glory in the ministration of condemnation, much more will the ministration of righteousness abound in glory. He says this because the gift of the righteousness of God through faith in Christ is greater than that of the old law; because there is more glory in salvation than in death: although he condemns justly, yet it profits his praise more if he pardons, so that the guilty man may be able to correct himself.

(2 Cor. 3:10) For neither was that made glorious which was glorified in this respect, because of the surpassing glory. It is manifest that what was seen as glorious in the face of Moses was not made glorious (Exod. 34:29). For since the glory of his face profited no one, but rather did harm, it did not have the fruit of glory, though not through its own fault but through that of those who sinned; yet in this respect there is no glory. But that is rather glory which abounds in grace; so that men, purified by the gift of God, the darkness wiped away, may be able to see the glory of Christ.

(2 Cor. 3:11) For if what is done away was in glory, much more what remains is in glory. He does not deny that there was glory in the law, or in the face of Moses, but it did not remain; because in Moses it was something yet to come, not the truth. Therefore, when the Savior came, the figure ceased; because the truth appeared, remaining forever; so that as great as the difference is between image and truth, so great is the difference between the glory of the face of Moses and the glory of Christ. Therefore he said above: Because of the surpassing glory. For that glory was as great as was fit to be entrusted to a servant: but this one is as great as is that of his Begetter; because the Lord Jesus Christ is in the glory of God the Father (Phil. 2:11). For just as in the evening the stars are glorious, but when the sun rises their brightness is done away; so too the glory of Moses is done away when the glory of Christ appears.

(2 Cor. 3:12) Having therefore such hope, let us use great confidence. He says that we have the hope of seeing glory, not such as was in the face of Moses, but that which the three apostles saw on the mountain, when the Lord revealed himself (Matt. 17:2). From this, therefore, we ought to gather how much the divine clemency has conferred on us, and how much it has deigned to enrich us with the gift of a higher grace than that which it had given to the Jews. For the Jews, though the glory of the face of Moses was lesser, could not gaze upon it: but we believe that we shall see not the glory of Moses, which is inferior, but the surpassing glory of the Savior. The Jews, then, did not merit to see the glory of a servant: but we are to see the glory of the common Lord. And therefore we too ought to make a return, as far as we can, to the benevolence of God, so that we may be the more devoted in love of him who, washing us from our sins, gave us confidence of seeing the glory of God. Now, therefore, there is need that confidence, prepared by good deeds, should grow in us; for we shall see as much as we believe.

(2 Cor. 3:13) And not as Moses put a veil over his face, so that the sons of Israel should not gaze on his face, which is done away. Since we have been made worthy through the grace of God to see the glory of Christ, for that reason he says: And not as Moses put a veil over his face, so that the sons of Israel should not gaze (Exod. 34:33). He put a veil for this reason, because they could not bear the splendor of his face, on account of sin; when this is taken away, power is given to see the glory of God up to its end; because it is not unveiled until, leaving the Law behind, they turn to the grace of faith, and so it is done away. For when dignity comes through faith, unworthiness is done away.

(2 Cor. 3:14) But their senses were dulled up to this very day. He calls them dulled for as long as they do not believe. This dullness comes about on account of unbelief; therefore, when they are converted to faith, the edge of the mind is sharpened, so that they may see the splendor of the divine light.

This same veil remains in the reading of the old testament, while it is not unveiled; because in Christ it is done away. This dullness, he says, remains in the reading of Exodus until they believe. For it is not unveiled unless they believe; for in Christ it is done away, that is, through faith in Christ the veil is taken away; for when the offense is removed, they will begin to see what, while sin stood in the way, they could not see.

(2 Cor. 3:15) But up to this very day, when Moses is read (Exod. 34:33), a veil is laid over their heart. It is manifest that when this part of the Law is read, the sentence of those who are under the Law is recited.

(2 Cor. 3:16) But when he has turned to the Lord, the veil will be taken away. To turn to the Lord is this: to believe in Christ, so that, acknowledging the Lord, he may merit to receive pardon. This is the taking away of the veil: that the sentence is done away by which he was held guilty under the Law.

(2 Cor. 3:17) Now the Lord is spirit. And where the Spirit of the Lord is, there is liberty. Since God is Spirit, he gave through Christ a law of the Spirit: not, indeed, written in letters, but imparted to minds through faith; not one that teaches visible things, but one that persuades us to believe invisible things; which the mind grasps spiritually, not which the eye perceives. This law gives liberty, demanding faith alone; so that, because he believes what he does not see, he may merit to be delivered from his condition.

(2 Cor. 3:18) We all, therefore, beholding the glory of the Lord with unveiled face, are formed into the same image from glory into glory, as by the Spirit of the Lord. He says that we all, who have obtained liberty by the gift of the grace of God, beholding the glory of the Lord through faith, are transformed into the same image which we hope for: that is, so that, when this life has passed, we may be found like the image of the glory of Christ, as John the apostle says: We know, he says, that when he appears, we shall be like him (1 John 3:2).

From glory into glory, as by the Spirit of the Lord; that is, lifted by the kindness of God from the glory of Moses, which on account of our guilt we could not behold, we are transformed into the glory which we believe is given to us by the Spirit of the Lord. For as much glory will be given as it is worthy for God to give through his Spirit; for this is why he said: As by the Spirit of the Lord; to show that such glory is given as befits the sublimity of the giver. For the glory of Moses was neither so great nor lasting; for he received glory, too, in proportion to the Law; so also, in proportion to the law of faith, in which is the Spirit of God, glory will be given to believers. For God has granted so much to the faithful that he gives them his Spirit, the pledge of that glory which he promised.

Chapter IV

(2 Cor. 4:1) Wherefore, having this ministration, according as we have obtained mercy, let us not fail. He says that he has such great hope of this ministration that he does not fail in afflictions, strengthened by faith in the promises. Hence he said above: Let us use great confidence (2 Cor. 3:12); for those who trust in the things that are promised endure whatever adversity befalls them. This he does not attribute to human merits, but to the mercy of God, which first washes a man, then justifies him, adopting him as a son to God, so as to endow him with a glory like the glory of God's own Son.

(2 Cor. 4:2) But we renounce the hidden things of shame. That a man may be worthy of that glory, he teaches that all base and polluted things that can be done and thought must be put away, so that they are driven out not only from deed but also from thought. These are the words of one who invites; for under his own person and that of his companions he exhorts them to a better life, because of the vices mentioned above, which he often censures in them. These too can be hidden things of shame: the things that some devise with a perverse mind for their preaching, in order to deceive; hence he added: Not walking in craftiness, nor adulterating the word of God. For he who with a deceitful mind fabricates doctrine to deceive the hearts of the simple works toward his own shame and disfigurement; for he will be found base in the day of the judgment of God. For the craftiness of an evil mind, in order to accomplish what pleases it, adulterates the words of God so as to overturn their sense. Now to adulterate is to wish to shut out the true sense by a false one. But by the manifestation of the truth commending ourselves to every conscience of men before God. He says this because in his preaching of the Gospel he made himself suspect to no one, adding something more when he says: Before God; so as to prove this very thing manifest not only to men, but also to God, from whom nothing is hidden. He calls, then, on the testimony of God, so that he may at least be believed, because he preaches just as it was given by the Author: and God is a witness in this way, when he grants signs and wonders to be done by his hands.

(2 Cor. 4:3) But even if our Gospel is still covered, it is so in those who are perishing. It is true that unbelievers, blinded by faithlessness, do not see the splendor of the power of God; for a veil is over their heart, and the dullness of unbelief, among the Jews most of all.

(2 Cor. 4:4) In whom the God of this world has blinded the minds of unbelievers, so that they do not see clearly the light of the Gospel of the majesty of Christ, who is the image of God. He says that God blinds the minds of worldly men, because they are ill-disposed toward faith in Christ, so that they do not see the truth of the Gospel of the majesty of Christ. He grants them, then, what they want. For because they are ill-willed men, and, though they understand the truth, say that it is false, they are helped in that very thing, so that they cannot believe what they do not want to. But Christ, though he is the image of God, they assert to be bodily only: and, leaving aside his deeds, they make mention of the flesh alone; of them Isaiah said: He has blinded their eyes, so that they may not see; and their ears, so that they may not hear, up to this very day (Isa. 6:10).

(2 Cor. 4:5) For we do not preach ourselves, but Christ Jesus our Lord. That is, we do not proclaim our own glory, lest anyone should say that we preach the Gospel for our own sake, so that it might perhaps profit us for a time: but we proclaim Jesus our Lord, subjecting ourselves to his power and majesty. Since, then, we burden no one, extort from no one, and confess Christ our Lord, what reason is there that we should be judged proud, as though we preached for our own advantage, in order to appear glorious? And ourselves your servants for Jesus' sake. He proves himself so much the servant of Christ that, at his command, he testifies that he is their minister in preaching; so that for their benefit they are made subject in the ministry of the Gospel. In saying servants, then, he means ministers: but he spoke so in order to speak humbly, so as truly to show that he preaches the Gospel not for his own glory, but for the splendor of our Lord, whom he obeys, whom he serves, as the Lord himself also says: I am in your midst not to be served, but to serve (Luke 22:27); not because of the merit of those whom he serves, but because of the command of the Lord.

(2 Cor. 4:6) For God, who said that light should shine out of darkness, has shone in our hearts, for the illumination of the knowledge of the glory of God in the face of Christ Jesus. He says this because it came about by the mercy of God, he says, that through us, who were unbelieving in ignorance, that is, in darkness, God should give light to the other Gentiles. How greatly does he still humble himself, so as to set forth the glory of God alone and of Christ? For enlightenment is given so that they may have knowledge of the glory of God through Christ. For this reason he says "knowledge of the glory of God", that is, not of God alone, but also of Christ, who is his glory; so as to signify that not only God is known, but also his works and mercy and providence, by which he created and saved the human race, seen in Christ through the glory of his power.

(2 Cor. 4:7) But having this treasure in earthen vessels, so that the excellence may be of the power of God, and not from us. By the treasure he signified the mystery of God in Christ, which is made manifest to believers, but for unbelievers is covered by a kind of veil; because just as a treasure is placed in hiding, so too the mystery of God is hidden within man, that is, in the human heart. He says, then, that this treasure in mind and body is given by God, so that the eminence of his power may appear through men who are merely preachers; so that every tongue may be reconciled to its Creator, not to the honor of men, but of God, who manifests himself through men: who, though they were lowly and unskilled, received from God the power both to speak great things and to do them. In saying earthen vessels, then, he signifies the weakness of human nature, which can do nothing unless it receives power from God, and God, for his own praise, proclaims himself through these things that are weak; so that the glory may be given to him, not to man, who was fashioned from clay.

(2 Cor. 4:8) Suffering affliction in all things, but not straitened. Now he shows why they were fit preachers: because God was always present to them in need. Therefore suffering affliction, he says, but not straitened: that is, God did not allow us to be oppressed so greatly, so that we might believe. Suffering want, but not abandoned: that is, God was present as a shepherd to those placed in want.

(2 Cor. 4:9) Suffering persecution, but not forsaken. It is true, because God was taking care of them, so that their enemies should not have their fill of them. Cast down, but we do not perish: that is, laid low by blows, but, since God stood in the way, not put to death; for whether shut up or bound, by the help of God they escaped the hands of their enemies. Indeed, when Paul and Silas were shut up, already beaten, and their feet already fastened in the stocks, they were eagerly singing a hymn to God, made stronger than those who had not been beaten.

(2 Cor. 4:10) Always carrying about the death of the Lord Jesus in our body, so that the life of Jesus also may be manifested in our body. There is no doubt that Christ is killed in the martyrs, and that in those who suffer for the faith, deaths or chains or blows are sufferings of Christ; so that his life also may become evident in their body. For they are sufferings which show merit for the future life which Christ promised. Hence he says in another place: When I am weak, then I am strong (2 Cor. 12:10). And again: Through tribulations we must enter into the kingdom of God (Acts 14:21).

(2 Cor. 4:11) If, therefore, we who live are delivered to death for Jesus' sake, so that the life of Jesus also may become evident in this mortal flesh of ours. What he says is plain: for this reason, he says, we do not refuse to be delivered to death for Jesus' sake, though we could live, so that the life by which Christ rose from the dead may be granted to this mortal flesh of ours, that is, not to fear to die, because of the promised resurrection.

(2 Cor. 4:12) So then death is at work in us, but life in you. He says this because they were subjecting themselves to death for their salvation; for in preaching to the Gentiles they stirred up enmities against themselves, from Jews as much as from Gentiles, even to death.

(2 Cor. 4:13) But having the same spirit of faith: that is, we have with you a common spirit, through which faith is strengthened. For this is why he suffered such great ills for them, so that they might be joined to one another in faith. According to what is written: I believed, for which reason I have spoken; and we believe, for which reason we also speak (Ps. 115:1). He gave an example from the hundred and fifteenth psalm for this reason, to show that he is ready to endure all things because he believes in the future resurrection. Secure, then, about the future life, he has no care for the present life; since indeed this is what is believed: that the blessed life to come is hoped for if this one is despised. Secure, then, because what he believed is true, he dares to preach it, so as to make others sharers in his hope. Knowing that he who raised up the Lord Jesus will raise us up also with Jesus, and will set us with you. He says that they labor for the faith in this hope, because the example of the resurrection of the Lord Jesus goes before, showing what those who believe will be; because as in Adam all die, so also in Christ all shall be made alive. For Adam is the pattern of death, because of sin: but Christ the pattern of life, because of righteousness; for he did no sin, nor was guile found in his mouth. And since he has called them sharers of a common faith, raised up, he says, he will set us with you; so that those of one faith may be in one house of peace. For when they understand that these men suffer deadly perils for them, and they themselves are anxious about their sufferings, as suffering with them, they will also be sharers together in the promised life; so that, having grieved together, they may rejoice together. He speaks thus on account of those who denied the resurrection, whom he also reproaches in the first epistle.

(2 Cor. 4:14–15) For all things are for your sakes, so that grace, abounding through the thanksgiving of many, may abound to the glory of God. God willed that no one should be a stranger to his gift: and because not all receive the word of faith, the Apostle, knowing the will of God, did not fear to suffer persecutions and dangers, provided that he preached faithfully to all, so that more might be able to believe; so that the abundant gift of God might not be diminished through the thanksgiving of a few, to the dishonor of God, but, profiting many, might overflow through the thanksgiving of many to the glory of God. How worthy of honor, then, is this man, who always exposed his life to death, so that the gift of God should not profit far otherwise than it was given? Is it not a great dishonor to one who prepares a rich banquet and invites many, if he has only a few in all?

(2 Cor. 4:16) Therefore we do not fail. It is manifest that these words belong to the sense above; for, to show his devotion still more ready in the things of God, he added these words, by which he proves that he in no way fails to see that what is pleasing to God is fulfilled; secure, however, about the promised resurrection. And even though our outer man is being corrupted, yet the inner is renewed from day to day. By afflictions, blows, hunger, thirst, cold and nakedness the flesh is corrupted, but the soul is renewed by the hope of the future reward; because it is purged by constant tribulations. For it advances in affliction, it does not perish, so that, as temptations come upon it, it daily gains merit; because this corruption also profits the body toward immortality, by the merit of the soul.

(2 Cor. 4:17) For that which at present is momentary and light in our affliction works in us beyond measure, in sublimity, an eternal weight of glory. The tribulations of the present time, which are inflicted for the sake of faith, he calls momentary and light, because they are temporal; yet beyond measure, in sublimity, they work an eternal weight of glory for those who suffer. For a great reward is rendered for small labors, and for light tribulation a sublimity of everlasting glory beyond measure will be weighed out in return.

(2 Cor. 4:18) For us who look not at the things that are seen, but at the things that are not seen; for the things that are seen are temporal: but the things that are not perceived are eternal. Having a desire for heavenly and spiritual things, he professes to despise these present and earthly things; because in comparison with spiritual things these are nothing. For these things are to those above as the figure is to the truth: the figure perishes, the truth remains; and on this account the righteous do not fear to depart from this world, but rejoice.

Chapter V

(2 Cor. 5:1) But we know that if our earthly house of this dwelling is dissolved, that we have a building from God, a house not made with hands, eternal in the heavens. With this confidence he says that one should not fear to be dissolved from the body by the violence of unbelievers or by the lot of life, because for men of this kind an eternal dwelling has been prepared in the heavens, so that, driven out of the temporal and earthly one, they may be received into a perpetual house. Hence this too is to be wished for, if it can happen; but since it is not to be wished for, lest another perish in your glory, yet if it should happen, it is to be borne gladly. For he called this flesh an earthly house because it is mortal, so that if we are dissolved from it, we shall find a house built in the heavens, eternal. This house signifies the immortal body, in which, rising again, we shall exist forever, and whose form has already been shown forth in the heavens in the body of the Lord.

(2 Cor. 5:2–3) For indeed in this we groan, longing to be clothed over with our habitation which is from heaven; if indeed, being clothed, we are not found naked. He says that they groan in prayer for this reason: that the glory promised from the heavens may clothe them as they rise again. Longing for this, therefore, they persist in prayer, lest, when they rise again, having of course received the body, they be found naked, that is, strangers to the promised glory. For this is what is needed: that the soul, clothed with the body, be clothed over also with glory by the judgment of God, which is the change into brightness. For death is from the earth, but resurrection from the heavens, provided that it is changed into glory. Other codices read thus: If indeed, being stripped, we are not found naked: that is, if, going out of the body, we have been clothed with Christ; because whoever are baptized in Christ put on Christ (Gal. 3:27). Therefore if we remain in the form of baptism and of the tradition, being stripped of the body we are not found naked, because Christ dwells in the inner man; and when we shall be seen clothed with him, or with the Holy Spirit given to us, we shall be worthy to be clothed over with the promised heavenly glory. For the promised brightness will descend upon him whom it sees to have the sign of adoption.

(2 Cor. 5:4) For while we are in this body, that is, the mortal one, we groan, being burdened, because we do not wish to be unclothed, but to be clothed over, so that this mortal may be swallowed up by life. The sense is the same. For he says this: that we, burdened by the sufferings of the body and by the storms of the world itself, direct our petitions to God, lest we be changed, overcome, and become unworthy; but that, when our departure has come, persevering in faith, we may be found clothed with the Holy Spirit, who in substance is Christ. For then we shall be able to be clothed over with the promised glory, if, unclothed of the body, we have not been stripped of the Holy Spirit. Thus the mortal is swallowed up by life, that is, so that, rising again, we are clothed over with immortality together with glory, so that it can no longer die or be entangled in sufferings; and this is to be swallowed up by life. For he is not to be said to be swallowed up by life who rises for this, that he may be driven to punishments.

(2 Cor. 5:5) But he who perfects us for this very thing is God, who also gave us the Spirit as a pledge. This which we ask with groaning, that is, that we may be found worthy not to be unclothed but to be clothed over, according to the sense stated above, he says God brings to perfection on the day of judgment, because he has promised and he is faithful, giving the Spirit as a pledge of the fulfillment of this thing; for he is the seal of our adoption.

(2 Cor. 5:6–7) Being confident, therefore, always, and knowing that while we dwell in this body we are abroad from the Lord; for we walk by faith, not by sight. It is plain that we are with the Lord by faith, not by presence, and for this reason we are abroad not in faith but in sight. And why, when the same man says in the Acts of the Apostles, For in him we live and move and are (Acts 17:28), does he say in this place that we are abroad from the Lord? If he is everywhere, how are we, placed here, abroad from the Lord? Without doubt he is everywhere; rather, all things are in God; but because the seat of God is in the heavens and there he is always seen, therefore, placed here, where he is not seen, we are said to be abroad from him. For when we do not see him, although he is present, we are absent from him.

(2 Cor. 5:8) We are bold, therefore, and consent rather to be abroad from the body and to be present with God. Rightly he says, we are bold; for, having confidence in the promise of God and knowing that it is much better to be there than in the world, they consent to and desire departing from the body, so that they may rest under the altar of God until the day of resurrection.

(2 Cor. 5:9) And therefore we strive, whether present or abroad, to please him. He says that we must act on this and persist in good works, so that whether still placed in this life or present before the judgment seat of Christ, we may please him. Hence, if discipline is kept, we shall please him both here and there; because he who pleases here will not displease there.

(2 Cor. 5:10) For we must all be made manifest before the judgment seat of Christ, so that each one may receive the things proper to the body, according as he has done, whether good or evil. Therefore if, when Christ judges, each of us will receive the deeds of the body, he will certainly not be adjudged to good or to evil without the body. And he did not say "the deeds of the flesh," because the vices of the flesh are always to be punished, but "the deeds of the body"; it acts spiritually, sometimes carnally.

(2 Cor. 5:11) Knowing therefore the fear of the Lord, we persuade men; but we are manifest to God. Since, then, the judgment of God is to come through Christ to distinguish the works of each person, therefore the Apostle says that he persuades men toward a good mind and life, so that his hearers may be secure from the punishment to come, and he himself may boast, having in them the fruit of his labors; because through the perverse talk of some, the day of God's judgment is brought into doubt: For evil conversations corrupt good morals (1 Cor. 15:33). We persuade men, he says, so that they may believe and take forethought, lest they incur it and repent then, when repentance has no fruit. But we are manifest to God; for he preached what God had commanded, and God is said to know what is good. Lastly, to those who do evil he says, I do not know you. But I hope that we are manifest also in your consciences (Matt. 25:12). Their purity was manifest to the Corinthians, because they had neither ever been caught in flattery, nor had their preaching displeased the saints, nor had they tried to draw anything secretly from any of them, as is usually done, so as to be simple outwardly before the crowds but impostors within. Therefore he appeals to their conscience, so that they themselves might be witnesses for themselves to the apostles' truthfulness.

(2 Cor. 5:12) We do not commend ourselves to you again. Since above, in calling to witness his true preaching, he seemed to praise himself, therefore he now signifies that he says these same things not for his own praise but for their glory, so that they may rejoice that they have learned the preaching whole from these men announcing it. But we give you an occasion of boasting about us, so that you may have something against those who boast in appearance and not in heart. He says this because many through pride boasted in themselves about the apostles, professing that they had been taught by those who were always with the Lord; and therefore he says here that he gives them a reason by which they too might boast against those men, because he was an Apostle, from whom these too had learned. Hence in another place he says: I have done nothing less than those who are apostles in the highest degree (2 Cor. 12:11). This he says under compulsion, lest his silence harm these people. He, then, who boasts in the heart restrains pride, knowing that God gives grace to the humble, but resists the proud (Jas. 4:6); for the mind of the proud does not bear fruit worthy of God.

(2 Cor. 5:13) For whether we are out of our mind, it is for God; or whether we are of sound mind, it is for you. He says this because, if he is thought to have spoken haughtily or proudly, since he seemed to praise himself while speaking the truth, this must be left to God; but if it is understood not as spoken proudly but for the glory of the hearers, he says that it profits the Corinthians; so that what was said is sound for the hearers then, if it is understood as it was said; but if it is thought to have been said boastfully, it must be left to God as something mad; for all pride is held to be a kind of madness.

(2 Cor. 5:14) For the love of Christ urges us, judging this, that if one died for all. Since Christ, loving the human race, gave himself up to death to redeem them, therefore the apostles, in order to repay him, exhort men in some measure to his service; and to draw them, they must bear witness that they preach the truth while others teach perverse things; nor should they keep silent about their own merit, so that they may point out the punishment of the evil teachers. They did these things, then, not from pride of mind, but so that the gift of Christ might be understood by all, and that, faithfully devoted, they might render thanks to him. Because of the love of Christ, therefore, they do not keep silent about what and how great his gifts are toward those who love him: not for the sake of boasting, but to invite their hearers to devotion to him, lest the death of Christ seem fruitless, not so that the praise of the apostles might be recognized. For through the merit and glory of the apostles the grace of Christ and the kindness of God are recognized.

(2 Cor. 5:15) Therefore all have died, and Christ died for all, so that those also who live may no longer live for themselves, but for him who died for them and rose again. Because all must die on account of Adam, Christ died for all, to free them from the second death; and therefore those who live in the body, knowing that Christ died for them, should be subject to him, professing him as Lord. For his resurrection bears witness that his death benefits them. Therefore he does not live for himself who is ready to do the will of his lord.

(2 Cor. 5:16) So we from this time know no one according to the flesh. And if we have known Christ according to the flesh, now we no longer know him. It is true, because, Christ having risen from the dead, the carnal birth now ceases in him, the weakness of the body ceases, and the suffering of death ceases too. For up to the cross there was a suspicion of weakness in Christ; but afterwards he appeared to be what he was not believed to be, as he himself also says: When you have lifted up the Son of man, then you will know that I am (John 8:28). The Apostle mentions this, therefore, in order to show with what devotion Christ is to be obeyed. For for the salvation of men he not only did not disdain to be born a man, but also willed to be treated with insults and to die, so that, understanding how precious his death is, they may serve him the more readily, repaying him not as a man but as God; because to each one service is to be rendered according to his person and merits.

(2 Cor. 5:17) Wherefore if there is any new creature in Christ, the old things have passed away, and behold, all things have been made new. It is plain that through Christ all things have been renewed, if they consider his dignity. For he becomes new to those to whom he formerly seemed only a man, when he is understood to be God: so that, as the weakness recedes, the divinity may be recognized, and, as the old error ceases which, by introducing the belief in many gods, had led the human race away from faith in the one true God, all things may return to the profession of simplicity, adoring one God in the Trinity.

(2 Cor. 5:18) But all things are from God. Although Christ has redeemed us, yet all things are from God, because from him is all fatherhood (Eph. 3:15); and therefore the person of the Father must be put first.

(2 Cor. 5:19–21) Who reconciled us to himself through Christ, and gave us the ministry of reconciliation; since indeed God was in Christ, reconciling the world to himself, not reckoning to them their own offenses, and placing in us the word of reconciliation for Christ, for whom we act as ambassadors, as though God were exhorting through us: beseeching on behalf of Christ to be reconciled to God. For him who had not known sin he made sin for us, so that we might be the righteousness of God in him. God almighty, the begetter of Christ (since all the things that he had made through Christ had been rendered fallen by error, forgetful of their Creator), deigned that Christ our Lord should come from the sacred seats to earthly things, made flesh in the form of man, so that he might be a pattern for men of how they might make God their Creator at peace with them. God, then, was in Christ. How? As in a vicar or an ambassador, as he was in the prophets, or otherwise? Can he not be understood to have been in the Son as well, just as he was in the prophets? For the Son is by nature the ambassador of God the Father. Hence he says: Because the Father is in me, and I in the Father (John 14:11); for the Father is understood to be in the Son by this, that their substance is one. For there is unity where there is no difference; and for this reason they are in one another, because both their image and their likeness are one, so that he who sees the Son is said to have seen the Father, as the Lord himself also says: He who sees me sees the Father also (John 14:9). Rightly, then, it is said: God was in Christ; that is, the Father in the Son, reconciling the world to himself; not reckoning to them their offenses; since the creature sinned against God and did not repent so as to return to him, God, not wishing his work to perish, sent his Son, so that, the remission of sins having been preached to them through him, he might reconcile them to himself through the very one through whom he had created us. Whether, then, through the Son or through servants, God exhorts the people; because the whole sum must be referred to him by whose will and providence Christ was incarnate to redeem the salvation of men; who, when he wished to return to the Father, gave to the disciples the dispensation he had received from the Father.

Beseeching you on behalf of Christ to be reconciled to God. This is what I said: that in place of Christ he gave the apostles as vicars, so that in his stead they might preach being reconciled to God.

(2 Cor. 5:21) Him who had not known sin he made sin for us. He says that God the Father made his Son Christ sin, him indeed who knew no sin, that is, who had not sinned; because, made flesh, not changed but incarnate, he was made sin; just as one who becomes a prefect does not lose what he was, but certainly takes on what he was not. Christ, then, was made man because of sin, he whom neither the lot nor the dignity of being born a man touched. But because all flesh is under sin, therefore, made flesh, he was also made sin. And since he was offered for sins, not undeservedly is he said to have been made sin, because in the Law too the victim that was offered for sins was called sin. So that we might be the righteousness of God through him, who knew no sin, as Isaiah says: Who did no sin, nor was deceit found in his mouth (Isa. 53:9); he was slain as a sinner, so that sinners might be justified before God in Christ. For Satan was jealous of the Savior, seeing him teach men how they might make God gracious to them by renouncing the devil; and for this reason he killed him, not knowing that it would turn against himself. For Christ, after the cross, descended to the underworld, and death was conquered, because he who knew no sin could not be held by death; because death through sin emptied hell, so that the death of the righteous one might profit sinners; so that henceforth death cannot hold those who have the sign of the cross.

Chapter VI

(2 Cor. 6:1) But helping, we also beseech you not to receive the grace of God in vain. The Apostle strives to undertake the care of human salvation in a twofold way: so that he might both be devoted to the providence of God and show men the service of love.

(2 Cor. 6:2) In an acceptable time I have heard you, and in the day of salvation I have helped you (Isa. 49:8): this is written in Isaiah the prophet. He teaches that the grace of God was predestined for the time of Christ; for God decreed that his mercy should abound in such a way that he would bestow help on those who ask in the name of Christ.

(2 Cor. 6:3) Behold, now is the acceptable time; behold, now is the day of salvation; giving no offense to anyone, so that our ministry may not be blamed. He says that the time is at hand in which sinners can advance to pardon; he says that the time is at hand in which medicine can be poured into mortal diseases; and that he is therefore anxious about the health of the sick, lest perhaps through some negligence the grace of the medicine should lack the effect of his good will. By his faith and vigilance, therefore, he cuts away every offense from the negligent, lest perhaps their sluggishness should give rise to an occasion of offense for the disciples; and in this way they signify that they are free, because with all earnestness they simply preach what brings salvation. Hence he added: So that our ministry may not be blamed; for their ministry would be blamed if, by their own works, they did not give examples so that the things they taught in words might be done.

(2 Cor. 6:4) But in all things let us commend ourselves as ministers of God. The ministers of God teach without flattery, so as to please him whose ministers they are; not like the false apostles, who, knowing that they had not been sent by God, were eager for present advantage. In much patience. It is patience that saves men; for unless he had used patience, how would he have saved the Corinthians, whom after his preaching he had found entangled in such great vices of conduct and in the errors of various sects, and whom by his patience he is gradually calling back to the true doctrine? In afflictions. He bore afflictions, knowing what has been promised by God for them. In necessities. It was a necessity for him to teach even when placed in affliction, because he had been sent by the Lord. In straits. Hard pressed, in the hope of what is to come he did not yield to the opposition of the faithless.

(2 Cor. 6:5) In stripes. Often beaten by Jews and Gentiles, he did not keep silent about the grace of Christ. In prisons. Often thrown into prison, with no regard for his own safety, even there he preached the gift of God. In tumults. He was so devoted to God that not even turmoil lessened the confidence he had in God. In labors. He did not cease to labor with his own hands, so as not to be a burden to anyone, sure that this profited him before God. In watchings. He was so careful about the office entrusted to him that he did not stop even at night. In fastings. He endured fasts, sometimes voluntary, sometimes of necessity because of want, and gave thanks to God, who fed him spiritually; for he was content for this reason, so as not to be bent for the belly's sake.

(2 Cor. 6:6) In chastity. By defending chastity, whether of the body or of the Gospel, he made not a few enemies. In knowledge. Asserting the knowledge of the Law and of the Gospel not in human wisdom nor in pretense, he showed himself to God a faithful steward of Christ. In magnanimity. He was of great soul in bearing the weaknesses of the brothers and in contempt of this world; hence in another place he says: The world is crucified to me, and I to the world (Gal. 6:14). In kindness. He was kind, because just as he reproved, so also he coaxed, so that after rebuke he might console with gentle exhortation. In the Holy Spirit. The Holy Spirit flees from what is feigned (Wis. 1:5). Since, then, he taught sincerely, he handed on the gift of God in the Holy Spirit. In love unfeigned. Feigned love is in those who desert the brothers in need; hence he, always making the needs of the brothers his own, was simple in love (Gratian, Decretum, De poenitentia, dist. 2, c. Ficta charitas). For he suffered with all, as he says in another place: Who is made to stumble, and I do not burn (2 Cor. 11:29)? For this is true love: if, disregarding one's own advantage, one takes care of him whom one professes to love; which the Apostle always did.

(2 Cor. 6:7) In the word of truth. The word of truth was in his teaching, because he handed on nothing other than what he had received from the Lord. In the power of God. The power of God was in him, which through signs and wonders proved him a fit minister. Through the armor of righteousness on the right hand and on the left. He signifies that he was free from both the faithful and the unbelieving. For the armor of righteousness destroys iniquity.

(2 Cor. 6:8) Through glory and dishonor. To those who considered the Gospel of God a glory, he says, he was clearly a sound and faithful preacher. In like manner he showed himself a faithful minister of God also to those who thought the words of the divine teaching a dishonor and a disfigurement, since he is not frightened, nor does he suffer shame at telling them things they shudder to hear. Through ill repute and good repute. He presented himself as a sound steward of God also to those who gave the faith a good reputation and to those who gave it a bad one, since he does not fear this ill will but is steadfast.

As deceivers, and truthful. Unbelievers called them deceivers; the faithful, on the contrary, declared them truthful and simple. Nor does he yield to this hatred so as to lay a false color over the truth. As unknown, and yet known. To the wicked they were unknown, to the good known; for these recognized the truth in them.

(2 Cor. 6:9) As dying, and behold, we live. Those who held them in hatred thought every day that they would not escape the threats of wickedness; but these men, because they taught with God's favor, were kept safe by the help of Christ from present and future death. As tempted, but not delivered to death. They seemed tempted when they were so treated that they were thought to be giving way; and because they were not overcome, they were not consigned to death. For he is consigned to death who does not remain in the faith; and to death not this one, but the one to come. It can also be understood of present death; for God allowed them to be tempted so that through afflictions they might grow in merit, yet he did not permit them to be killed.

(2 Cor. 6:10) As sorrowful, yet always rejoicing. It is true, because this sorrow works joy, and those who saddened them with insults were heightening their joy. As poor, yet enriching many. As far as the present life is concerned, they seemed poor; but they bestowed spiritual riches on believers: needy on earth, rich in the heavens. As having nothing, yet possessing all things. This was glorious in the apostles: that without anxiety and without the name of possessing, they possessed not only the things that were in possessions but even their owners themselves; for everything had to be laid at their feet because of their powers, as we read in the Acts of the Apostles (Acts 4:35).

(2 Cor. 6:11) Our mouth is open to you, O Corinthians. He speaks this because of his freedom and pure conscience; for a mind conscious of evil is afraid to speak, loses its sense, and errs in its words. Our heart is enlarged. The heart of those is enlarged who rejoice within themselves through confidence in their good conduct, or at least who, in the hope of a future reward, are not straitened in tribulation; because just as in this life no return follows unless labor has gone before, so also in divine things, if deaths have not gone before, the reward will not follow.

(2 Cor. 6:12–13) You are not straitened in us, but you are straitened in your own bowels, having the same recompense of reward. He says this because it did no harm to the teachers if the disciples turned out badly, despising the vigor of the teaching; because each one will receive a reward according to his works; because, as far as the teachers were concerned, they did not keep silent, as the Lord says to the prophet Ezekiel: Lift up your voice and speak to the people; if they hear you, you will gain them; if not, you will save your own soul (Ezek. 3:19).

(2 Cor. 6:14) I speak as to sons: be enlarged you also, and do not bear the yoke with unbelievers. He exhorts them to good conduct and hope, so that, being confident and cleansing their consciences, they may be able to rejoice in themselves, having the confidence of a pure mind, as their teachers also do, separating themselves from the fellowship of unbelievers in evil works. So he wants them to be enlarged in spirit both by doing good and by hoping for the things to come; for he who labors and does not believe in the things to come, like an unbeliever, is narrow of spirit through despair of the future.

(2 Cor. 6:15–16) For what participation has righteousness with iniquity? Or what fellowship has light with darkness? Or what agreement has Christ with Belial? Or what part has the believer with the unbeliever? Or what concord has the temple of God with idols? It is plain that these things he enumerates are contrary; and for this reason he teaches that they must be fled, because the Lord says: No one can serve two masters (Matt. 6:24). For the Law preaches righteousness, so that iniquity may be fled; it shows the light, which is the truth, so that one may withdraw from ignorance, which is darkness; it proclaims Christ in the mystery of God, so that one may depart from the devil, who wishes falsely to pass himself off as God; it promises eternal life to believers, so that, stripped of faithlessness, they may be strangers to every error of unbelievers; it forbade idols to be worshipped, because they are hostile to the temple of God. For you are the temple of the living God. Nothing is so hostile to man as idols, because they force him to withdraw from faith in the one God.

(2 Cor. 6:17–18) As God said (Lev. 26:12): I will dwell in them and walk among them, and I will be their God, and they shall be my people. Therefore go out from their midst and be separated, says the Lord (Isa. 52:11), and do not touch what is unclean; and I will receive you, and I will be a father to you, and you shall be sons and daughters to me, says the Lord almighty. Meanwhile let us explain the sense of the first matter, so that we may make plain whose person's words they are, who says: I will dwell in them and walk among them, and I will be their God, and they shall be my people. These are the words of Christ; for this is what Jeremiah testified among other things, saying: After these things he was seen on earth and conversed among men (Bar. 3:38); for he dwelt in us, as John the apostle says: And the Word was made flesh and dwelt in us (John 1:14). And that he is our God, Jeremiah says again: This is our God (Bar. 3:36). And that we are his people there is no doubt; for the Church is Christ's. And therefore he wishes us to be separated from all defilement, so that he may receive us as sons, as he says: Little children, yet a little while I am with you (John 13:33). And that he is almighty, I think ought not to be doubted. For if, as he himself says, whatever the Father does, the Son also does likewise (John 5:19), this means that the Son can do all things that the Father also can. If there is something the Father cannot do, the Son also cannot. But the Son is trustworthy, who says of the Father: But with God all things are possible (Matt. 19:26). By this testimony he has both exhorted us to a pure life and shown that our Lord Jesus was long ago predestined as our Lord and as the one who would receive us in the affection of his love.

Chapter VII

(2 Cor. 7:1) Having therefore these promises, dearly beloved, let us cleanse ourselves from all defilement of the flesh, perfecting the holiness of the Spirit in the fear of God. What he says is plain; yet the defilement of the flesh is to be understood in many ways. For this is why he did not say, from defilement of the flesh, but from all defilement: so that we may flee all carnal vices entirely; for everything that the law forbids is carnal. That we may perfect the holiness of the spirit in the fear of God. We perfect the holiness of the spirit in this way, if in the fear of God we follow what is right, so that, abstaining from sins under the name of Christ, we may be holy. For those who appear to restrain themselves from vices without the profession of Christ are sanctified according to the world, not according to the Spirit of God; because according to God those are clean who are faithful, while the rest, whatever sort they may be, are unclean. For whatever is without Christ is unclean, as he says to Titus: For to unbelievers nothing is clean; for both their mind and conscience are defiled (Titus 1:15).

(2 Cor. 7:2) Make room for us. We have harmed no one, we have burdened no one, we have overreached no one. He wants them to consider what he says, so that, having taken these things into their mind, they may weigh within themselves that what he speaks is true; and, spurning those at whom he touches, may transfer their whole mind to those whom they see love them with true affection. For it was the false apostles who both harmed them, corrupting their understanding, and burdened their purses by the overreaching of a serpent's cunning.

(2 Cor. 7:3) I do not say this to condemn you. That is, I do not cast you off, but I admonish you to correct yourselves; for he who condemns someone does not forgive him. For I have already told you before that you are in our hearts, to die together and to live together. From what he said before he wants them to recognize with what mind he speaks to them. For those whom he wishes to have as sharers both in present sufferings for Christ and in the life to come, he certainly does not cast off; rather he exhorts them to make themselves worthy of that sharing.

(2 Cor. 7:4) Great is my confidence toward you, great is my boasting about you: I am filled with consolation, I overflow with joy in all our affliction. This confidence comes from the rebuke of the first letter: because they did not receive it harshly, they gave him confidence to admonish them; for they appeared willing to correct themselves, and that is why he boasts. On their account he declares that his own mind too has been consoled in this respect, so much so that he solemnly affirms that he overflows with joy in all affliction; for seeing that there is hope in those for whom he suffers hardships, he rejoices when he is afflicted, certain that he will receive from God a reward for the salvation won in them.

(2 Cor. 7:5) For indeed when we had come into Macedonia, our flesh had no rest. He recalls the afflictions and beatings which they suffered for the sake of believers, in order to provoke them all the more to love. Our flesh, he says, had no rest: so that they might suffer with them, being certain that they would hand over their lives even unto death for the salvation of the faithful. For before one tribulation and bodily injury had passed, another came, so that the sufferer had no rest. For since all flesh is dull in sensation, he therefore indicates that this flesh had and has no rest in suffering; but that the soul, although it too suffered in the body, nevertheless has rest in this respect, that it hopes God will give a reward for these tribulations, which are inflicted for the faith by the faithless. In fact, in the very affliction they were singing hymns to God.

(Acts 16:9 ff.) But we were afflicted in every way. According to the history, at the Lord's call they entered Macedonia; and when not a few had believed, and they had found comfort in Lydia, a woman who believed with all her household, then it came about that he drove out a spirit of divination (a python), that is, a soothsaying spirit, from the slave girl of certain men, who, seeing that they had lost no small profit, which the girl brought in by soothsaying, stirred up a riot against Paul and Silas, and dragged them into the forum before the magistrates; and these, laying many blows upon them, sent them into the depths of the prison, so that they shut their feet in the stocks. This is the affliction which they suffered in Macedonia. And this is why he says: Our flesh had no rest.

Fightings without, fears within. There were fightings for the body while it was being beaten; for the mind, fears. For fear is where understanding is; but this fear was on account of those who had believed, lest they be scandalized by his suffering. For what could he still fear for himself, he who was suffering? Besides, he says in the Acts of the Apostles that he is ready not only to be bound but also to die for the name of Jesus Christ (Acts 21:13). He seems therefore to have meant that the whole man was afflicted in the flesh, so that, with respect to the spirit, which is given to man in order to remain, since it is impassible, it is by no means to be understood as having been able to be afflicted; but so that the whole man is said to be afflicted in the flesh with respect to body and soul, while with respect to the spirit he is impassible. It can also be understood thus: that fightings without, that is, in public, were waged by the faithless against the faithful, while, full of fury, they resisted the truth; and the news of this bred fears within, where the Apostle was. For what man would not fear hardships? But because he was the more inclined toward the things of God, he overcame this fear by hope.

(2 Cor. 7:6) But God, who consoles the lowly, consoled us by the coming of Titus. Since God is not unmindful of his own, but always gives them comfort in tribulation, he hastened the coming of Titus, to be a refreshment to those placed in the heat. For it is a great consolation to one who suffers if he has with him someone who suffers with him.

(2 Cor. 7:7) And not only, he says, by his coming, but also by the consolation with which he himself was consoled in you. He adds more to the consolation, because he indicates that he has heard from Titus of their ready will to conversion, so much so that Titus, who was grieved at their disobedience, received consolation from their repentance. The Apostle shows how great an affection he had toward them: such that he took no account of the depths of the prison, nor of the pain of the blows on his torn body, nor of the stocks in which his feet were shut; but, having heard of their correction, he rejoiced, and, become forgetful of his sufferings, gave thanks to God for their salvation, regarding this as a kind of recompense for his tribulations. Announcing to us your longing. Titus announced that they had a longing to amend themselves; for learning what has been promised to those who live well, they were stirred to a longing for these things. Your weeping. Being rebuked, they wept, grieving that they had sinned, nor did they attempt to excuse themselves; that is why the Apostle boasts of them. Your zeal for me; for learning the Apostle's love toward them, they began to defend him against his adversaries.

(2 Cor. 7:8) So that I rejoiced the more; hence, even if I grieved you in the letter, I do not regret it. It is plain that he ought not to regret this matter, from which such an effect followed, namely that in the first letter he rebuked their errors rather severely. I do not regret, he says, having written rather harshly, because the case demanded it.

(2 Cor. 7:9) And if I did regret it, I see that that letter, even if it grieved you for an hour, now I rejoice. He says this: for if I did regret it, he says, on account of love, because I grieved you, the consolation of joy would follow, because it was profitable that I grieved you. Not because you were grieved, but because you were grieved unto repentance; for you were grieved according to God. This is why he says he rejoiced: because they were grieved with shame, not with anger. For he who, when reproved, suffers shame promises to correct himself; but he who grows angry shows that he will be worse. So that you might suffer loss in nothing from us. So that all that is ours, he says, may have its effect in you; even our grieving you is for your sake.

(2 Cor. 7:10) For the sorrow which is according to God works repentance unto steadfast salvation. It is plain that he who is sorrowful because he has sinned is sorrowful according to God; for he grieves because he did what God hates. This pertains to the steadfastness of salvation. But the sorrow of this world works death. He says this: just as sorrow according to God works life, for one repents and hopes for God's mercy, so sorrow according to the world works death. For the sinner who is found out is sorrowful as one about to be punished, having no one from whom to hope for mercy; and even if for the present there should happen to be no one to punish him, he will nevertheless not be able to escape the judgment of God.

(2 Cor. 7:11) For behold, this very thing, being grieved according to God, how great an earnestness it produces in you. It is true that he who repents is earnest not to sin again. But excuse. Rightly, because repentance has no excuse, but confession. But fear. He shows fear in the sinner who, because of his offense, asks for pardon. But longing. He who knows that he has been made deformed through sin longs to be reformed. But zeal. He who understands that his being rebuked is for his own good begins to feel zeal for accomplishing good works. But vindication. It is necessary that one vindicate him whose affection toward himself he feels; and he takes vengeance on himself who afflicts himself because of his offense. In all things you have shown yourselves to be pure in this matter. Since they are seen to have meditated on all things that pertain to progress toward a better hope, having zeal for the Apostle, their teacher, in all the duties of human conduct, with faith as their guide, they strive to be seen as upright.

(2 Cor. 7:12) Therefore, even if I wrote to you, it was not on account of him who acted wickedly. He calls him who acted wickedly the one who had committed incest; and he also touches those whom in the first letter he indicates had done injury and fraud to their brothers. Nor on account of him who was treated wickedly (1 Cor. 6:7). These were treated wickedly, because they suffered wrongs from their brothers. This is the sense he recalls at the head of the letter, where he says: If you have forgiven anyone anything, I too (2 Cor. 2:10). For he shows that he wrote that they ought to be forgiven not so much for the sake of those who had sinned as rather for the sake of the Church; because when one does evil there are many who are put to shame, and when one suffers insult or fraud there are many who are indignant; for if one member suffers anything, all the members suffer with it. But that our earnestness, which we have for you before God, might be made manifest. By amending the unjust, and sanctifying the polluted, and reconciling them to the Church, he shows that he has earnestness for the whole people, according to what I mentioned above.

(2 Cor. 7:13) Therefore we also received consolation. He says that he received consolation, and through this also gives it. He received it when he learned that those whom he had reproved wished to correct themselves, so as to reform themselves through repentance; and he gives it while he calls them back to the Church, lest, grieved for a long time, they should despair of themselves and turn aside to a public and deadly way of life. But in our consolation we rejoiced still more and more over the joy of Titus, because his spirit was refreshed by all of you. Hearing that those rebuked by the first letter wished to correct themselves, he received consolation. But learning from Titus that they felt grief for their error, he was increased in consolation, filled with joy; because their will had begun to be proved in deed, Titus, a fit witness, reporting this with gladness of heart.

(2 Cor. 7:14) For in whatever I boasted about you, I was not put to shame. Before Titus went to the Corinthians, he heard from them, that is, from the Apostle and those who were with him, that the Corinthians had a good will toward amending their vices; and therefore, when he returned and reported the same things, the Apostle was certainly not put to shame, but was made glad, because Titus had found it no otherwise than he had heard from them.

But as we have spoken all things to you in truth, so also our boasting before Titus has become truth. The Apostle writes these things exulting in spirit, rejoicing in them in such a way as to prove their effectiveness as true as his preaching to them also is, at least in rebuke. For the truthfulness of the one who reproves is seen to be manifest precisely then, if those who are reproved begin to amend themselves; for while those who are rebuked are changed, they bear witness to the one who reproves them.

(2 Cor. 7:15) And his inward affection is more abundantly toward you. He says that the mind and affection of Titus are toward them, because he saw their progress; for the mind of a holy man is in every good thing. As he remembers the obedience of you all, how you received him with fear and trembling. The Corinthians, knowing that Titus had been sent to them by the Apostle, since they had already been rebuked by him for such great vices, were terrified at his coming. And because they had begun to amend their life, they were careful to obey his precepts, so that on his return he might soften the Apostle's mind toward them: this is why he indicates that Titus praised them, for in Titus they revered the Apostle.

(2 Cor. 7:16) I rejoice that in all things I have confidence in you. He was glad not only at their good will, but also at the good works by which they were amending the sins they had committed. Therefore, in all things, he says, I have confidence in you.

Chapter VIII

(2 Cor. 8:1–2) We make known to you, brothers, the grace of God which has been given in the churches of Macedonia, that in much proving of affliction their joy abounds. Since the Macedonians received the word of faith with a devoted mind, he therefore says that grace was given them by God, so that in the tribulation of Paul and Silas, which I mentioned above (2 Cor. 7:5), they should not suffer scandal, but should receive it in exultation of mind, confident of the promised hope, so as to show themselves proved by these sufferings. And that their deep poverty has abounded in the riches of their simplicity. He praises their devotion: because, although they were of slender means, their mind was found rich in the ministry to the saints; for they acted with a pure conscience, not to please men but to please God.

(2 Cor. 8:3) For I bear them witness that according to their strength, and beyond their strength, they were willing. They had given of their means for the ministry as much as was fitting and pleasing to God, who knew their strength. And because they had given themselves to God with their whole heart, they wished to offer more than their strength allowed.

(2 Cor. 8:4) Begging with many prayers to be received into the grace and fellowship of the ministry which is done for the saints. He proclaims that they offered the ministry, which was beyond their strength, with such simplicity and devotion of mind that they offered it entreating with tears, so that even in this way they might compel the acceptance from them of what did not seem fit to be accepted, because it was more than their substance could bear; lest perhaps afterwards want should persuade them to regret their good work. But because they showed themselves such that, with a pure mind already setting present things aside, they strengthened themselves in the future promises by the confidence of faith, it seemed right to accept from them, lest the grace of a good heart should lose its fruit.

(2 Cor. 8:5) And not as we hoped; but rather they gave themselves first to God, then to us by the will of God. Therefore, he says, it was right to accept from them, because, first amending their former errors and the vices of their life and conduct, they devoted themselves to God beyond what was hoped. For it was seen that they did this in simplicity, so that he to whom it was offered need not fear to accept it; because they certainly did not offer it with this intent, that, buying over their overseers, these should tolerate their vices and not reprove them; for gifts blind the eyes and bend the force of authority. Giving themselves therefore to God, while they amend their vices, and then to the brothers, while they offer support, they ought to have been grieved, they who wished to be perfect almost before they had begun. By their example, therefore, he invites the Corinthians, so that, strengthened by this exhortation, they may with diligent mind complete what they had begun.

(2 Cor. 8:6) So that we asked Titus that, as he began, so he would complete in you this grace also. Since he knew that Titus's affection toward them was sincere, and also that they were obedient to him, he therefore indicates that through him he could more easily exhort them to this work also; so that, as in other matters he had the fruit of his exhortation in them, he might have it also in this grace, making them ready for the ministry to the saints; so that, since they were already amending their vices, they might have the fruit of this generosity. For those who give to this end, that they may not be reproved, will have no fruit of it.

(2 Cor. 8:7) But as you abound in all things, in faith, and in word, and in knowledge, and in all earnestness, and moreover in that love which is from us and you, see that you abound more in this grace also. He exhorts them, so that he may boast of them before the other churches. For this is the proof of their amendment, if they are ready for the ministry to the saints.

(2 Cor. 8:8) I do not speak by way of command, but on account of the care of others, and approving the good mind of your love. It is plain that he does not command but exhorts: that they send support to those who suffer need and relieve their care, showing their good mind to God and to men, for which they will without doubt receive a reward.

(2 Cor. 8:9) For you know the grace of our Lord Jesus Christ, that for your sake he became poor, though he was rich, so that by his poverty you might be rich. He says Christ became poor, because God deigned to be born a man, humbling the might of his power, in order to acquire for men the riches of divinity; so that, as the apostle Peter says (2 Pet. 1:4), they might be partakers of the divine nature. He was made man, then, in order to take man up into God, as it is written: I said: You are gods (Ps. 81:6). In this way he exhorts them, by giving generously, to become as it were poor; let their poverty profit, just as Christ's poverty profited us. And Christ indeed became poor for our sake, not for his own; but we, so that it may profit us.

(2 Cor. 8:10–11) And in this I give advice; for this is useful to you, who began not only to do but also to will from the former year: now therefore complete the doing also; so that, as the will is ready in you to will, so may it be also to perform, out of what you have. He says this so that their will may appear in deed, if it is true, according to their strength; that one should give as much as one can and as the mind wills, so that the conscience may be pure: not in pretense, to please men, and so have no reward with God.

(2 Cor. 8:12) For if the will is ready, it is acceptable according to one's means, not according to what one does not have. Since the Corinthians are being called to this work of ministry, this is enjoined on them, that they give no more than they can; lest perhaps, offering more under compulsion, not willingly, they should seem burdened and be left without reward; for he who does anything under compulsion has no reward. For the churches of Macedonia offered of their own accord, with prayers, so as to prove that they were doing this with their whole will, to the point of offering more than they could; and therefore it was acceptable. For the more they offered, the more they will receive. For if someone gives as much as he wills or can, it is accepted; for he is seen to do this by judgment; and therefore let him give as much as he can and as the mind wills, so that he may have a reward for this thing.

(2 Cor. 8:13–14) For it is not that there should be relief for others, but distress for you. It is true, for one should give in such a way as not to cause want to the givers. But out of equality at this time. He says this: that whatever one has, one should share with the saints for the time; for no more is required than what one ought to keep for oneself. For, You shall love, it says, your neighbor as yourself (Lev. 19:18). Hence Zacchaeus: Behold, half, he says, of my goods I give to the poor (Luke 19:8).

That your abundance may go to their want, and their abundance may come to your want. This is what he says: that since, according to the time, the saints suffer want, forsaking all the things of the world and setting themselves to divine works alone, so as to apply themselves to teaching and prayer for the progress of many, those who have believed, and who apply themselves to crafts or business, or at any rate have their fathers' property, should minister to the wants of the saints; and again, the saints, there where they are rich and these are in want, should share with them, as though repaying their ministry in turn, as the Lord says: As long as you did it to one of these least, you did it to me (Matt. 25:40).

So that there may be equality, as it is written. This is the equality: that since these minister to the saints at this time, a return may be made to them in the future; for they make the saints their debtors.

(2 Cor. 8:15) He who had much did not abound, and he who had little did not have less. This is read in Exodus (Exod. 16:18). For the saints have more in the hope of the world to come, and indeed more than those who seem rich at this time; and yet both will be made equal there: so that, just as the want of these saints is sustained by the kindness of those, so by the kindness of the saints these too may become rich in the world to come, in which they seem poor. For they did not give themselves wholly to God, so as to be poor here and rich there; but in this respect, because they are rich here and poor there, they will be enriched by the support of the saints, who, though placed here, are already there in mind. For it is no ordinary merit to minister to the saints from one's just labors.

(2 Cor. 8:16–17) But thanks be to God, who has put this same care for you into the heart of Titus, for he indeed received consolation. God, who is just, knowing that the Corinthians wished to make progress, kindled the affection of Titus toward them, so that by his exhortation he might fulfill their will in good work; and he, seeing their progress, was consoled and rejoiced in them. But being more earnest, he has set out to you of his own accord. That is, seeing them make progress in good actions, he became more earnest regarding their disposition, so much so that he set out of his own accord to those from whom earlier, even when asked, he had excused himself on account of their vices.

(2 Cor. 8:18–19) And we have sent with him our brother, whose praise is in the Gospel throughout all the churches; and not only that, but he was also appointed by the churches as companion of our journey in this grace, which is ministered by us through the glory of the Lord and our care. They commend him because he was unknown to them, so that they might know in how good a reputation they already stood, to whom such men were being sent; so that these might rejoice together with them, increasing them in faith in the working of God, to whose glory the vicars of Christ were earnestly doing this, so that God, the maker of heaven and earth, might be acknowledged in them.

(2 Cor. 8:20) Avoiding this, that anyone should blame us in this abundance which is ministered by us. Since the ministry was in question, he therefore added this, lest he should be judged negligent in caring for the poor or the saints, if he did this more sluggishly. For the apostles had agreed among themselves on this matter, that they should be mindful of the poor, as he indicates to the Galatians. Since, therefore, the Apostle was blameless in all things (Gal. 2:10), lest he be judged careless in this matter, he indicates that he gives this warning beforehand, so that, once this work was fulfilled, his care and providence might appear in all things.

(2 Cor. 8:21) For we provide good things not only before God but also before men. He provides good things before God while he teaches that what God commands concerning the ministry to the saints or the poor should be done; and he provides good things before men, because he sends to encourage this work such men as by their uprightness would not give them scandal, but would spur them on; lest the good teaching of the Apostle should fall into blame through his improvident ministers.

(2 Cor. 8:22–23) And we have sent with them our brother also, whom we have proved in many things to be often earnest, but now much more earnest, with much confidence in you, whether through Titus, who is my partner and helper among you, or through our brothers, apostles of the churches for the glory of Christ. He calls Titus his partner because he too was a bishop, to whom he gave as minister one known in all the churches of that province; to these he adds also a third brother, proved in many good works and earnest. But through the report of Titus, who related the good will of the Corinthians, and of the others who were sent, whom he calls apostles of the churches for the glory of Christ, he shows that he has become more eager to see them and to exhort them to the ministry to the saints. For he received confidence on hearing that the Corinthians had become better. Hence he was eager to go to them, certain that they would obey in doing good works.

(2 Cor. 8:24) Show therefore the display of your love, and of our exultation on your behalf, in them, in the face of the churches. He admonishes them to show their love in those whom he sends, so that they might prove true the good things they had heard about themselves. For they would show that they had profited by the Apostle's admonitions in this way, if they received those sent by him with honor, so that the other churches might recognize that the good things which had begun to be said of them were true. He therefore spurs their minds on, because one of whom good is thought usually shows himself better.

Chapter IX

(2 Cor. 9:1–2) For concerning the ministry that is done for the saints, it is superfluous for me to write to you; for I know your ready will, for which I boast of you among the Macedonians. These too are the words of one coaxing them. For he says that it is superfluous for him to write about the ministry that is done for the saints, so that he may be seen to think well of them: for it is superfluous to remind one whom you know will do it. But to show his diligence, it is necessary that he write, both to make them readier and to make plain what he proclaims about them to the others. For superfluous things usually produce greater care; for the Lord too, not doubting the love of the apostle Peter toward himself, says to him a third time: Simon son of John, do you love me (John 21:17)? This threefold repetition seems superfluous, as it were, but it serves the completeness of the admonition, so that he may know that he must attend with great diligence to what is enjoined frequently. Since Achaia has been ready from the former year. After the Macedonians, Achaia, that is, the churches of the province of Achaia (not all, since the Corinthians too are Achaeans), had prepared themselves, led by emulation of the Macedonians, to minister to the saints. By saying this he exhorts the Corinthians the more, when he says that those were ready from the former year, but were now waiting for this: that their ministry be divided according to the need of each one. And your emulation has stirred up many. He says that after the Macedonians Achaia was ready, then that the Corinthians too were to become ready according to their promise, while the other churches, through emulation of the Corinthians, when they heard that these had earlier been entangled in many errors, but afterward, correcting themselves, had come to be of good will, were incited to the good work; because if those who had conducted themselves badly after receiving the faith had begun to have this will, how much more these, in whom these vices were not found.

(2 Cor. 9:3) But I have sent the brothers, so that our glory, by which we set you forward, may not be made empty in this respect; that, as I said, you may be ready. He mentions the brothers whom he mentioned above, that is, Titus and those he joined to him as helpers, who were to exhort them more earnestly to fulfill their promise in the ministry of the saints, so that not only by letters but also face to face they might coax them with frequent admonitions, lest the exultation of the Apostle, by which he boasted of the splendor of their mind, be made void. By mentioning this more often, then, he shows his care for them, not wishing them to be put to shame.

(2 Cor. 9:4) Lest, when the Macedonians come with me and find you unprepared, we blush, not to say all of you, in this respect. It is plain that if what he testified about them is not found, he himself would blush for them, and they would be the more confounded, who did not strive, even because the Apostle had stood forth as their witness, to do what would make it true, if only on account of the person of so great a man.

(2 Cor. 9:5) I therefore thought it necessary to ask the brothers to go ahead to you and to prepare this promised blessing of yours to be ready: but thus, as a blessing, not as an overreaching. To show his care still further, he said that he had asked the brothers, so that, admonished through them, they might fulfill what they had promised and were not fulfilling. Although above he says that Titus set out of his own accord, that is, that it was not extorted from him, but as soon as he heard, he gladly embraced it, so that he might be seen to have set out to them willingly because of his hope in them: now he signifies that he did not ask these because they were unwilling to go; but he proves his affection toward them when he asks them besides, though they are willing to go, so that what both they wish and he begs may be done without any delay. This, then, is what he is doing with them: that they be faithful in their promise, and that he admonishes them for the sake of their reputation, not to overreach them; but that they offer only so much as they will not regret.

(2 Cor. 9:6–7) But this I say: that he who sows sparingly will also reap sparingly. By the sparing man he means the miser, from whom it is extorted that he contribute; for he regrets that he promised. This he infers from their slowness, who had promised long ago and were deliberating a long time. For this sparing man the harvest is small, because he sows with doubt; for he does not know that what he does profits him. But he who sows in blessing will also reap of blessing. He sows in blessing who does this with good will, in hope of a future reward. Each one according to the purpose of his heart, not out of sadness or necessity; for God loves a cheerful giver. He teaches them that this profits for the future only if it is done with a glad mind. For of those who contribute, God has chosen this one to be rewarded, who works with a devoted heart, as if laying up treasure with God; because he who does it unwillingly on account of present shame, lest he be found disgraceful while others contribute, has no reward.

(2 Cor. 9:8) God is able to make all grace abound in you. He prays that the power of God may be present to them, so that, just as in amending their vices and by the truth of doctrine it pricks their hearts, so in this too it may favor their undertakings, that they may abound in every good work through the grace of God. That, always having all sufficiency in all things, you may abound in every good work. He says and prays this: that in every matter they may always be sufficient for themselves by God's bidding, and not lack the things that are necessary for their well-being. If, then, they choose for themselves sufficiency alone, they will be able to abound in God's work; for, keeping for themselves what suffices, they must spend the rest on the uses of the saints or of the poor: and this will be to abound in every good work. For although what a man of slender means gives is small, yet it abounds, because it is done with right judgment, and it is asked not only how much, but also out of how much, and with what mind it is given. Indeed, that widow in the Gospel (Luke 21:3) was praised for a little, and those who were casting in much were not praised. For her little is much, because she cast in more than she could; but the rich cast in less, though they could do more; and therefore the small amount of her who cast in beyond her strength was found to be more than the much of the rich, because they cast in out of what they had in abundance. He, then, who gives as much as he can does rightly: yet he has that widow ahead of him, because she cast in all that she had.

(2 Cor. 9:9) As it is written: He has scattered, he has given to the poor; his justice remains forever. This is written in the hundred and eleventh psalm, and by the example he adds to their care for the poor, so that they may abound in every good work. For if the justice of this man who gives generously to the poor remains forever, how much more that of him who ministers to the saints? For those can be called poor who are publicly needy; but the saints are distinguished from these, because these are servants of God, devoting themselves to prayers and fasts, leading a pure life, as did Anna the prophetess (Luke 2:37), who did not depart from the temple, serving with fasts and prayers night and day, setting aside all care of the world. This mercy, then, has been called justice, because he who gives generously, knowing that God gives all things in common to all, since his sun rises for all, and he rains for all, and he has given the earth to all, therefore shares with those who do not have the abundance of the earth, lest they seem deprived of God's benefits. He, then, is just who does not keep for himself alone what he knows was given to all: and just not only in this time, but also forever, because in the world to come he will have it with him perpetually.

(2 Cor. 9:10–11) Now he who supplies seed to the sower will also supply bread to eat, and will multiply your seed, and will increase the fruit of your justice; that you may be enriched in all things in all simplicity. All things are God's, and both seeds and the things that spring up grow by God's bidding and are multiplied for the uses of men: God, then, who gives these things, himself also commands that a share of them be given to those who are in need; and therefore he who gives according to God's will cannot fail to be increased by God's bidding with increments of this kind, to increase the fruit of justice; for he adds to the giver, so that he may have the more from which he can give generously. This is justice: that because God gives, man too should give back out of it to him who lacks. For he wants these to be ministers of those who, choosing better things, spurn the riches of the present time, binding themselves wholly to the things of God. For God grants help to each one according to his good will; for the saints, who wish to possess nothing here except to have food and clothing, God has appointed those who wish to have these things to be their guardians, so that he who has ministered to them well and with simplicity may be increased by God's bidding, so that he may always have something from which to give generously, being enriched both for the present and for the future. For sowing once, he will reap twice; so that, if he has regard for future hopes, he will not wish to have them subject to himself, but will rather bow himself to them, because he will receive the reward for this from God. For if he wishes them to be humbled before him in the present, he loses the hope of the future; for he receives the reward of his work here, not doing this with simplicity, as the Lord says: Amen I say to you, they have received their reward (Matt. 6:16).

(2 Cor. 9:12–13) Which works thanksgiving to God through us. Since the ministry of this service not only supplies the things that the saints lack, but will also abound through the thanksgivings of many in God, through the proof of this ministry, glorifying God in the subjection of your confession in the Gospel of Christ, and in the simplicity of your sharing toward them, and their prayers for you, as they long to see you because of the superabundant grace of God in you. But thanks be to God for his unspeakable gift. He says this because whoever, through the stewards of God's gift, does this work by which thanks are given to God by those who receive the necessary support in the name of Christ, subject not to beggary but to God, by whose goods they know they are fed: thanks are returned to them not only by these, but also by the rest of the brothers, who give thanks to God for them, commending their deed to the Lord; so that, giving to a few, they are commended to God by the entreaties of many. For, approving their mind in this work, they magnify the Lord in them, strengthened by hope in whom they obey the Gospel of Christ in subjection of mind: not wishing to have them humbled before them in order to supply what is necessary for the saints. And this will be to give thanks in simplicity for their sharing, commending themselves to their prayers and to their desires. For who would not long to see with his eyes those who, for the name of God, are made subject to his needs? Accordingly they are called gifts of God; for he who ministers to these gives to God; because what is given is credited to him who commands it to be done. But they give thanks to God in these things, and for his ineffable gift. For it is the gift of God that incites men to the good work; for the promised hope spurs them on to the ministry spoken of above.

Chapter X

(2 Cor. 10:1) But I Paul myself beseech you by the meekness and modesty of Christ, I who to your face am indeed humble among you, but when absent am confident toward you. That is what he now says: that he is such when absent as he is also when present. For he was not humble to their face through flattery or through subjection in any matter, he who was so zealous for his constancy that he sometimes refrained even from lawful things, lest he be made to stoop. Hence he says in another letter: All things are lawful for me, but I will be brought under the power of none. Food for the belly, and the belly for food; but God will destroy both it and them (1 Cor. 6:13). Therefore, then, when absent he beseeches them and interposes the modesty of Christ, lest when present he be found harsher, because of their men, than he is in the letter; so that those who had not yet amended themselves, composing themselves, might soften the Apostle's rigor toward them; so that, finding them restrained, he might hold back his severity and rejoice in them. By assurance, therefore, he signified the confidence of severity, which perhaps he was thought to have only in a letter, but not when present, face to face; because in the first letter he seems severe and too rigid: and therefore he warns them, lest he use the same severity when present, as they were not expecting.

(2 Cor. 10:2) But I ask that, when present, I may not be bold with that confidence with which I am thought to be bold against some who think that we walk according to the flesh. It is the same sense, which he repeats in order to make it clear; for he prays that he may find them such that he is not compelled to be angry, but may rest with them. Here, then, he has disclosed what he signified above, and he shows it still more fully in what follows: that this assurance, which here he called confidence, is that of the power given to him by Christ, whose vicar he is, to punish those who, acting under the name of Christ, do not obey so as to correct the things for which they are reproved. He means those who, not receiving the spiritual things that were said by the Apostle, thought of him as though he were speaking carnal things that were not to be received. Therefore he says: Who think that we walk according to the flesh; because he who spurns spiritual things thinks them carnal.

(2 Cor. 10:3) For though walking in the flesh, we do not wage war according to the flesh. That is, though placed in the body, we live spiritually; for he lives spiritually who does what pleases God. He, then, wages war according to the flesh who obeys the desires of the flesh; for all error is called flesh. But he wages war for Christ who obeys him in faith and discipline.

(2 Cor. 10:4) For the weapons of our warfare are not carnal, but strong for God. Strong for this reason, because they are incorrupt; for all carnal things are corrupt. Weapons, then, for this reason, because they fight against vices, bearing the standard of their emperor Christ, who is the enemy of vices; for just as an emperor defends his realm through his soldiers, so too the Savior, through us his servants, defends the confession and discipline of the one God.

(2 Cor. 10:5) For the destruction of fortifications, destroying thoughts, and every height that exalts itself against the knowledge of God. The spiritual weapons are the faith of incorrupt preaching. Through these God conquers the principalities and powers, which, it is evident, exalt themselves against the faith of Christ in order to usurp rule for themselves, inserting themselves into the thoughts of men so as to call them away from the dominion of God; and these thoughts the truth of faith destroys. By fortifications, then, he means the principalities and powers and the spiritual forces of wickedness, which exalt themselves and arm the minds of unbelievers to speak against Christ: against these the law of God fights, scattering their counsels. And bringing every understanding into captivity, and leading it to the obedience of Christ. He takes the understanding captive when he overcomes by reason one who speaks against him, and leads him, humble and meek, to the faith of Christ, which he had previously resisted.

(2 Cor. 10:6) And ready in all things to punish all disobedience, when your obedience has been fulfilled. It is plain that he punishes disobedience when he condemns it through obedience, destroying it at the moment when he leads those who resist to the faith; so that faithlessness is condemned by those by whom it used to be defended.

(2 Cor. 10:7) Look at the things that are before your face. Now he wants them to consider the things that are in plain view, that is, the things he is about to say, because they are open to judgment. He says, then: If anyone is confident that he is a servant of Christ, let him consider this again within himself, that as he is Christ's, so are we also. He speaks to those whose pride he touches, who, presuming upon themselves with a puffed-up mind, thought less of the Apostle than was fitting, as though they had no need of his precepts. These he admonishes: that if they were confident about themselves, because they were servants of Christ, they certainly ought not to have doubts about the Apostle, but ought to think at least the same of him, though he is unequal to them, when they certainly ought rather to prefer to themselves the teacher of the Gentiles. But here he teaches them humility in his own person, making himself equal to them, though he was a vessel of election and the teacher of the Gentiles in faith and truth. This, then, he wants them to consider, which is certainly seen in clear light: that there is none of the believers who would not esteem the Apostle more than himself; how much more, then, ought no one to esteem him less than himself? This, then, he solemnly declares and teaches, lest through elation of mind they lose the merit of a good life. For let him who knows that he is something humble himself, that he may become greater.

(2 Cor. 10:8) For even if I boast somewhat more of our power, which the Lord gave us for building up, and not for your destruction, I shall not blush. Now he signifies that above he humbled himself by comparing himself with them, whereas if he were to raise himself up he would not blush, because he received from the Lord the power of preaching, to save, not to destroy, those who obey. If, then, this man, who was sent by the Lord, bows himself and compares himself with inferiors, how much more ought those who had no testimony at all not to compare themselves, not to say prefer themselves, to their betters?

(2 Cor. 10:9) But that we may not be thought to frighten you, as it were, by letters. He can be seen to frighten by letters who is neither of any authority nor, when present, has the confidence to reprove: but he is bold when absent for this reason, because when present he is afraid.

(2 Cor. 10:10–11) For the letters indeed are weighty and strong, but the bodily presence weak and the speech contemptible. Let him who is such consider this: that such as we are in word through letters when absent, such we are also in deed when present. He proves that he is not other when absent than he is when present for this reason, because he was not presumptuous. For he had received power from the Lord; for one to whom power has not been given can be bold when absent, but when present he suffers shame. Therefore the Apostle does not blush when he rebukes, because he does this in the confidence of his power. He says these things on account of those who, without being sent, were preaching with flattery, because they wanted to commend themselves.

(2 Cor. 10:12) For we do not dare to join or compare ourselves with some who commend themselves. Those who commend themselves are those who, without having received power, want to lord it, claiming authority for their own name. For he who is sent claims power not for himself but for him by whom he is sent: by which he also shows that he was chosen by the Lord as a steward. Thus, then, he does not join or compare himself with those who preach without being sent, since he presumes nothing beyond what has been granted.

(2 Cor. 10:13) But measuring ourselves by ourselves, and comparing ourselves with ourselves, we will not boast beyond measure, but according to the measure of the rule which God has apportioned to us, a measure reaching even to you. He says that he uses as much power as has been granted by the Author, and does not go beyond the measure. Therefore, he says, our boasting will not be proud, since we do not overstep the limit of the power given. He put glory in place of the authority which he used in rebuking sins, so as to save them. Yet he does not say that he boasts in exalting his power, but for their building up. For he shows a power of which, if he wished to boast, he would not be ashamed, because it was given to him by the Lord. But he uses it in such a way as not to boast except in the progress of believers, rebuking the vicious with constancy, so that he may boast in their salvation, so that the power given may serve salvation, not elation. In a twofold way, then, he testifies that he does not boast beyond measure: both because it is according to the power given, as he said, and because he did not claim authority for himself beyond where his preaching resounds. For he was confident about those whom he himself had founded in the faith of Christ. For he says that to each one were apportioned those to whom he was directed in preaching, so that each might have certain cities of his own, of whose faith he might boast. For by God's bidding this Apostle was called to preach to the Macedonians; and he was admonished by the Lord to evangelize the Corinthians. To these, then, he speaks boldly, whom he himself founded and to whom he came by God's bidding; to the rest, not with such confidence, because they were in another's rule, that is, in the portion of another evangelist.

(2 Cor. 10:14) For we are not overextending ourselves as though we did not reach as far as you: that is, we are not overextending ourselves in preaching as though we had not come as far as you, but, being appointed, with God sending us, we came to you. Now to overextend oneself is to be extended further than is granted, but not further; because God has given a measure which his evangelists were to keep, and this he testifies that he guards. For he shows this so that they may know that he was directed to them by God; and therefore that they ought to obey his admonitions, lest by despising them they should seem to resist God, by whom he was sent.

(2 Cor. 10:15) For we have come as far as you in the Gospel of Christ, not boasting beyond measure in others' labors. Here what he says appears more plainly: that he himself had founded them. Therefore he speaks to them with such confidence; for they had not been won to the faith by another's labor, as he says in another letter: For in Christ through the Gospel I have begotten you (1 Cor. 4:15). He, then, does not boast beyond measure who boasts in his own labor.

But having hope, as your faith grows, of being magnified in you. Because he hopes for the fruit of the labor by which their faith was won, therefore he professes that he does not do this negligently, so as not to give way to weariness and troubles; because he believed that he would be magnified before God if his hearers increased in faith, as the Lord says: Father, as you sent me into this world, I also have sent them into this world, and for them I sanctify myself (John 17:18). He says this because the testimony of a teacher lies in obedient disciples; hence he says:

(2 Cor. 10:16) To be magnified in you according to our rule in abundance, and that I may evangelize even in the regions that are beyond you. Gladder because of their faith, he professes that he must of necessity preach beyond them, to increase the glory of his preaching, where it had not yet been preached. That we may not boast in things prepared in another's rule. It is plain that a prudent man does not trust in, nor hope for glory from, things that rest on the labors of others. Therefore the Apostle too is not bold toward those who believed through the preaching of others, lest he seem to boast in others' labors; but he strives for this: to preach to those to whom it had not been announced, so as to seek glory by his own labor.

(2 Cor. 10:17) But he who boasts, let him boast in the Lord. By saying this he signified that this very assurance and glory must be given to the Lord, whose grace it is; so that he who is confident in the power of the Gospel may be confident with God as its author; so that all the gain of the divine business may accrue as profit to the Lord. But he who does not receive power from God cannot boast in the Lord, because he seeks his own glory.

(2 Cor. 10:18) For it is not he who commends himself that is approved, but he whom the Lord commends. It is true that the Lord commends him, and he is approved, whom he holds worthy and sends to preach his gift; but him whom he does not send, he does not commend. And he commends himself who preaches without being sent; and therefore he is not fit, but presumptuous and reprobate.

Chapter XI

(2 Cor. 11:1) Would that you would bear a little of my folly! But do bear with me too. As he begins to tell true things about himself, he calls himself foolish, because this seems to pertain to his own praise. For which reason it has been said: Let not your own lips praise you, but your neighbors (Prov. 27:2). But here he is driven by grief on account of those who, though they ought to have thought well of him above the others, thought of him unworthily.

(2 Cor. 11:2) For I am jealous for you with the jealousy of God; for I have prepared you to present you as a chaste virgin to one husband, to Christ. He shows that what he is about to say he will say out of love for them, so that it may serve not so much his own praise as their progress: that they may learn how they ought to support their parent in the Gospel; because the disparagement of a father is the detraction of his sons, and the praise of sons is the glory of the father (Sir. 3:13). He wants them, then, to be virgins in faith; hence he also jealously keeps the corrupters of faith away from them, so that on the day of judgment he may present them undefiled to Christ the judge. Hence it is that in the Apocalypse of John too one reads: These are, he says, those who have not been defiled with women; for they have remained virgins. These follow the Lamb wherever he goes (Rev. 14:4). By women he signified error, because error began through a woman, just as he also speaks of the woman Jezebel on account of the wife of Ahab, who out of zeal for Baal killed the prophets of God (1 Kings 18:40); since what is to be understood is idolatry, by which morals are corrupted, and the truth of faith as well. For if you understand it of women, so as to think that they are called virgins because they kept their bodies undefiled, you exclude the saints from this glory; for all the apostles, except John and Paul, had wives. And see whether it is fitting to accuse the apostle Peter, who is the first among the apostles; how much more among the rest?

(2 Cor. 11:3) But I fear lest, as the serpent seduced Eve by his cunning, so your minds be corrupted from the chastity that is in Christ Jesus. He shows for what reason he is compelled to declare true things about himself; for he professes the glory given to him for this purpose, not to praise himself, but to discredit those who under the name of Christ were preaching against Christ, by whom the Corinthians were being led astray; and this truth would profit them more than him. For he did this so that the seducers should not be listened to, whom he compared to the devil because of the cunning of their malice; for they try to corrupt the hearts of the faithful away from the truth of Christ. Fearing this, then, he is always anxious about them, lest they be violated.

(2 Cor. 11:4) For if he who comes preaches another Christ, whom we did not preach; or you receive another Spirit, whom you did not receive; or another Gospel, which you did not accept, you would rightly bear it. Why does he say, You would rightly bear it, if another Christ had been preached to you, or if another Spirit or another Gospel had been delivered to you, when he says to the Galatians: If anyone announces to you anything besides what you have received, let him be anathema (Gal. 1:9)? If it was perverse for the Galatians to receive anything other than what had been handed down by the apostles, how was it right for the Corinthians if another Christ had been preached to them? But the Apostle, knowing that no other Christ is ever preached than this one who was crucified, therefore says: You would rightly bear it, if another, greater Christ had been preached to you by those who come. To the Galatians, however, he pronounced anathema if they heard anything else, which would surely not be greater, but contrary. Furthermore, he says, if the same Christ is preached by them who is announced by us, and this same Gospel, what reason is there that we should be held inferior, when nothing more is learned from them? Above he speaks of the false apostles, corrupters of the faith, but in this place of true preachers; because the people of the Corinthians were tossed about by various errors, so that some favored the false apostles, some those from whom they heard, in polished words, the same things they had heard from the Apostle; while others favored the apostles, because they had been with the Lord, disparaging their own apostle Paul, because he had not seen the Lord in the flesh.

(2 Cor. 11:5) For I reckon that I have been in nothing inferior to those who are exceedingly apostles. He does not want the grace of God in him to seem inferior; because he taught in like manner, and did the same things that the apostles did, who seemed of greater weight, because they had begun to teach earlier and had been with the Lord, whence they were thought to be of greater authority; whereas the election of this apostle was proved by a notable testimony, and he himself labored more than the others, and saw the Lord while praying in the temple, and spoke with him.

(2 Cor. 11:6) But even if I am unskilled in speech, yet not in knowledge. This does not pertain to the apostles, because they were not eloquent, being as they were without letters, yet full of the Holy Spirit; but he touches those whom the Corinthians preferred on account of their polished speech; whereas in religion what is needed is the force of speech, not a sweet sound. How, though, does he humble himself so as to say that he is unskilled in speech? And where is that which Festus says: Paul, he says, Paul, much learning makes you senseless (Acts 26:24)? In saying this, then, he did not wish to be understood as not knowing how to speak, but he said it on account of those who wished to be commended not through faith but through eloquence. And yet he called himself unskilled in that which carries no guilt; but in that which acquires salvation, and ignorance of which is not without guilt, he did not declare himself unskilled; because he who is unskilled in speech is not guilty before God, but he who does not have the knowledge of God is guilty of ignorance; for it is not permitted to be ignorant, above all of what pertains to salvation. Therefore the Apostle, despising eloquence, aimed at this, that faith should be acceptable with power as its witness; so that it would not be eloquence that commended faith, but power, to which eloquence yields. But in all things I am manifest to you. He says that he is manifest to the Corinthians in all things; because they were not unaware that he also had skill in speaking, and, knowing this, they favored others in a matter in which they did not know him to be inferior: and therefore he speaks with feeling, because this was worse: that though they knew him to be lacking in nothing, they abased him beneath the others, when they had seen through him not only the preaching fulfilled, but also apostolic signs and wonders done.

(2 Cor. 11:7) Or did I commit a sin in humbling myself so that you might be exalted, because I preached the Gospel of God among you free of charge? Since they were puffed up because he had been unwilling to accept support from them, and on account of this they set others before him; therefore he says, Did I sin, because I humbled myself so as to judge myself unworthy to receive; because you are angry with me for this, when this is not a matter of sin, but of glory? Thus he humbled himself in this way, when he did not exact what was owed to him. For it was owed to him that he should receive support from those to whom he preached the Gospel of God, as the Lord established, saying: The laborer is worthy of his pay (Luke 10:7); but so that it might serve their advantage, he refused it. For he refused the support offered for two reasons: so that he would not be found like the false apostles, who were preaching not for the glory of God but for their own advantage, so that they would not get an occasion for plundering, for when one does not accept, the other cannot demand much; and so that the vigor of the Gospel's authority would not grow numb; for he who receives from those who sin loses the authority to reprove. Thus, then, they are exalted, when nothing is accepted from them precisely so that, being rebuked, they may be amended, raised from death to life.

(2 Cor. 11:8) I despoiled other churches, taking wages for the ministry to you. And when I was with you and in need, I was a burden to no one. He weighs upon them; for he shows not only that he was unwilling to receive from them, but also that he received from others and ministered to them. And to show that this was much, not little, he says: I despoiled other churches for the ministry to you. He indicates, then, that he spent no small amount on them; for when he says I despoiled, he indicates many things received and given, not extorted. Hence he added: taking wages; for wages are what a man serving as a soldier receives for his expenses, in place of pay. So he made them blush; because he surely did not refuse out of guile, since he himself ministered to them, and so that through this they might more easily fulfill the ministry they had promised, knowing that they were debtors; so that their ministry would no longer be very glorious, but owed; lest they should boast even of that.

(2 Cor. 11:9) And when I was with you and in need, I was a burden to no one. He wished to profit them so much that he consoled himself in his own want for the sake of their salvation. For, suffering want, he was content, so that they would not suffer any stumbling block. For he who gives thinks that he is exalted, and no longer allows himself to be reproved. For the brothers who came from Macedonia supplied my need. From the Macedonians he always received, because they had first corrected their vices. And just as he was not to receive from the Corinthians, lest it turn into a stumbling block for them, so again he was to receive from the Macedonians, lest, sowing well, they lose the fruit.

(2 Cor. 11:10) And in all things I have kept myself from being a burden to you, and will keep myself so. He says that he will always persist in this, so that they may thus correct themselves, on account of the false apostles, who were seeking an occasion to plunder them.

The truth of Christ is in me, that this glory shall not be broken in me in the regions of Achaia. He exhorts all Achaia to minister to the saints, but says that he does not allow it for himself, lest he fail to complete the glory he had begun; for it is glory to abstain from lawful things, above all for the salvation of others. In the regions of Achaia, then, he would not make use of it; in the others he did; and further he promises under the witness of Christ, saying that he will persist in it, so that he would never receive from the regions of Achaia.

(2 Cor. 11:11) Why? Because I do not love you? God knows. Having given his reason, he also gives the knowledge of God as witness of the love which he had toward them; that it was not because he did not love them that he was unwilling to accept, but rather the more because he loved them, so that this very thing might benefit them, as I have said.

(2 Cor. 11:12) But what I do, I will also go on doing, so as to cut off the occasion of those who want an occasion, that in what they boast of they may be found just as we are. The glory of the false apostles lay in receiving money. Therefore the Apostle refused it, lest he seem like them; because unless this had been avoided by the Apostle, a greater occasion would have been given them of receiving, or of extorting even more, since in this matter they would point to the example given by the Apostle.

(2 Cor. 11:13) For such false apostles are deceitful workers, transforming themselves into apostles of Christ. They are deceitful because they preached Christ for the sake of avarice, not with a religious mind nor as men sent, usurping for themselves the apostolic name.

(2 Cor. 11:14) And no wonder, for Satan himself is transformed as an angel of light. It is plain that Satan frequently deceives many, showing himself to them as an angel of God, so as to deceive them. Hence Joshua son of Nun, being anxious, asked who it was that appeared to him (Josh. 5:13). And the apostles in the boat, terrified, thought it was a phantom. Hence the apostle Peter, when the Lord said: Do not fear, it is I (Matt. 14:27), not readily believing, said: If it is you, bid me come to you upon the waters (ibid., 28), so that, if he had set his foot firmly upon the waters, he would know it to be true that what appeared was not a phantom.

(2 Cor. 11:15) It is no great thing, then, if his ministers also are transformed as ministers of righteousness. The ministers of righteousness are the apostles, whose companions these men falsely claimed to be, so as to deceive their hearers. Whose end will be according to their works. Without doubt, because such men will be judged according to the wickedness of their mind; for not for God's sake but for their own belly's sake they pretend to be ministers of God. Yet he says that their end is according to their works: so that, just as the works and the care of the belly perish, so these too may be understood to perish.

(2 Cor. 11:16) Again I say, let no one think me foolish; otherwise, receive me as foolish, so that I too may boast a little. Above (2 Cor. 11:1) he already said: Would that you would bear a little of my folly; this he now repeats; for he always sets forth something that he follows up later, after much has been put in between. What, then, he set forth above, he now begins to recount; for, being about to say true things, he calls himself as it were foolish, because they seem to pertain to his praise. So that I too, he says, may boast a little. He said "a little" because it lies in the words, not in the meaning. He does not, then, truly say these things for his own glory, but wishes to show them that those whom they think glorious have nothing more than he has: and so, if these are thought glorious, so am I, he says. He therefore says that it is a fool's part to pursue anything for his own praise, but that he is compelled to speak so. For it grieves him that they should think those men exalted, with him despised, when they had nothing more than he.

(2 Cor. 11:17) What I speak, I do not speak according to God. He does not speak according to God, because these things pertain to the swelling of the flesh, and glory before God does not arise from being puffed up; for before God humility is glorious. But as in foolishness, in this substance of boasting. He did not speak of full foolishness, but of partial; for it is the truth. But because self-exaltation is a faulty thing, therefore, although what is said is true, it is nevertheless as it were foolishness for anyone to praise himself. But it is full foolishness if someone praises himself with false things: for the former is proud about what is true, the latter a fool about what is false; the substance of the boasting in this case, however, is carnal; for as the flower of grass, so this glory falls.

(2 Cor. 11:18) Since many boast according to the flesh, I too will boast. He says this because some of the Jews who believed were presuming on the fact that they were sons of Abraham. This is to boast according to the flesh: to claim for oneself the nobility of the flesh. For on this ground they defend for themselves the prerogative of their race; because those are called his sons who have believed God: Since the soul that has sinned, it shall die (Ezek. 18:20). If, then, this is glory, he says, I too will boast; for I too am a son of Abraham. But it profits nothing before God to be a son of Abraham; for it is faith that saves, not descent from the race of Abraham. And therefore this glory is a glory of the flesh, as if, for instance, it were said: a noble son of a man.

(2 Cor. 11:19–20) For you gladly bear with the foolish, though you yourselves are wise. He calls foolish those who boasted of the circumcision of the flesh; and since they were borne with by the Corinthians so as to seem glorious, the Apostle therefore wishes himself also to be borne with by them, so that he too might show that he was what they were, and yet that he did not boast on this score according to God; hence he calls this glory foolishness. For he says that he boasts according to the flesh; but he professes that this is foolish, so that he might empty this glory, and he shows that he was compelled to break out into praise of the flesh. This, therefore, is irony. For how are they prudent who are often rebuked? Does not the foolishness of the Apostle have more wisdom than the prudence of these men? For the wise man here is one who bears with the imprudent, who endures them by his patience so that they may make progress, lest by provoking them or casting them off he make them worse, or at any rate, if he cannot make progress in them, he guards his own moderation. Of these things there is nothing in the Corinthians. How, then, do they gladly endure the imprudent, when they themselves are wise, unless because he does not want it to be understood as the words sound? This lies in the sense and in the delivery; because the same words do not have the same sense when he pronounces them otherwise, so that at one time he affirms, at another denies, what he says. For these, as prudent men, were bearing with the foolish false apostles. For you bear it if someone brings you into slavery. This is true; because they were being brought into slavery, not through prudence but through foolishness, so as to serve the false apostles. If someone devours. Nor is this false, because they were content that their goods should be devoured by the false teachers. If someone takes. To take is to catch someone by guile; and so he means that these were caught by deceitful teachers, and bore what was not expedient. If someone is lifted up. Being lifted up is pride. He touches, then, those who were proud, wrongly censuring the Apostle; and to these they were not displeasing. If someone strikes you in the face. He is struck in the face upon whose face an insult is inflicted. Now he means those who, because they were of the race of Abraham, disparaged them for being uncircumcised, preferring themselves and humbling them.

(2 Cor. 11:21) I speak according to ignobility. He calls "ignobility" what he said above: And I will boast according to the flesh (2 Cor. 11:18). For he does not say that it pertains to praise that he was not weak in that in which those men boasted, whom he touches covertly. Hence he also added: As if we had been weak in this respect. It is plain that he was not weak in this glory in which the believing Jews preferred themselves, whom these were tolerating, as if their Apostle were unlike them in this matter; because he himself too was of the seed of Abraham, and so he was not weak in this glory of the flesh. Those, however, were judged weak who had been attached to the race of Abraham. For these, as being weak in that in which those preferred themselves, because they were Israelites, wish to be borne with in this very thing. To show that in this glory of the flesh they were not unlike them and weak, when he speaks of his own case he also brings in the person of Barnabas, his companion. Hence in the first epistle too he says: Or do I alone and Barnabas not have the power to do this (1 Cor. 9:6)? They were not, then, lesser in this glory in which the Jews boasted, but they did not applaud themselves on that account. Finally he says: In what anyone dares, I speak in foolishness, I too dare: that is, that he was not weak in the glory in which the Israelites boasted; nor yet would he be declared prudent in this matter; for the prudent do not want to prefer themselves. He says, therefore, that he dares in foolishness in that in which those dare who boast that they are sons of Abraham, so as to empty their glory: he declares himself imprudent, lest they should seem prudent in this respect, or noble.

(2 Cor. 11:22) They are Hebrews, and so am I: they are Israelites, and so am I: they are the seed of Abraham, and so am I. In these things he shows himself the equal of those whom he indicated above, so as to show to be false their detractions, by which he was judged unworthy by them.

(2 Cor. 11:23) They are ministers of Christ, and I, I speak as one foolish, I more. Saying true things about himself, he declares himself foolish, so that he may be understood to have been compelled to burst out into his own praise. For one who of his own accord relates things that serve his praise does not call himself imprudent. He shows, then, what the things are that make him more a minister of Christ than the other preachers are. For, having found the occasion, he touches the persons of all, even of the apostles, grieving that the grace of God was judged lesser in him in this respect, though he worked the same things as the other apostles, and for the sake of the faith endured greater deadly perils. Hence in another epistle too he says: I have labored more than all of them (1 Cor. 15:10).

In very many labors. He says "in more labors" for this reason: because they too labored, but not so much. For he sought his food with his own hands from morning until the fifth hour, and from then until the tenth hour he disputed publicly with such great labor as to persuade those who contradicted him. In prisons more abundantly. It is evident that he was sent to prison more frequently than they were. In stripes beyond measure. "Beyond measure" for this reason: because more than was fitting. For they too were beaten, but not so much. In deaths more often. It is true, because he endured dangers more frequently than the others.

(2 Cor. 11:24) From the Jews five times I received forty, less one. He says this because he was flogged five times by the Jews according to the law of Moses, receiving thirty-nine stripes, as is written in Deuteronomy (Deut. 25:3). What was done on five occasions, then, he here reports in sum.

(2 Cor. 11:25) Three times I was beaten with rods. This he suffered from the Gentiles. Once I was stoned. He was stoned by the Jews in a city of Lycaonia (Acts 14:18). Three times I suffered shipwreck. For one who is read to have sailed at all times, it was easy, with adverse storms, to suffer shipwreck three times. A night and a day I was in the depth of the sea. This happened when he was sent to Rome, having appealed to Caesar (Acts 25:12). Then, despairing of life, he was on the deep, that is, in the depth of the sea, having death before his eyes. For so it is written in that very place: So that we despaired even of living (2 Cor. 1:8).

(2 Cor. 11:26) In journeys often. Now he recalls other perils, to show that he was harassed for Christ on land and sea according to the flesh. In perils, he says, of rivers. If he is recounting the perils of journeys, why is it that he says: In perils of rivers? But traveling in winter, since rains are of course continual, he incurred danger when the rivers overflowed. In perils from robbers. When the devil could not kill him in the city, he stirred up robbers against him on the road, though he carried nothing that robbers would desire. In perils from my own people. He means the persecutions of the Jews, who were his brothers according to the flesh; for they grieved that, having deserted the Law, he had turned to the Gospel of Christ. In perils from the Gentiles. From the Gentiles he suffered persecutions on account of the mystery of the incarnation of our Lord Jesus Christ, and the preaching of one God. In perils in the city. So great was the peril in the city that he escaped through a window from the hands of those lying in wait for him (2 Cor. 11:33). In perils in the desert. This peril was contrived by the Jews when, having laid an ambush, they wanted to kill him on the road (Acts 27:43). In perils in the sea. He already said above: Three times I suffered shipwreck, a night and a day I was in the depth of the sea; what other peril at sea was there? But this is the peril: when at sea, that is, in the ship, the soldiers had planned to kill all the prisoners, lest anyone should swim away and escape. The centurion prevented this peril from being inflicted, lest Paul be killed, so that he might bring him alive to Rome. In perils among feigned brothers. False brothers are those of whom he speaks to the Galatians: But because of the false brothers brought in secretly, who slipped in to spy out our liberty which we have in Christ Jesus (Gal. 2:4). For these were partly Christians, partly Jews, perfect in neither, who out of zeal for the Law stirred up ill will against the Apostle, because he said that, now that Christ had been preached, the Law was ceasing. Finally, under their pressure, he was compelled to circumcise Timothy. A feigned Christian, then, is one who out of rivalry for the Law persecutes a Christian: and this is a false brother. For if these were only Jews, and he calls them false brothers on account of their Judaism, what need was there to say it again? For he already said above: In perils from my own people. But according to the flesh they are not false brothers, whereas in Christ they are false, because they are not Christians with their whole mind (2 Tim. 1; 4). There were also other false brothers, such as Demas and Hermogenes (2 Tim. 4:9).

(2 Cor. 11:27) In labor and hardship. It is true that there can be labor without hardship; but here he added And hardship for this reason, to show that the labor itself was deadly. In many vigils. His vigils were many, but some were voluntary, others of necessity; for, placed in affliction, he had to keep vigil seeking the help of God, and to teach not only by day but also by night, as he says in the Acts of the Apostles. In hunger and thirst (Acts 20:31). Often put to flight by the Jews who persecuted him, he necessarily suffered hunger and thirst through want. In fastings often. He fasted rather often, so as to deserve to be protected by the help of God: for he fasts who, while he has something to eat, abstains. In cold and nakedness. He was in cold and nakedness when he suffered shipwreck. Finally, he says, the barbarians on the island of Malta made a fire and refreshed us because of the impending rain and the cold (Acts 28:2).

(2 Cor. 11:28) Besides those things that are from without, there is my pressing burden, the daily care of all the churches. This care, which he calls daily, comes down from the practice of tradition, that every day he should instruct the people entrusted to him; but the things he mentions above were laid on him in addition, under the constraint of necessity. For the day is given for works, the night for rest; but because necessity pressed upon him to help all, he taught even by night. For he did not hesitate to afflict himself, lest the grace of God be idle.

(2 Cor. 11:29) Who is weak, and I am not weak? He says that he suffers with all, and grieves with them with that affection by which he provides medicine for a wound. And he pursues this in order to show with what care he guards and rules the Church entrusted to him, and on this account that he ought to be judged in nothing less than the other apostles, since, as he has declared, he labored more than all of them. For he labored under greater ill will among the Jews than the other apostles; because, when he was persecuting the Church, he was suddenly changed and became its defender, testifying and confirming that he had heard Christ, whom he had previously reckoned a dead man, speaking with him from heaven (Acts 9:6 ff.). Many would necessarily follow his testimony, knowing that it was surely not without reason that from a persecutor he had become a defender. For this reason the Jews were tormented, and always sought to kill him. Who is scandalized, and I do not burn? To be scandalized is to suffer the desire of the flesh, as far as this place is concerned. And because people are often scandalized even against their will, enticed by the form or the beauty of a body, or by the heat of their own flesh, the Apostle, considering the necessity of human nature, says that he burns, so as to provide for such people, suffering with their weakness: not condemning them, but admonishing them in humility of speech, so that by the hope of the promised reward they may resist the goads of the flesh; lest any of those who hear him, overcome by the desires of the flesh and despairing of themselves, thinking that God would no longer accept it if they corrected themselves, and remaining in the same things, should perish.

(2 Cor. 11:30) If I must boast, I will boast in the things that belong to my weakness. He says this because, if a Christian must boast, he must boast in the humility by which one grows before God. Hence in another place he says: When I am weak, then I am powerful (2 Cor. 12:10), that is, when I am humbled for the salvation of the brothers, then I am exalted. In this one must boast, not in the progeny of the flesh, because one is of the seed of Abraham.

(2 Cor. 11:31) The Father God and of our Lord Jesus Christ, who is blessed forever, knows that I do not lie. He gives God as witness, so that what he says may readily be believed; and this only on account of those who did not honor him as a true apostle.

(2 Cor. 11:32–33) At Damascus the governor of the nation of King Aretas was guarding the city of the Damascenes in order to seize me: and through a window I was let down in a basket through the wall, and I escaped his hands.

Chapter XII

(2 Cor. 12:1) If it is necessary to boast, yet there is no need for me. The governor of Damascus, seeing the Jews laying snares for the Apostle, wanted to carry out their evil design by his power, so that he himself, if he could, might seize him, both to please the Jews and to appear diligent in the office entrusted to him, if for the sake of peace he put to death the man he heard publicly called a disturber. The Jews had been inflamed at this in the very beginnings, at the time when the Lord appeared to him on the road as he was going to Damascus, seeing that the man who had come to bring believers in Christ in chains to Jerusalem was preaching the sect he had come to condemn (Acts 9:2 ff.); and so it came about that he escaped, let down by the disciples through a window, through the wall, in a basket. Some say that this was done unworthily, because he was not set free by God's help. But what need was there for him to be set free by God's aid in a thing that could be done by men, since God's aid is needed only when human help fails? Having done this, he says that it is not expedient for him to boast, because the joy of the present life is fragile; for if he had not escaped, he would have achieved martyrdom. But since it is commanded to flee in persecution (Matt. 10:23), he deliberately escaped; for no one ought to wait for sin to be committed against himself. So he says that he should boast, because he escaped; but it is not expedient, he says, because of pride: for one grows through humility, and God's help benefits the meek, not the proud. Moreover, since he would be boasting that he had escaped a death that is inflicted for the faith, he indicates that suffering for Christ would seem a vain thing; therefore it is not expedient to boast.

(2 Cor. 12:2) But I will come to visions and revelations of the Lord. Now he is going to show to where he was caught up, so that it may accordingly be understood how great and of what kind were the things shown and told to him, lest, as it seemed to some, he should be believed to be less than the other apostles. I know, he says, a man in Christ fourteen years ago, whether in the body, I do not know, or outside the body, I do not know, God knows, such a one caught up as far as the third heaven. He affirmed both, because he did not deny that both could happen: that he could have been caught up both without the body and in the body. And perhaps to some it may seem no great thing if a man of Christ was caught up as far as the third heaven, since the moon is said to be in the third circle. But it is not so, for he is understood to have been caught up beyond all the stars of the world; and therefore what he relates is magnificent and wonderful; for he means that he himself was caught up above the firmament, into the heaven that is third in number among the spiritual heavens.

(2 Cor. 12:3–5) And I know such a man, whether in the body or outside the body, I do not know, God knows, that he was caught up into paradise and heard secret words which it is not lawful for a man to speak. On behalf of such a one I will boast. He says that he was caught up twice, that is, first from here as far as the third heaven, then into the heavenly paradise, the one of which the Lord told the thief that he would be with him in paradise, he says (Luke 23:43), the Father's paradise: there where the city of God, Jerusalem our mother, is also said to be. What wonder is it, then, if it is believed that after the resurrection the body, by then cleansed and made fine, will be immortal in the heavens (Gal. 4:26), when the Apostle does not doubt that a body still mortal could be lifted up into the heavens and taken into the heavenly paradise of God the Father? On behalf of such a man, that is, one so worthy of God that he should experience this, he says that he will boast; and he does not wish to say openly that he is speaking of himself, lest he seem to praise himself. So too John the apostle, though it is known that he is speaking of himself: This is, he says, the disciple whom Jesus loved, who also reclined at supper on his breast (John 21:20), lest he seem to speak gloriously of himself. But on my own behalf I will not boast, except in my weaknesses. Thus he does not boast of himself, as long as he does not declare openly that it is himself. In my weaknesses, he says, I will indeed boast. For to set out the afflictions of sufferings and the weaknesses of straits does not seem glorious, but lamentable. Therefore he says that he boasts in these, knowing nevertheless that they lead to the gain of heavenly rewards. For what will be so glorious for a Christian in the future as to recount the deadly perils inflicted on him for Christ?

(2 Cor. 12:6) For even if I wish to boast, I shall not be foolish; for I shall speak the truth. He says this because, if he declares the merit that he has with God, he will not be imprudent; for he speaks what is true. So if someone speaks of his merits, which have been shown to him, he is certainly not foolish; but if he keeps silent, he is even prudent. But why he does not boast openly here he now adds, saying: But I refrain, lest anyone think of me beyond what he sees in me, or hears anything from me. So he shows why he does not boast openly: Lest anyone think, he says, that I stretch my glory further than the boundary of my merits allows. He means those whom he had as detractors because of the Law, as I mentioned above. For it is certainly good not to keep silent about the glory of one's merits, so that believers may be drawn on.

(2 Cor. 12:7) And lest I be exalted by the sublimity of the revelations, there was given me a goad of the flesh, an angel of Satan, to buffet me, lest I be exalted. He testifies that God provides for good merits, in that he allows the good to be pressed down by temptations, not only so that they may not be deprived of the fruits of their labors, but also so that by these they may be enriched to the full measure of an eternal possession. This remedy, then, was given to the Apostle, so that, pressed down by injuries, he could not be lifted up in mind on account of the greatness of the revelations; for an idle heart was bound to be lifted up by the things he had seen.

(2 Cor. 12:8–9) For this I asked the Lord three times that it might depart from me; and he said to me: My grace is sufficient for you; for power is made perfect in weakness. He testifies that he besought the Lord three times that the goads of the devil, which he faithlessly thrusts into minds and incites to harm the servants of God, might cease from him, and that he did not obtain it: not that he says he was disregarded, but because without knowing it he was asking against himself, that the temptations through which he was being made more perfect should cease from him. That is why he says to the Romans: For what we should pray for as we ought, we do not know (Rom. 8:26). Hence he says that this answer was given him, that the grace of God should suffice him, by which either strength to endure was added or the time of affliction was shortened, so that devotion, while it is not broken by the weakness of the affliction that befalls it, might be seen to be proved. Most gladly, therefore, I will rather boast in my weaknesses, that the power of Christ may dwell in me. It is plain that he teaches that one should boast then, when one is humbled by injuries. But the power to endure is supplied by Christ, so that injuries, which at first seemed harsh and for that reason to be loathed, are afterwards to be gladly accepted, so that they may be softened by Christ's help. So he says that he is glad to be struck, provided he is healed by Christ, knowing that the medicine of Christ adds more to salvation than weakness takes away from health.

(2 Cor. 12:10) Therefore I take pleasure in weaknesses, in insults; for when I am weak, then I am powerful. It is true that the Christian conquers then when he is thought to lose, and faithlessness loses then when it congratulates itself on having conquered. So he applauds when he is insulted, and rises when he is pressed down.

(2 Cor. 12:11) I have become foolish; you compelled me. He says that he was forced to show what he was. And certainly he is no fool who has spoken the truth about himself; but he lowers himself, to make it plain that he does not display his merits willingly. For I ought to have been commended by you. It is plain that these, among whom he established the Church and showed the wonders of the signs of apostleship, ought to have borne witness for him against his detractors, so that, while he kept silent about this, they would defend him whom they had as father in the Gospel of Christ. For I have done nothing less than those exceedingly apostles. Exceedingly, that is, too much, as it seemed to some; for they were what the apostle Paul also was. He says this, then, because he is not less, either in preaching or in working signs, than the apostles, his predecessors not in dignity but in time. For if it is thought that a claim should be settled by time, John began to preach before Christ; and yet it was not Christ who baptized John, but John who baptized Christ. Does God, then, judge in this way? Again, Andrew followed the Savior before Peter, and yet it was not Andrew who received the primacy, but Peter. Why then did he not seem an apostle to some, when by the grace of God he could do the same things as the apostles? Therefore he grieves, and, compelled, shows what he merits by the Lord's favor. Hence it is that he was forced to declare not only that he was not less, but also that he had labored more than all. So he displeased them, and he did not seem to be an apostle to those who, out of zeal for their ancestral law, rejected his preaching as that of an enemy of the tradition of Moses, because he said that it was now ceasing; they favored the apostles rather, and humbled him beneath them, because the apostles did not assert anything against the Law so firmly.

(2 Cor. 12:12) And if I am nothing, yet the signs of an apostle were done among you in all patience, by signs, and wonders, and mighty works. Thus he humbles himself in order to raise himself up; yet he mentions patience first, because for a long time he bore with them as with impatient sick people, so that, applying the medicine of signs and mighty works, he might heal the wounds of their error.

(2 Cor. 12:13) For what is there in which you had less than the other churches, except that I myself did not burden you? Forgive me this wrong. He still commends himself through humility; for he shows that they had not had anything less than the other churches, but rather more; for he preached the Gospel of God to them free of charge, which was granted to no churches, since the Lord says: For the laborer is worthy of his wage (Luke 10:7). If then, he says, I deserve a wrong in return for a good work, forgive me: for every fool is an accuser of the good. So, to show them to be ignorant, he asks their pardon for that deed for which he deserves praise.

(2 Cor. 12:14) Behold, this third time I am ready to come to you, and I will not burden you. Lest it perhaps be thought that he had done this at the beginning to commend himself, but afterwards wished to accept a reward for preaching the Gospel, he shows that he persists in that resolve, lest he seem afterwards to correct what he had done, and there be something for his detractors to find fault with. For I do not seek what is yours, but you. He shows that he is unwilling to accept from them for this reason, that he might win them themselves, so that, understanding that he puts them before money, they might at last recognize his affection toward them. For children ought not to lay up treasure for parents, but parents for children. He indicates that he is their father, as he says in the first letter: Through the Gospel I begot you (1 Cor. 4:15). Yet it is carnal parents who lay up treasure for their children; for spiritual parents it is fitting to receive support from their children to sustain the present life. For it is no great thing that those who hand on spiritual things should obtain carnal things. But here he proves so far that he is unwilling to accept, that he transfers the case of the carnal father to the spiritual one; so that not only does he not accept from them, but, if it can be done, he even gives to them himself, as I have already said above: I despoiled other churches, taking wages for your ministry (2 Cor. 11:8). By saying this he touches them, so that they may understand how great he is and what those deserve who neglected the providence of so great an apostle.

(2 Cor. 12:15) But I will most gladly spend and spend over and above, and I myself will be spent for your souls. Now he openly shows the affection and love that he had toward them, when he says that he is ready not only to spend his own goods for them, and to spend them more readily still, but even to die himself for the salvation of their souls, as the apostle John also says, that after the Savior's example we ought even to lay down our lives for the brothers (1 John 3:16). Loving you very much, I am loved less. He provokes them to love, because they themselves ought to love him more, him above all through whom they learned the way of salvation, through whom also they were gained for God, made faithful from faithless, and purified from defiled.

(2 Cor. 12:16) But be it so: I did not burden you; but being crafty I took you by guile. He does not keep silent about anything that could be put forward from the other side, so as to clear himself in every respect; for he did nothing with them by overreaching, but in simplicity, providing for himself once, for them in a twofold way: both to provide for them before God and not to seek their means here, while for himself what is promised by God for the future was enough. For perhaps they might suspect that he had spurned what was offered because it was small, so that he might obtain greater things: that, considering his merits, he was not refusing money, but despising the amount, judging it unworthy of himself.

(2 Cor. 12:17) Did I overreach you through any of those whom I sent to you? What he says is this: that those whom he sent did not intimate to them that, if they wished to have the Apostle appeased, they should offer an amount worthy of his person, since he had refused to accept for this reason, that less than was fitting was being offered. They would have understood that as something contrived by the Apostle's cunning, so that what he did would be shown to have been done not out of disdain but out of avarice.

(2 Cor. 12:18) I asked Titus, and I sent the brother with him. Was Titus avaricious toward you? Did we not walk in the same spirit? Not in the same footsteps? It is plain that, since nothing of the sort was said or done by those whom he sent, their single mind is proved to be for good, the suspicion of avarice being excluded.

(2 Cor. 12:19) Do you think again that we are excusing ourselves to you? What he says is this: You have long been in doubt about us, thinking that we do not deal with you sincerely. For they were being stirred up by the false apostles, who, wishing to plunder them, asserted that the Apostle was doing this by subterfuge, as if in order to be able to receive more. We speak before God in Christ; but all things, beloved, for your edification. He so desires that they should think truly and well of him that he satisfies them both by reasoning and by attestation: that he shows himself to be such not to profit himself, but to further their edification; so that even in this way he may provoke their love toward himself, while he does not burden their means and does not bend the authority of his power in reproving vices. For without doubt it benefits the one who is rebuked, so that he amends, if the one set over him refuses what is offered for this reason, that he may reprove freely.

(2 Cor. 12:20) For I fear lest, when I come, I may find you not such as I wish. Since not all whom he rebuked in the first letter had yet amended themselves of the vices for which they had been reproved, he therefore wishes to appear as one who will come to them with authority, so that, frightened, they may be corrected, and he may find them such as he wishes. And I may be found by you not such as you wish. He says this so that they may show themselves such that they are in accord with the Apostle, so that they may rejoice together with one another. For he finds someone not such as he wishes who makes himself such as to be at discord with him. Lest perhaps there be among you contentions, rivalries, animosities, dissensions, detractions, whisperings, swellings, seditions. Now he mentions the errors which he reproved in the earlier letter, when, deferring to persons and preferring one to another, they were at odds with one another.

(2 Cor. 12:21) Lest again, when I come, God humble me among you, and I mourn many of those who sinned before and did not repent of the uncleanness and fornication and immodesty that they practiced. He warns of this beforehand, so as not to find them such as he had found them formerly, with various vices of unclean works, or rather of names; for every work is without crime, but the name of disorder makes a crime. For a thing is unlawful then when it is done otherwise than is permitted. So he says that he is humbled when they sin, because like a devoted father he begins to weep for the sins of his children. For what father would not rejoice when his children act well? So again he must mourn if they conduct themselves perversely. Therefore he admonishes that, if any have not repented of past fornications or of other uncleanness or immodesty, as there are some whom he indicates did not repent, they should repent now, so that when he comes he may be with them without sadness. For when he says: And I mourn many of those who sinned before and did not repent, he proves that some of them had repented and some had not. This is contrary to Novatian, who says that fornicators cannot do penance and be received into communion. Here he proves that they did penance, and were therefore received into the peace of the Church. So he proves three forms of sins, when he names fornication, and immodesty, and uncleanness.

Chapter XIII

(2 Cor. 13:1) This third time I am coming to you. Above he said that he was ready; now he indicates that he is about to come. In the mouth of two or three witnesses every word shall stand. He said this according to the Law (Deut. 19:15), by which a person is either cleared or condemned on the word of two or three witnesses: so he warns these too to be found cleared at the Apostle's third coming.

(2 Cor. 13:2) For I have foretold and I foretell, as when present on my second visit, and being absent I now write to those who sinned before and to all the rest, that if I come again, I will not spare. He indicates that he said earlier, when present, that they should correct themselves, lest they be rebuked with shame; and now he declares that, being absent, he says the same things again, so that after the second rebuke, if they have not amended themselves, there ought to be no sparing them.

(2 Cor. 13:3) How is it that you seek a proof of him who speaks in me, Christ? They seek a proof of Christ speaking in the Apostle when they do not obey his precepts, wishing to prove whether he would dare to punish, so that by this he might be understood to be of trustworthy authority and be feared thereafter. For he who despises his teacher seeks to be corrected. Who is not weak in you, but is powerful in you. Christ is powerful in them, because they saw in his name the dead raised, demons put to flight, paralytics made well, the deaf hearing, speech restored to the mute, the lame running, the blind seeing. All these belong to power, not to weakness: indeed, it was for this reason that they were drawn to the faith.

(2 Cor. 13:4) For though he was crucified from our weakness, yet he lives from the power of God. It is true that Christ was crucified because of our sins, so that, destroying death, he might free from it those who believe in him. So he suffered himself to be made weak for our sake, that he might conquer death for us. For descending to the underworld, he who knew no sin at all, and was slain as though a sinner, blotted out the bond of Adam, so that thereafter those who died under his name should not be held by death, but should have confidence of going to the heavens. So, slain on account of our weakness, he came back to life by the power of the Father, that he might prove to the disciples that what he had taught was true, by the example shown in himself. For we too are weak in him. This pertains to the person of the apostles, who, preaching him, that is, Christ, were made weak, while they were subjected to injuries, imprisoned, beaten. But we live with him from the power of God toward you. It is plain that if they were made weak by the faithless, in the eyes of believers this was not weakness but advancement; for the faithful become more powerful from this, while they are made weak. So death inflicted by the faithless is life with respect to believers; for by the power of God they will rise again, to live with Christ.

(2 Cor. 13:5) Test yourselves, whether you are in the faith: prove yourselves. He instructs the brothers to examine one another; for they become more careful if they confer with one another. So he meant this testing to be understood as proving; for every proving is a testing, yet not every testing is a proving. For if God is said to test, it is a proving; but if the devil is said to test, it is a temptation, because he strives to overthrow. And a man sometimes tests in order to prove, sometimes in order to ensnare. Or do you not know yourselves, that Christ Jesus is in you, unless you are reprobates? He says this because, if you do not know how to prove one another, you do not know that Jesus Christ is in you; and this is to be a reprobate: not to know the faith of one's own religion and profession. For whoever holds in his heart the sense of his faith knows that Christ Jesus is in him.

(2 Cor. 13:6) But I hope that you will know that we are not reprobates. He provokes them to the knowledge of faith and of right living. For when they begin to know the sure authority and merit of their apostle with God, they will also be able to be careful about themselves.

(2 Cor. 13:7) But I pray God that you do nothing evil: not that we may appear approved, but that you may do what is good, and that we may be as reprobates. With that affection with which he always prays for them, that they may refrain from evils. Not that we, he says, may appear approved, that is, not that we may be seen to be in authority while you sin. But that you may do what is good, and that we may be as reprobates. What is this? Does the Apostle pray to be a reprobate? Certainly not; but he prays for this: that, these acting well, since the apostles would not dare to rebuke them, they may be as it were humbled, and, humbled, may appear even as reprobates. For they are seen to be approved by God when they judge sinners by the authority granted them. If, then, there are none for them to judge, since their authority lies idle in these, they are seen as reprobates, as it were.

(2 Cor. 13:8) For we can do nothing against the truth, but for the truth. He says this because power was not given against the truth, to reprove one who lives well, but for the truth, to punish one who is an enemy of the law. And so power will lie idle if they do what is good. Hence he also says to the Romans: Do you wish not to fear the power? Do good, and you will have praise from it (Rom. 13:3). So when someone does not fear, because he does good, the power has been made void in his case: this is to be as a reprobate, because that by which the power is proved ceases in him.

(2 Cor. 13:9) For we rejoice when we are weak, but you are strong. This is to be weak: not to exercise power; and he wishes them to be strong, so that by acting well they may conquer vices and keep punishment or rebuke away from themselves. This too we pray for, and your perfection. In prayer he asks that they may be made perfect in this, and, perfected in the power of a good life and in uncorrupted faith, may not sin.

(2 Cor. 13:10) Therefore I write these things while absent, so that when present I may not act more harshly. It is plain that he admonishes them while absent for this reason, that he may find them more amended, and not, when placed there, rebuke them with shame, so that he who sins should blush in the assembly of the brothers. According to the power which the Lord gave me for building up, and not for destruction. This is the power which above he wishes to lie idle while they act well; if otherwise, he wishes to exercise himself in it, so that sinners, being rebuked, may be built up, not put to death. For those who are reproved in order to be corrected are not destroyed, but built up for life.

(2 Cor. 13:11) Finally, brothers, rejoice, be perfect, receive consolation, think the same thing, live in peace; and the God of peace and love will be with you. This joy will come from amendment, from which perfection too will be able to result: but first there will be consolation, so that in hope of the things to come they may forsake present delight. Think the same thing: he says this because of the old discord, so that they may now be of one mind. Live in peace. If they are in concord, they will have peace. And the God of peace and love, he says, will be with you. The God of peace is Christ, who said: My peace I give you, peace I leave you (John 14:27). He is also the God of love, because he says again: A new commandment I give you, that you love one another. And by this, he says, all will know that you are my disciples, if you have for one another the love with which I have loved you (John 13:34). Yet the peace of God is one thing, the peace of the world another; for the wicked and the filthy also have peace, but for damnation; whereas the peace of Christ is free from sins. So it flees faithlessness, spurns guile, resists evil deeds. This peace is pleasing to God and his friend, but the enemy of the devil. Whoever has it will have both love and, together with it, their God, for perpetual protection.

Greet one another with a holy kiss. This greeting is the Church's; for these kisses are not carnal but spiritual, joining souls by the embrace of bodies, not with the desire of the flesh but of the spirit. All the saints greet you. He provokes them to holiness, so that they too may venture to return the greeting to the saints; for they are greeted by the saints for this reason, that they may imitate them.

The grace of our Lord Jesus Christ, and the love of God, and the communion of the Holy Spirit be with you all. Amen. Here the Trinity is embraced together, and the unity of power, because it is the perfection of the whole of salvation. For the love of God sent us the Savior Jesus, by whose grace we have been saved. That we may possess this grace of salvation is the work of the communion of the Holy Spirit; for he guards those loved by God and saved by the grace of Christ, so that the perfection of the three is the consummation of man unto salvation.
