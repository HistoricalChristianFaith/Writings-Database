Commentary on Philemon

Latin source: the commentary on the Epistle to Philemon by the anonymous Roman writer called Ambrosiaster (written at Rome about 366–384), from the Maurist text of 1690, printed under the name of Ambrose in J.-P. Migne, Patrologia Latina 17 (Paris, 1845), cols. 503–508, verified against the page images and collated in full with the Maurist edition (Paris, 1690). Migne's misprints are corrected where the Maurist edition he reprints or Ballerini's edition (Milan, 1877) has the right reading, as are his misprinted Scripture references and verse labels. The lemmata are translated from Ambrosiaster's own Old Latin text of Paul, and all other Scripture as he quotes it, from his own Latin; italics mark the lemmata and the other Scripture he quotes. The edition's verse labels are given as (Philem. verse). This English translation is released into the public domain.

Prologue

Philemon was endowed with no dignity of ecclesiastical ordination, but was a praiseworthy man, one of the people. To him he writes a letter on behalf of Onesimus, his household slave, that he may not only pardon him but also give thanks to God, because he has received him back such that he should regard him not as a slave but as a most beloved brother.

Chapter I

(Philem. 1–3) Paul, a prisoner of Christ Jesus, and Timothy the brother, to Philemon the beloved brother and sharer with us in labours, and to Appia the dearest sister, and to Archippus our fellow soldier, and to the church in his house. Grace to you and peace from God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ. To give dignity to the letter sent by him, he began from the indignity done to him. For just as to be bound because of sin is a reproach, so on the contrary to endure the chains of custody for Christ is the greatest glory; for at the time when he sent this letter he was in custody. Now although Philemon was not a cleric, yet for devotion's sake he took part in ecclesiastical needs, which is why he calls him a sharer in labours. For there are many who, though they are worthy, nevertheless excuse themselves, judging themselves too lowly for so great a ministry: whereby they appear truly worthy.

(Philem. 4–6) I give thanks to my God, always making mention of you in my prayers, hearing of your faith and love which you have towards the Lord Jesus and towards all the saints; that your sharing may become acceptable in the acknowledgement of every good that is in us in Christ. He says that he rejoices and gives thanks to God in his prayers, because Philemon was firm in faith and pressed on in good works: for he who loves Christ ought to prove this towards his servants, knowing that the Lord will reckon to himself whatever is shown to have been spent on his servants. The Apostle, however, says that he adds this to his prayers, that his working may be done with knowledge of goodness; that is, that he may do this work with such a mind as to expect the reward of it in the future.

(Philem. 7) For I have had great joy and consolation over your love, because the hearts of the saints have found rest through you, brother. It is plain that he indicates that he is glad for this reason, that Philemon was supplying necessities for the needs of the saints: hence he rejoices the more, being sure from this that he would also obey the commands of him who, it is agreed, is the greater.

(Philem. 8–9) Wherefore, though I have much confidence in Christ to command you what is fitting, yet for love's sake I rather beseech you, since you are such a one as Paul, an old man: but now also a prisoner of Christ Jesus. As far as reason required, it was fitting for the teacher to command the disciple out of confidence in his apostolic authority; but because Philemon was a good man, he calls him to obey through affection, as being of the same age, and a prisoner of Christ Jesus, that is, bound to the business of the Lord, or subjected to chains for his name, as the apostles themselves also were. For such were Judas and Silas, who, though they were of the people, were leading men among the brethren and had bound themselves to the works of the Lord (Acts 15:22 ff.), so that together with the apostolic men they might contend jointly for the faith of God. Hence Silas is found to be a sharer in the labours of the Apostle.

(Philem. 10–14) I beseech you for my son, whom I begot in chains, Onesimus, once unprofitable to you, but now very profitable to you and to me, whom I have sent back to you. Do you receive him as my own heart: whom I had wished to keep with me, that he might minister to me in your stead in the chains of the Gospel; but without your counsel I wished to do nothing, so that your good might not be as it were of necessity, but voluntary. It is no great thing for a humble man to bow himself, but it is praiseworthy for an exalted man to humble himself; therefore the Apostle asks one whom he has power to command, so as to be a pattern in all things for the advancement of merits. Onesimus, then, a fugitive who ran back to divine help, the Apostle baptized when he was in custody in the city of Rome (Acts 28:16), seeing in him the hope of usefulness. He commends him in such a way as to signify his own mind towards him: that from unprofitable he has been made fit for worldly as well as divine services.

(Philem. 15–16) For perhaps he departed for an hour for this reason, that you might receive him for ever: no longer as a slave, but instead of a slave a beloved brother, especially to me; but how much more to you, both in the flesh and in the Lord? Onesimus, having offended his own master, fled by a long journey to the Apostle, so as to show that he had not withdrawn in order to be liable for an offence, but so that, his sins blotted out, he might return useful to such a degree that he would not only become equal to his master in merits, but a brother also to the teacher himself. And lest Philemon, as is usual, should feel some disdain of pride, as a master against a slave, he humbles him when he calls him a brother both in the flesh and in the Lord; so that, the consideration of human condition being removed, since we are all from the one Adam, we may recognize ourselves as brothers: especially when faith comes between, which cuts off all pride.

(Philem. 17) If then you hold me as a partner, receive him as me. He shows that he has such affection for Onesimus that he calls Philemon, whom he praises above, his sharer only if he receives this man as he would the Apostle. For this is what Solomon said: Free men will serve a wise slave (Sir. 10:28). This saying is meant to make Philemon fear, so that he might receive him at least for this reason, if he could not be moved by love.

(Philem. 18) But if he has wronged you in anything, or is a debtor, charge this to me. Now he tears away the excuse of anger, lest perhaps Philemon should complain that he had been so wronged by him that he seemed by no means to deserve pardon. But if it were so, he says that it is to be charged to himself, through whom those who sinned before are to be heirs of the kingdom of heaven.

(Philem. 19) I, Paul, have written with my own hand: I will repay; not to say to you that you owe me even your own self. If there is any offence on the part of Onesimus, he testifies that his own bond is sufficient to make satisfaction for him. And to speak the truth, he draws himself up and says that he demands less from him than he is proved to owe. For in the case of the slave he is compelled to make satisfaction, because he burdens himself with the debt.

(Philem. 20–21) Even so, brother, so may I enjoy you in the Lord: refresh my heart in Christ. Having confidence in your obedience I have written to you, knowing that you will do beyond what I say. Now he coaxes him, so that his obedience may be voluntary, that he may be able to have its fruit. For thus he provokes him to do more than is asked of him; for it usually happens that one who sees that good is thought of him shows himself better.

(Philem. 22) But at the same time prepare a lodging for me as well; for I hope that through your prayers I shall be given to you. To make him more careful and more obedient, he indicates that he is going to come to him; for the absent are wont to be despised. And why does the Apostle not disdain to be still here, he who always suffered wearinesses and deadly perils, beatings, persecutions and chains, and whose rest was nowhere but in Christ alone? But for the advancement of believers, and that he might confound the enemies of the cross of Christ so as to increase the glory of God, he offered his flesh to sufferings, that he might acquire crowns of immortality for his soul and his body.

(Philem. 23–24) Epaphras, my fellow captive in Christ Jesus, greets you: Mark, Aristarchus, Demas, Luke, my helpers. All these he testifies are sharers of his glory.

(Philem. 25) The grace of our Lord Jesus Christ be with your spirit, amen. This is the subscription of the Apostle, confirming the letter he has sent.
