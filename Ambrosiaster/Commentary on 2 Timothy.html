Commentary on 2 Timothy

Latin source: the commentary on the Second Epistle to Timothy by the anonymous Roman writer called Ambrosiaster (written at Rome about 366–384), from the Maurist text of 1690, printed under the name of Ambrose in J.-P. Migne, Patrologia Latina 17 (Paris, 1845), cols. 483–498, verified against the page images and collated in full with the Maurist edition (Paris, 1690). Migne's misprints are corrected where the Maurist edition he reprints or Ballerini's edition (Milan, 1877) has the right reading, as are his misprinted Scripture references and verse labels. The lemmata are translated from Ambrosiaster's own Old Latin text of Paul, and all other Scripture as he quotes it, from his own Latin; italics mark the lemmata and the other Scripture he quotes. The edition's verse labels are given as (2 Tim. chapter:verse). This English translation is released into the public domain.

Prologue

He writes another letter to Timothy, in order to rouse him by his own example to martyrdom, now that he has been instructed in ecclesiastical discipline and in what the hope of the promise is: and because it is going to come about that some will turn away from sound doctrine, because it seems hard to them, to fables, that is, to human inventions.

Chapter I

(2 Tim. 1:1–2) Paul, an apostle of Jesus Christ by the will of God, according to the promise of life which is in Christ Jesus, to Timothy, his beloved son. He indicates that he was called an apostle of Christ Jesus by the will of God for this reason: to teach what and how great a promise there is for the future in the faith of Christ; so that by the preaching of it he may bring about an increase in those who are to be saved by the discipline of the Lord. For those servants are fit who by their diligence and faith enlarge the business of their masters. And he writes this to him as to a dearest son so that he may become an imitator of this thing, handing on the same things to others; so that through the transmission of successions there may be an unfailing fit teacher, through whom the truth, once asserted, may win many to the promised life. Grace, mercy and peace from God the Father and Christ Jesus our Lord. He wishes for him the grace of the Father in mercy, that it may be present to him; and that of Christ Jesus our Lord, because God bestows his grace through Christ; so that the grace of both is one.

(2 Tim. 1:3–5) I give thanks to my God, whom I serve from my forefathers in a pure conscience, that I have an unfailing remembrance of you in my prayers, night and day greatly desiring to see you, mindful of your tears, that I may be filled with joy, receiving the remembrance of the sincere faith which is in you, which dwelt first in your grandmother Lois and in your mother Eunice; and I am sure that it is in you also. Since it is fitting to remember good men, he therefore gives thanks to God that he was mindful of Timothy, an admirable man, so that even this itself is a gift of God; for he who thinks of good men is not without merit. And because the God of the Christians is the same who was the God of the Jews, he testifies that he renders thanks to that God whom he has served from his forefathers. For since his ancestors hoped for this which he had begun to preach, their faith is one. And because he succeeded to the service of his parents, and because he was still in them when they served God, he rightly says: Whom I serve from my forefathers. How? In a pure conscience, he says; for when he once persecuted the Church, he did it in such a way that he acted out of love of God, not out of malice. For in a like way it is also written in the epistle to the Hebrews that Levi, who received tithes, paid tithes to Melchizedek; because Levi, he says, was in the loins of Abraham when he offered tithes to Melchizedek (Heb. 7:9–10): so here too he says that he served God from his forefathers, because Levi also is said to have offered tithes in his great-grandfather. For Levi's father is Jacob, his grandfather Isaac, his great-grandfather Abraham. He professes, then, that he is very mindful of Timothy, and that he longs for him eagerly because of the remembrance of his tears, which, as is understood, he had shed out of love for the Apostle as he was departing from him. Not without reason, then, does he long for one who loves him, so that he may be filled with joy from their common gladness, on account of the sincere faith which he indicates was in his grandmother and in his mother, and most certainly in him too. All this affection, therefore, is on account of the faith which was inviolate in them.

(2 Tim. 1:6–7) For this reason I admonish you that you revive the gift of God which is in you through the laying on of my hands; for God has not given us a spirit of fear, but of power, and of love, and of sobriety. He indicates that he recalled the sincerity of faith of his forebears for this purpose, to make him stronger. For who, hearing the praise of those with whom he is a sharer in that very thing, would not grow, adding courage to such strengths? So, then, he revives in himself the gift of the grace of God received through the ordination of the presbytery, when he warms his spirit with eagerness of mind, rejoicing in himself, as one rejoices in the newness of an ordination. Thus he added: For neither, he says, has God given us a spirit of fear, but of power. For the renewed man goes out of fear, receiving a spirit of gladness because of justification; for every unrighteous man is in fear: not strong but weak; not in love but in hatred; not in sobriety of mind, but wounded by error. But if he is rid of these, through faith he will be strong, and beloved, and sober.

(2 Tim. 1:8) Do not, therefore, be ashamed of the testimony of our Lord, nor of me his prisoner. He teaches that constancy must be kept, and that one ought not to tremble in confessing the faith. For there is nothing in it to be ashamed of; for if Christ was seen as a man, yet by his deeds he appeared as God; although he was crucified, yet he rose from the dead and, while many looked on, a cloud receiving him, he ascended into heaven (Acts 1:9). It is not, then, a matter of weakness but of power; for where weakness is supposed, there power appears. So he who is proved to have conquered is foolishly said to have been made weak. In this way there is nothing in the Apostle either that should cause a blush; for if he is said to have been humbled by afflictions and beatings, from that side he confounds his detractors, because he is plainly seen to be wonderful, by his mere command, in signs and wonders; so that it may be understood that he is humbled not through weakness but willingly, for a certain advance of merits. For God permits his servants to be humbled by the wicked for this reason, lest he seem to give them rewards unjustly.

(2 Tim. 1:9) But labour together in the Gospel according to the power of God, who has saved us. Since death was conquered by the power of God so that we might be able to be saved, accordingly it is fitting that we make the Redeemer some return, at least in part. For since we cannot respond to all his benefits, let us at least carry out his embassy faithfully and earnestly. And has called us with his holy calling. It is a holy calling when they are chosen to govern the holy people of God: and the elder who is chosen as a leader in holiness is called to a holy thing. Not according to our merits. It is true; for if you inquire into it clearly, no man can seem worthy to be the vicar of Christ; for all whom he chose had previously been sinners. As far, then, as comparison with others is concerned, these were found worthy; but as far as the thing itself is concerned, all are unworthy. But according to his own purpose, and the grace which was given to us in Christ Jesus before the ages of time. Since God by his condescension decreed long ago to save sinners (for God foreknew what would come about in man before he made him, and before he sinned), he predestined how man should be restored: at what time, and through whom, and by what means they could be saved; so that this gift may be seen to be bestowed neither by the merit of those who are saved, nor by that of those through whom they are called, but by the grace of God, through faith in Christ.

(2 Tim. 1:10) But now made manifest through the illumination of the coming of our Savior Jesus Christ. He testifies that the gift of God, which before the ages of time lay hidden in mystery, was made manifest by the incarnation and coming of Christ, when men began to receive the remission of sins.

(2 Tim. 1:11) Who indeed destroyed death, and illuminated life with eternity through the Gospel, in which I have been appointed a preacher, and an apostle, and teacher of the Gentiles. He shows in what way Christ saved his people, saying that he destroyed death, which was the enemy of man, so that man could not rise again from the underworld; and then at last he illuminated life with eternity, so that one who rises from the dead can no longer die. For that life is dark which is bounded by time, while it slumbers, while it is ignorant, while it conducts itself badly. And he shows how life is illuminated: because through the Gospel eternity is given to believers, with the teacher of the Gentiles preaching it.

(2 Tim. 1:12) For which cause I also suffer these things. Preaching the gift of God, he suffers the devil as his enemy, so that the devil stirs up afflictions against him, because he rescues the people from his jaws. But I am not confounded. Secure in this, that a great reward has been promised for tribulations, he is not confounded, but glories in afflictions. For I know whom I have believed, and I am certain that he is able to guard my deposit unto that day. This is what he says: with what hope and confidence in the magnificence of the Savior he is secure; because what he entrusts to him is in safety. And what does he entrust to him, except his salvation? So that, suffering here for him, he may find salvation with him when he begins to judge; so that, while punishing the unbelievers, he may pronounce this man worthy of eternal life.

(2 Tim. 1:13–14) Holding the form of sound words which you have heard from me in the faith and love which is in Christ Jesus, guard the good thing that has been entrusted, through the Holy Spirit who dwells in us. He calls his precepts, together with his deeds, the form of saving doctrine, which he commands this man to follow; so that he may guard them, with the Holy Spirit's favour.

(2 Tim. 1:15–16) For you know this, that all who are in Asia have turned away from me, among whom are Phygelus and Hermogenes. These whom he mentions were full of deceit; for they feigned friendship with the Apostle, so that by keeping close to him they might learn the grounds on which they could bring a false charge against him, or bring one in through others. After they saw that they had been exposed, they withdrew from him. May the Lord grant mercy to the house of Onesiphorus; for he often refreshed me, and was not ashamed of my chain. This refreshment is to be understood in a twofold way: for he both refreshed him with his comfort in the hardship of custody, and supplied whatever was needed, whether by him or by the needy. For through the hope of the things that have been promised, he was not ashamed to approach and seek out a man held in custody; for those whom he mentions above, because they were ashamed of the cross of Christ, deserted the Apostle in his need.

(2 Tim. 1:17–18) But when I was in Rome, he sought me out diligently and found me. May the Lord grant him to find mercy with the Lord in that day. The Apostle, coming to Rome after he had appealed to Caesar, was kept under guard by soldiers; for at that time Onesiphorus, for love's sake and for the sake of his teaching, sought him with great diligence and found him. His visit was so welcome to the Apostle that he commends him to the Lord in such a way that he may find mercy with the Lord on the day of judgment; so that, just as he found the Apostle by seeking him, so also, seeking mercy, he may find it with the judge. For this is what he prays: that the Lord, the Father of heaven and earth, may grant him to find grace with the Lord of the human race, that is, the Son. This is like that passage in Genesis: And the Lord rained, he says, sulphur and fire upon Sodom and Gomorrah from God out of heaven (Gen. 19:24).

And how much he ministered to me at Ephesus, you know better. Since some are wont to serve persons rather than causes, wishing to gratify their superiors, the Apostle therefore shows the general goodness of Onesiphorus, lest he should perhaps seem to have commended himself to a person when he ministered to the Apostle; since it was clear that he had been great-hearted towards all.

Chapter II

(2 Tim. 2:1–2) You therefore, my son, be strengthened in the grace which is in Christ Jesus, and in the things which you have heard from me through many witnesses: entrust these to faithful men, who will be fit to teach others also. He instructs him to be constant and robust in teaching the grace of God which was given in Christ Jesus: and not only in the grace, but also in the mysteries which concern Christ. He wishes the secret things to be handed on to the faithful and worthy, who are able to teach others, not indiscriminately to common and careless men; for a teacher must be chosen with great care.

(2 Tim. 2:3–4) Labour as a good soldier of Christ Jesus; for no one serving as a soldier to God entangles himself in worldly business; so that he may please him to whom he has proved himself. What he says is plain; because no one can serve two masters (Matt. 6:24); for worldly business must needs serve avarice: and unless men have lied, they cannot have concluded it. It is, then, a sin, but a light one. But the churchman proves himself to God for this reason: so that, devoted to him, he may fulfil the office which he has pledged, careful in the things of God, a stranger to worldly business; for it is not fitting for one man to have a double profession.

(2 Tim. 2:5) Even if someone contends, he is not crowned unless he has contended lawfully. He wants the soldier of God to be so well trained in the works of God, or in the office assigned to him, that he may be able to attain the merit of glory: watchful with his whole mind in divine services, despising the present things, loving the heavenly; for he has proved himself for this purpose, that, labouring here, he may be rewarded in heaven.

(2 Tim. 2:6–7) The labouring farmer ought to be the first to receive of the fruits. Understand what I say; for the Lord will give you understanding in all things. Timothy was a saint of such abstinence that he restrained himself even from lawful things; for this is what he wants to be understood: that he abstained from a share in the treasury, though the Lord decreed this, that those who proclaim the Gospel should live by the Gospel (1 Cor. 9:14). Therefore the Apostle instructs him, since he is the first, to take first, and so to distribute to the rest.

(2 Tim. 2:8–9) Remember that Christ Jesus, of the seed of David, has risen from the dead according to my Gospel, in which I labour even to bonds, as a robber; but the word of God is not bound. Since there were going to be many sons of the devil who would deny that the Son of God was incarnate, he therefore recalls this, because they strive deceitfully to assert this under the name of the apostles. He added, according to my Gospel, so as to shut out the false discourses of an evil mind; for heretics are not to be believed about the Apostles more than these very men bearing witness to their own preaching, a preaching which he complains that he carries on with labour. For he was troubled with afflictions and prisons, because he offended both Jews and Gentiles by preaching, as it seemed to them, incredible things; for he had the Jews angry with him on account of the Law, which he taught had ceased: and he made the Gentiles his enemies, because he preached one God in Christ. Hence they treated him as a robber, binding him, shutting him up, to see whether he could be silenced, not knowing that the word of God could not be bound. Thus, often shut up, he suffered no loss but made a gain; for there was no lack of someone to whom he might preach in prison, so that those whom he could not invite freely to the faith, he might, placed in bonds, feed with the word of God.

(2 Tim. 2:10) Therefore I endure all things for the sake of the elect; so that they too may obtain the salvation which is in Christ Jesus, with heavenly glory. In order to save by his preaching men predestined to life, he was subjected to deadly perils, knowing that he would have profit from the salvation of theirs that he had sought.

(2 Tim. 2:11–12) A faithful saying: for if we have died with him, we shall also live with him: if we endure, we shall also reign with him. He indicates that the immortality and glory of Christ can be attained thus: if we remain in the form of baptism, in which all offences die; so that, renewed, we may flee all old vices and crimes, so that, having followed the footsteps of our author, we may be seen to be dead to sins. But if this is added to the case, that we also suffer tribulations and afflictions for the name of the Lord without resentment, then, enduring these things, we shall reign together with him; for it is fitting that he who suffers reproaches and deadly perils for him should appear glorious in his kingdom. If we deny him, he also will deny us. It is true, for if we have not confessed him before men, then he too will say to us in that hour of need of the judgment to come: I do not know you (Matt. 25:12). This signifies punishment.

(2 Tim. 2:13) If we do not believe, he remains faithful; he cannot deny himself. No one can take away from the Lord what he has. For who is stronger than he, through whom all things came into being? Nor can anything be added to one who is perfect; for he who believes him confers nothing on him, just as he who does not believe in him takes nothing from him. Whatever, then, is done concerns our own profit, so that we may not remain in ignorance. For he is not made firm by us, since he is firm of himself and in himself.

(2 Tim. 2:14) Admonish them of these things, testifying before the Lord. Do not fight with words; for it is useful for nothing but the subversion of the hearers. He warns that contentions are to be avoided, and that precepts are to be given under the fear and dread of God; because contention can breed a scruple in those not yet firmly established. For in contention such things are commonly thrown up, with the polished steel of malice, as to unsettle the minds of brothers who are beginning.

(2 Tim. 2:15) Take careful heed to yourself, so that you show yourself approved to God, a workman not shameless, rightly handling the word of truth. Since there are some who say that contention benefits the hearers, but the Apostle, knowing that it rather harms them, gives this command: that each should take care to make himself approved by spurning altercation; and he calls shameless those who strive by contentions. For contention necessarily wrests out something, indeed many things that are said against conscience; so that a man loses inwardly in his mind while he goes away outwardly a victor. For no one allows himself to be beaten, even if he knows that what he hears is true: but he handles the word rightly, and with a good conscience, who speaks to the willing and the peaceable. There ought, then, to be conference among the servants of God, not altercation.

(2 Tim. 2:16–17) But avoid profane novelties of words; for they advance greatly towards emptiness, and their speech creeps like a cancer. He still pursues this same point, that contentions should be avoided; for it is not fitting for men of one faith to have contention, but in discussion let them bear with one another and agree to what is well said: and if anything seems unacceptable, let it be passed over in peace. For it is adversaries and those who disagree who are wont to provoke one another, of whom one is catholic and the other profane. This man, then, to whom the Apostle gives the command to spurn and turn aside from empty fables, is the catholic: he ought to be quiet, so that he always utters words from conscience, and does not allow impious words to be brought forth in his presence. For they give some people occasion to advance into worse; for there is no lack of those who, hearing profane things, conceive an evil thought: and therefore contention does harm.

(2 Tim. 2:18) Among whom are Hymenaeus and Philetus, who have strayed from the truth, saying that the resurrection has already taken place, and have subverted the faith of some. Now he has disclosed the names of those very men on whose account he warns that contentions are to be avoided, whom he marked out as both profane and impious, straying from the truth; for they denied that which is the chief point of Christians, that they believe they will rise from the dead in the future. And these men, as we are taught from another Scripture, said that the resurrection takes place in their children; and they did not lack one who gave perverse assent, against the faith which he had obtained at the beginning.

(2 Tim. 2:19) But the firm foundation of God stands, having this seal: for the Lord has known those who are his: and let everyone who names the name of the Lord depart from iniquity. The foundation of God is faith, which holds what God has promised; and this faith cannot be broken by the inventions of the faithless and the heretics; for although these deny the things to come which we believe, yet God will give what we believe he will give, his promise bearing witness. The seal of God's promise, therefore, is our faith, which makes firm the words of God. On the day of judgment God will know those who have his sign, dissenting from the iniquity of the faithless and the heretics, which thinks it weakens God's promise; for nothing is so iniquitous as to go against the faithfulness of God. And so it was said by Moses to Dathan, and Abiram, and Korah, when they presumed to offer sacrifices against the precept of God (Num. 16:35): The Lord has known, he says, those who are his. By this saying he showed what destruction awaited those who would pervert the words of God.

(2 Tim. 2:20) But in a great house there are not only vessels of gold and silver, but also of wood and of earthenware: and some indeed for honour, but some for dishonour. By this saying he indicated that there are different men in the Church: some good and eminent men, whom he wants to be understood by the gold; some only good, whom he designates by the silver; but others not good, whom he calls vessels of wood and earthenware. I think this is doubtful to no one; for in the church of the Corinthians the frequent speech of the Apostle as he rebukes them proves it. And now in our own Church it is plain, and in the temple at Jerusalem, in which there was a type of the Church, it is proved by clear readings. For there were the Sadducees there, from whose root were Hymenaeus and Philetus: when the Apostle had spoken of their perverse opinion, he at once added this, that it is not to be wondered at that men who dissent are seen in the Church. For in the church of the Corinthians too, of which I have spoken, there were some who, dissenting, denied the resurrection. Yet the mad Novatian, in order to flatter his own error, teaches that this is to be understood otherwise, so that the great house is believed to signify the whole world, which holds a diversity of men; whereas it is clear that the Apostle used the example of the house of a rich and wealthy man, which, although it is well supplied, nevertheless has vessels of wood and earthenware: in this way the Church too, although it is called holy, nevertheless has both the vicious and those who hold perverse opinions, as I mentioned above. These he shows to be worthy of dishonour, yet not to perish, but to be purged through fire; because they are in some respect necessary to the house. But Novatian, puffed up with pride, in order to defend the truth and general holiness of his own church, decreed that it is to be understood in this way, as though the Church were in his keeping and had all its members purified, on account of those causes for which he accuses us, who are innocent. But what need was there in this place to say anything about the world, or why should he affirm that the great house signifies the world? For the Lord says to the apostles: You are not of the world, but I have chosen you out of the world (John 15:19). How, then, are those to be enclosed within the world who are denied to be of the world? And the same Apostle says: What is it to me, he says, to judge those who are outside? (1 Cor. 5:12). For in one house all are under one name; for all are reckoned by the one name of their Lord: but the world contains men of different professions; so much so that they profess to have different gods and lords. He does not, then, call the world the great house, but the Church, in which all are under the name of one Lord.

(2 Tim. 2:21) If anyone, then, purges himself from these, he will be a vessel for honour, sanctified, useful to the Lord, prepared for every good work. Now another heresy is convicted of its deceit, which asserts that certain souls are evil by nature and cannot be brought to the good; whereas he says here that the evil can be changed into good, and the unclean be purified. For Hymenaeus and Philetus could have cleansed their minds, had they wished, so as to become a sanctified vessel, prepared for honour.

(2 Tim. 2:22) But flee youthful desires. Youthful desires are hostile; for they are worldly pleasures, which are enslaved to every allurement; and for this reason they are to be fled by the servant of God. But pursue righteousness, faith, love, peace, with those who call upon the Lord from a pure heart. He bids him pursue these things, which seem out of the way and harsh to youthful age. For he who chooses righteousness does nothing contrary to it; and he who loves faith keeps the commandments: and he who follows love is humble towards all; and the guardian of peace provokes no one to a quarrel.

(2 Tim. 2:23) But avoid foolish and unlearned questions, knowing that they breed quarrels. Senseless questions belong partly to unbelievers, partly to heretics: on the side of the unbelievers, they are those which drive modest men to quarrelling; for they are changed by the excessive contradiction of their impiety, and in anger say something against their overseer. For some of the heretics are wont to feign patience (although they cannot bear to hear anything harsh from a catholic), in order to commend their perverse discourses; therefore one must pay them no heed.

(2 Tim. 2:24–26) But the servant of the Lord must not quarrel, but be gentle towards all, teachable, patient, modest, correcting those who think otherwise: if at some time God may give them repentance unto the knowledge of the truth, and they may come to their senses out of the snares of the devil, by whom they are held captive by him at his will. These things need no interpretation; for they are plain. Yet he has declared how much modesty profits; for if this is approved in certain heretics, because they seem humble, how much more praiseworthy and fruitful will it be in a catholic, if he is gentle, in whom both a useful understanding and a gravity worthy of imitation are perceived!

Chapter III

(2 Tim. 3:1–5) But know this, that in the last days perilous times will be at hand; for men will be lovers of themselves only, avaricious, insolent, proud, blasphemers, disobedient to parents, ungrateful, ungodly, without faith, without love, accusers, incontinent, ungentle, enemies of the good, traitors, wanton, swollen with pride, lovers of pleasures rather than of God, having the form of godliness but denying its power; and avoid these. This is a prophecy of the holy Apostle, in which he foresaw how great would be the evils to come from the heretics and the Jews; for in them all these things that are hostile to the faith are marked out. For the Apostle himself said: For what is it to me to judge those who are outside (1 Cor. 5:12)? For those who think ill of God, and do not fear to profess what tends to his injury, how can they be well-disposed in human conduct? For this is why he said: Having the form of godliness, but denying its power; because they indeed profess themselves Christians, although they are both most wicked in their works and perverse in their understanding. For the praise and power of God lies not in profession more than in deeds.

(2 Tim. 3:6–7) For of these are those who creep into houses and lead captive silly women laden with sins, who are led by various desires, always learning and never coming to the knowledge of the truth. Although this fits all heretics, that, slipping into houses, they capture women with crafty and cunning words, so as to deceive the men through them after the manner of their father the devil, who circumvented Adam through Eve; yet it suits the Manichaeans above the rest. For none are so importunate, so deceitful, so captious as they, who, as is well known, worship one thing and profess another; carry one thing within, and claim another outwardly. For they champion chastity, and, with their law favouring it, live shamefully: they praise mercy, while they are found to be unjust among themselves: they assert that the world is to be spurned, and always go out elegantly dressed; they boastfully proclaim that they persist in fasts, while all of them are seen to be well fattened; only that by a certain art they are seen to be pale, in order to deceive. These things, then, the Apostle prophesied above all of these men, who, as is well known, did not exist in the time of the apostles, any more than the Arians did; seeing that the emperor Diocletian marks them out in his constitution, saying: "This filthy and impure heresy, which", he says, "has lately come out of Persia" (See the Collation of Mosaic and Roman Laws). These men find women who out of vanity desire to hear something new, and through the things that please them they persuade them to foul and unlawful things; for they are eager to learn, though they have no judgment to test what they learn: this is to be always learning and not to have the knowledge of the truth.

(2 Tim. 3:8–9) But just as Jannes and Mambres resisted Moses, so these also resist the truth, men corrupt in mind, reprobate concerning the faith. But they will not advance further; for their ignorance will be evident to all, as theirs also was. This example is from the apocrypha (Exod. 7:1 ff.); for Jannes and Mambres were brothers, magicians or sorcerers of the Egyptians, who thought that by the art of their magic they were resisting, with a counterfeit rivalry, the mighty works of God which were done through Moses. But when the power of Moses increased in his works, they were humbled, and with the pain of their wounds confessed that God was at work in Moses. Hence he says that these likewise will advance no further in the impiety of their assertion; because their lack of skill and their seduction will become plain: and, although unwilling, they will nevertheless confess their lack of skill at the last.

(2 Tim. 3:10–11) But you have followed my doctrine, instruction, purpose, faith, long-suffering, love, patience, persecutions, sufferings. He wants him to be an emulator of the things he enumerates; for at the last these will profit not for repentance but for gladness.

(2 Tim. 3:12–13) Such things as happened to me at Antioch, at Iconium and at Lystra, such persecutions as I endured: and out of them all the Lord delivered me. But all who wish to live godly lives in Christ Jesus will suffer persecution. But wicked men and deceivers will advance into worse: themselves straying, and entangling others in errors. He recalls what and how great things he suffered for this reason: to show the help of God, by which he defends his servants and keeps them safe from the impudence of Satan; so as to make them secure and raise them up, lest they fear what can be inflicted by the faithless, but that they may be eager in persecution, certain that all who conduct themselves rightly in Christ are held in hatred even by the members of their own body. But the wicked advance into worse when they see many giving them assent, so that they send the rest into error. For while they are known to be wicked and deceivers, and nonetheless to flourish and prosper in the world, they draw some to become their imitators, so that they are to be punished not only for their own errors, but also for those of the men whom they ruin by their example.

(2 Tim. 3:14–15) But you, persevere in the things which you have learned and which have been entrusted to you, knowing from whom you have learned them, and that from infancy you have known the sacred writings, which are able to instruct you unto salvation. He indicates that it profits much to have knowledge of the Old Testament; for he calls those the sacred writings which intimate the person of Christ and declare his incarnation for redeeming the salvation of men. And he exhorts him to advance in these; for this antiquity is the strength of the newness, since he knows that he from whom he learned is a firm authority; for he saw signs and wonders done through him, to which every word yields.

(2 Tim. 3:16–17) All Scripture divinely inspired is useful for teaching, for correction, for restoration, for reproof, for instruction which is in righteousness itself, so that the man of God may be ready, complete for every good work. It is plain that all scripture of which God is shown to be the author is useful; for it was given for this purpose: to profit the unskilled and restore the misshapen, drawing the unrighteous to righteousness, so that the man of God may be perfect in every good work; for, advancing by degrees in the new man, it makes him a man of God.

Chapter IV

(2 Tim. 4:1–2) I charge you before God and Christ Jesus, who is to judge the living and the dead, by his coming and his kingdom: preach the word, be insistent in season, out of season; reprove, exhort, rebuke in all magnanimity and teaching. So earnestly does he command that the gift of God be preached inviolate that, with the witness of God the Father and of Christ his Son, he warns that care must be taken over this and that one must be insistent in it; so that by assiduous preaching and frequent reproof, as occasion offers, he may call them on to better progress: and, so that they may more readily assent to the one who reproves them, he exhorts that this be done without bitterness.

(2 Tim. 4:3–4) For the time will come when they will not endure sound teaching; but according to their own desires they will heap up teachers for themselves, itching in their ears: and they will indeed turn their hearing away from the truth, but will be turned to fables. This too, in like manner, is a prophecy of the holy Apostle, who, foreknowing things to come, earnestly commands that one be insistent in teaching; so that, formed in faith and good works and instructed in sound discipline, they may remain strong, so that in the last time, when certain people have begun to be turned from the truth to error, they may persevere unmoved. For he says that there will be such as wish to be taught according to their own desires; so that from steadfast and truthful teachers they turn to those who teach them the things they gladly hear; because the truth will seem harsh to them, so that, having abandoned their own teaching, they give their time to fables. For they do not want their wicked works to be reproved: therefore they want such teachers as will serve their habits; for their ears itch to hear fables of vanities composed under the name of teaching, in which they may take delight. This is proper to none so much as to the Manichaeans, who have I know not what various fictions called by inflated names, though they are frivolous things and a kind of ravings.

(2 Tim. 4:5–6) But you, be sober, patient in all things; do the work of an evangelist, fulfil your ministry; for I am already being offered as a libation, and the time of my dissolution is at hand. He admonishes and exhorts him to keep watch in ecclesiastical discipline, to act manfully, to explain the Scriptures, so that the prudent and learned may avoid empty fables. For the Apostle, knowing that his passion was imminent, wishes to leave in holy Timothy an example of a godly way of life and of sound teaching; so that through a line of transmission suitable teachers might never be lacking: yet he calls his passion a libation; for he who suffers for God's righteousness is sacrificed to God.

(2 Tim. 4:7–8) I have fought the good contest, I have finished the course, I have kept the faith. As for what remains, there is laid up for me the crown of righteousness, which the Lord, the just judge, will render to me on that day; and not to me only, but also to those who love his coming. He recalls that he has kept all the things that were useful and prosperous for himself and for others; for not only in words but also in works did he present himself as a pattern to his hearers; so that he might both stand forth as a faithful steward to his author by the truth of his preaching, and, having wrestled against the princes and powers of the world with all care and skill, might leave an example of how they were to be overcome; so that inviolate teaching and the lawful contest of the struggle might make them worthy to be crowned; because God, the just judge, will give the most ample rewards to those who love him and await his Son from heaven.

(2 Tim. 4:9–12) Be diligent to come to me as soon as possible; for Demas has forsaken me, loving this world, and has gone to Thessalonica, Crescens to Galatia, Titus to Dalmatia; Luke alone is with me. Take Mark and bring him with you; for he is useful to me in the ministry: but Tychicus I have sent to Ephesus. Although it is clear that the whole instruction of a churchman is contained in the epistle, yet because of his great love he longs for his presence: and he bears witness to Mark for this reason, that he is mindful of his past labours.

(2 Tim. 4:13) The cloak (paenula) that I left at Troas with Carpus, bring with you when you come, and the books, but especially the parchments. The Apostle was a Jew by nature, and had nothing foreign about him. Whence, then, did he have a cloak? But because he was from Tarsus, whose people are known to have been received into alliance with the Romans, so that thereafter they were called Roman citizens, for that reason they must also have had a curia, into which they would assemble wearing the paenula after the manner of the Romans. For whoever at that time had gone out with gifts to meet the Romans were granted perpetual immunity and were called Roman citizens and their brothers, as the same Apostle bears witness. It may therefore be that he had a cloak, if not his own, since he had given himself wholly to the Law, then his father's.

(2 Tim. 4:14–15) Alexander the coppersmith showed me many evils: the Lord will repay him according to his works. Avoid him yourself too; for he greatly resisted our words. This Alexander and the Demas mentioned above were colleagues. These were formerly with the Apostle, feigning friendship toward him: and because the deceitful are proved by time, both withdrew from him. But this coppersmith, who was the bolder in evil, so withdrew from him that he even stood forth as an opponent of his words. Since, then, he had dared to contradict the Apostle, not undeservedly does he admonish that he be avoided, leaving him to the judgment of God.

(2 Tim. 4:16) At my first defence no one stood by me; but all forsook me: may it not be imputed to them. He calls the afflictions and tribulations inflicted on him his defence; for to Christians suffering is life, and persecution a defence: for affliction inflicted for righteousness' sake defends him on the day of judgment, establishing the faithless as guilty. So he complains that at the very beginning of the affliction brought upon him he was forsaken: and because he knows that this is the custom, he does not want it imputed to them; for the disciples too left the Lord when he was seized by the Jews; since indeed it is written in the prophet: I will strike the shepherd, and the sheep of the flock will be scattered (Zech. 13:7).

(2 Tim. 4:17) But the Lord stood by me and strengthened me, so that through me the preaching might be fulfilled, and all the Gentiles might hear. Because he says that he was fortified by the aid of our Lord and increased in power, so that he might fulfil the preaching among all the Gentiles, for that reason he asks pardon for those by whom he was forsaken in his need; so that, being well accepted by God, he may not seek vengeance. For on this account he believes that he was helped the more by God, because he was forsaken by men.

(2 Tim. 4:18) And I was delivered from the mouth of the lion. The Lord will deliver me from every evil work, and will bring me safe into his heavenly kingdom: to whom be glory for ever, amen. In like manner Peter too, his fellow apostle, says: For our adversary the devil goes about, roaring like a lion, seeking whom he may devour (1 Pet. 5:8). But because he found no occasion in the Apostle by which to deceive him, he stirred up affliction and punishments against him through his sons; so that, if not as a sinner, then at least as a righteous man he might yet be removed from this life, lest he exhort many to the faith. When he had all but accomplished this, the Apostle, delivered by the providence of God, mocks him, testifying that he is weak, because he was snatched from his mouth; because what he desired he could not accomplish. Therefore he rejoices that by the aid of God he was snatched from death, and he is so secure about the providence of God that he believes he will be delivered from future temptations: and not only delivered for the present, but also that he will be saved on the day of judgment and will reign with the Lord Christ for eternal ages.

(2 Tim. 4:19–21) Greet Priscilla, and Aquila, and the household of Onesiphorus. Erastus remained at Corinth: but Trophimus I left sick at Miletus. Be diligent to come before winter. These are Aquila and Priscilla, with whom he lodged and worked with his hands (Acts 18:3). And this is the Onesiphorus to whom he bears witness in the second epistle (2 Tim. 1:16). Yet how is it that, with the Apostle present, who raised the dead, Trophimus was sick? But signs were done for the sake of the unbelieving: and because merit is laid up with God from different causes; so that whether in tribulation or in sickness, he who gives thanks to God and does not seek help from the opposing side may advance in merits with God. And because of the rains and the cold he urged him to come to him in autumn. Eubulus greets you, and Pudens, Linus and Claudia, and all the brothers. In the customary manner the epistle is completed with the greeting of the brothers.

The Lord Jesus Christ be with your spirit. This is the Apostle's subscription, which he says is the sign in every epistle. And because the Son of God is the same as the son of man, and is called both Jesus and Christ: so that both man and God may be signified, he is called by two names. Yet by each name a dignity is designated, which is the reason for which the names were given.
