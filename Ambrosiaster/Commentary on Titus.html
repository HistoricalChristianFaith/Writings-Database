Commentary on Titus

Latin source: the commentary on the Epistle to Titus by the anonymous Roman writer called Ambrosiaster (written at Rome about 366–384), from the Maurist text of 1690, printed under the name of Ambrose in J.-P. Migne, Patrologia Latina 17 (Paris, 1845), cols. 497–504, verified against the page images and collated in full with the Maurist edition (Paris, 1690). Migne's misprints are corrected where the Maurist edition he reprints or Ballerini's edition (Milan, 1877) has the right reading, as are his misprinted Scripture references and verse labels. The lemmata are translated from Ambrosiaster's own Old Latin text of Paul, and all other Scripture as he quotes it, from his own Latin; italics mark the lemmata and the other Scripture he quotes. The edition's verse labels are given as (Titus chapter:verse). This English translation is released into the public domain.

Prologue

The Apostle made Titus a bishop; and therefore he admonishes him to be careful in ecclesiastical ordination: that is, with regard to certain men who by a kind of pretence display themselves as worthy, so that they may hold a lofty order; and at the same time that the heretics of the circumcision too are to be rebuked.

Chapter I

(Titus 1:1–4) Paul, a servant of God, but an apostle of Jesus Christ according to the faith of the elect of God and the acknowledgement of his truth, which is according to godliness, in hope of eternal life, which God, who is true, promised before the ages of time; but in his own times he manifested in the preaching his word, which has been entrusted to me according to the command of God our Savior; to Titus, my genuine son according to the common faith. Grace and peace from God the Father and the Lord Jesus Christ our Savior. He declares that the gift decreed by God, which he had promised before the ages, was manifested in the time of the Savior in the preaching of the apostles; for so he had promised. This too he says was entrusted to him, that he might make known to the Gentiles the predestined mercy of God; so that Titus also, his true son, that is, according to the Gospel (for this is true, what is eternal), also revering this will of God with care, might preach the grace of God and peace, so that, having received the gift, they may become peaceable with God and the Father, because all things are from him; and with Christ Jesus our Savior, because he too freed us from death.

(Titus 1:5–11) For this reason I left you in Crete, that you should correct the things that are lacking and appoint presbyters city by city, as I also arranged for you: if anyone is without crime, the husband of one wife, having faithful children, not under accusation of luxury, or disobedient. For a bishop must be without crime, as the steward of God: not insolent, not wrathful, not given to wine, not a striker, not seeking shameful gains; but hospitable, kind, prudent, just, holy, continent, holding fast that word which is faithful according to doctrine, so that he may be able to exhort in sound doctrine and to refute those who contradict. For there are many who are not obedient, vain talkers and seducers, especially those who are of the circumcision, who must be reproved: who subvert whole houses, teaching what they ought not, for the sake of shameful gain. What has been read is plain; for he wants a bishop to be ordained who is both holy in works and prudent in words, so that he may be able both to exhort the brethren and to convict those who contradict, especially those who were from the Jews, who under the name of Christ taught men to judaize (Gal. 2:4), whom elsewhere he calls false brethren. For though reborn in Christ, they were not pure Christians; for they wished to venerate partly the Law, partly Christ. These are the ones he means, who were subverting houses with that perverse doctrine. For they were eager for shameful gains of a twofold kind; because they did these things all the more in order to please the Jews, from whom they hoped for support, that they might gain money and the foreskin. These were their gains, as the Lord says to the Jews: You walk sea and dry land to make one proselyte (Matt. 23:15). These two he calls shameful gains; because he who teaches under the name of God, and not for the sake of devotion but of gain, is a hunter of shameful gain; likewise also he who wishes to acquire someone through circumcision. Hence elsewhere he says: And glory in their shameful parts (Phil. 3:19).

(Titus 1:12–13) One of themselves, their own prophet, said: The Cretans are always liars, evil beasts, lazy bellies. This testimony is true. One of the Cretans, made better by the discipline of the Lord, expressed what was to be thought of the untaught Cretans: that they were both evil and wished to eat while idle; for they wished to feed on malice, men who, though they are of no use, are eager also to do harm. So the evil is twofold. Such were the Cretans; hence he calls this man a prophet, but in the matter of the Cretans. For he asserts that this man spoke things so true of them as to be a prophet; for it is impossible for a prophet to be deceived.

(Titus 1:14) For which cause rebuke them sharply, that they may be sound in the faith, not giving heed to Jewish fables and to the commandments of men who turn themselves away from the truth. He commands that they be pressed hard in the correcting of them, so that they may be converted and follow those things that are just and worthy of the law of God, turning themselves away from Jewish fables; because in the Law no one is justified before God, how much less in the commandments of men! For whatever is set against the truth is human invention; for, not knowing the force of the Scriptures and the inner meaning of the words of the Law, they follow the colour, not the savour. Therefore they are said to tell fables, not the truth; for they think that one must never depart from the things that Moses handed down, as, for example, concerning foods, or marriages, or new moons, or the blood of a weasel, or a house unclean for seven days; though they know that their forefathers Abraham, Isaac and Jacob were justified without these things and were called friends of God; and as the prophet Ezekiel testifies that, because they had profaned the law of God and his justifications, they received precepts and commandments that were not good, in which they could not be justified. Hence the Lord too says to the Jews: Because Moses wrote these things for the hardness of your heart (Mark 10:5). Hence it is that the apostle Peter too: Why do you lay, he says, a yoke upon the necks of the brethren, which neither our fathers nor we have been able to bear (Acts 15:10)? For if they had been useful, they would have been given then, when the Law too was given on the tablets; but because, after such great signs of wonders and after the Law had been published, they were found irreverent and distrustful and stiff-necked, they received commandments of such a kind and so many, in which they could not draw breath. Hence it came about that the good also laboured along with the bad, though they would receive a reward. These things, therefore, were given as a punishment, so that at the time when the promised gift of God came, the mercy of God should loosen all the things that it had previously forbidden. Not understanding this, the Jews remain under the yoke, and wish to bind the liberty of certain people with these chains; and therefore he admonishes that these men be rebuked, so that they may be turned away from them.

(Titus 1:15) All things indeed are clean to the clean; but to those who are defiled and without faith nothing is clean, but both their mind and their conscience are polluted. He indicates that those are clean who know both for what reason these things were forbidden and for what reason they were afterwards permitted. For if in Genesis it is read that all things which God made are good (Gen. 1:31), there is no doubt that they are also clean; but those who do not understand are unclean, for they are accusers of Scripture. For if they are read to have been made good, why are they forbidden as if execrable? If they are not execrable, because they are good, they are known to have been forbidden because of hardness of heart, because men were held unworthy to use the whole creation of God. For those who are seen to be prohibited from the better things, their mind and conscience are polluted; because they accuse the creation of God in order to excuse themselves.

(Titus 1:16) They profess that they know God, but in deeds they deny him; since they are execrable, disobedient, and reprobate for every good work. Although the saying pertains to all heretics, yet he is understood to have spoken this of the Jews, who say that they alone know the God of Abraham, but deny him by their works, as the Lord says: If you were sons of Abraham, you would do the works of Abraham (John 8:39). How then can they know the God of Abraham, who are ignorant of his mystery and his faith, following not the works of the Gospel but those of the law of deeds, in new moons and circumcision and the rest, disturbers, ungodly and irreverent? These are the works that do not know God.

Chapter II

(Titus 2:1) But speak the things that befit sound doctrine. That which is called sound doctrine is what knows God; for the doctrine of God is saving.

(Titus 2:2) That the older men be sober, grave, prudent, sound in faith, in love, in patience. He shows who are of sound doctrine, so as to indicate that those who are less sober, less grave, unbelieving, without love and impatient are disciples of unsound doctrine. For by these works God is not known, nor his righteousness.

(Titus 2:3–5) The aged women in like manner, in a bearing worthy of religion, not slanderers, not enslaved to much wine, teaching good things; that they may train the young women to love their husbands, to love their children, to be prudent, modest, chaste, having care of the house, subject to their own husbands: that the word of God be not blasphemed. He commands the older women to be such that they may be an example of good conduct to the younger; so that, if they have unbelieving husbands, these may rather rejoice with them than blaspheme the name of God, when they see that for the fear of God they obey their husbands more readily.

(Titus 2:6–8) Exhort the younger men likewise to be continent, in all things showing yourself an example of good works, in doctrine, in integrity, in gravity, a sound word, irreproachable; that he who is on the opposite side may be abashed, having nothing worthy of reproof to say of us. Since youth is wont to go beyond measure, indeed to be the readier to fall, therefore he bids it be held in with wholesome reins, so that, bridled, it may be governed by the divine laws. And so that this may not be arduous or difficult to bring about, he commands the teacher to be a pattern, that he may show how the things he teaches are done; so that those who are profane and enemies of the faith may blush, seeing that what he teaches in words he proves true by deeds.

(Titus 2:9–10) That slaves be subject to their masters in all things, excellent, not answering back, not defrauding, but showing all good faith; that they may adorn the doctrine of God our Savior in all things. He says that the doctrine of the Lord is adorned by good works and faith; for the doctrine is pleasing then, if he who professes it does not make it hateful, denying it by his deeds, but makes it lovable and true; for what is true cannot be unseemly. When, therefore, words are fulfilled by the deeds that follow, they are shown to be true, and through this seemly. Then masters can not only not hold their slaves back from the faith, but even rejoice in it; because they see that their faith profits them too. For no one who keeps the faith of God can be unfaithful to men.

(Titus 2:11–12) For the grace of God our Savior has shone upon all men, instructing us that, denying impiety and worldly desires, we should live temperately and justly and godly in this world. He says that the gift of God has shone upon men through Christ, that is, the truth of the one God made manifest in Christ: that with a godly profession we should proclaim the Creator in the unity of the Trinity, which before was hidden from the human race, but has now shone forth by the mercy of God; so that, avoiding the darkness of error, that is, fleeing the ignorance and impiety of worldly men, we may be found godly towards God the parent of all, professing him in the truth which the Gospel of his Son hands down; and that we may be able to have the reward of this, let us do good works. For just as there is no hope for him who proclaims the Father alone, if he does not profess the Son also in the same truth, so too the hope of him who has only the profession of faith without good works is in vain.

(Titus 2:13–15) Awaiting the blessed hope and the coming of the glory of the blessed God and of our Savior Jesus Christ, who gave himself for us, to redeem us from all iniquity and to cleanse for himself a people abounding, zealous for good works. He says that this is the blessed hope of believers: that they await the coming of the glory of the great God, which is to be revealed with Christ as judge, in whom the power and glory of God the Father will be seen; so that they may obtain the reward of their faith. For to this end Christ redeemed us, that, following a pure life and filled with good works, we may be able to be heirs of the kingdom of God. These things speak and exhort, and rebuke with all authority. Let no one despise you.

Chapter III

(Titus 3:1) Admonish them to be subject to principalities and powers, to obey what is said, to be ready for every good work. He admonishes them what the salvation of believers is, to make it plain, when they are rebuked, so that they may know for what good they are being fitted: that they may not take it harshly, being sure that they are corrected for their own salvation; and that they may be slow to evil but ready for good, not ignorant of what ought to be rendered to each.

(Titus 3:2) To blaspheme no one, not to be quarrelsome, but modest, showing all gentleness towards all men. He wants Christians to appear humble to all men; for thus the faithless can be called to the hope that is to come.

(Titus 3:3) For we too were once ill-advised, disobedient, erring, slaves to various desires and pleasures, living in malice and envy, hateful, hating one another. He recalls that these good things, which unbelief does not have, have been given to Christians, so that, shining in all righteousness, they may be made manifest as worshippers of God. For when those who were once ill-advised are now seen to be of sound counsel in all things, God is praised in them.

(Titus 3:4–7) But when the goodness and kindness of our saving God shone forth, not by works of righteousness which we did, but according to his mercy he saved us through the washing of regeneration and renewal of the Holy Spirit, whom he poured out upon us abundantly through Jesus Christ our Savior; that, justified by his grace, we may be made heirs according to the hope of eternal life. This good that is seen to flourish in Christians springs from the root of the divine godliness; for God by his mercy has saved us through Christ, reborn by whose grace we have received the Holy Spirit abundantly, so that we may be able to lean upon good works, he himself helping us in all things, that through these we may be able to attain the inheritance of the kingdom of heaven. Wherefore we ought to submit to him with all devotion and obey his precepts; because whatever is beautiful in us, he himself paints with spiritual strokes.

(Titus 3:8) A faithful saying: and concerning these things I wish you to affirm firmly, so that those who have believed God may be careful to accomplish good works. These things are good and useful to men. Nothing is so plain as that mercy and kindness are good for the present and useful for the future; for here it has praise, and on the day of judgment a crown. For everyone who believes God ought to be zealous for mercy, through which he lives. The ruler of the people, therefore, is admonished to be careful about these things.

(Titus 3:9) But avoid foolish questions, and enumerations of origins, and contentions, and battles over the Law; for they are useless and vain. He calls those questions foolish which have no effect for salvation and confer no skill of knowledge for any use. With these words he marks out the dogmas of many heretics. Although all things that are not true are to be called foolish, yet it is one man who brings in fabulous things, and another who with cunning evasion puts forward a thing full of poison, as Arius does, and Photinus. Now what is fabulous is not the Law but heresy, as far as concerns that time of the Jews, when certain of them claimed for themselves the prerogative of the origins of the patriarchs, which arose from marriages, though there is no merit with God from this. Others, again, taught the baptism of pitchers and other vessels, and that the blood of a weasel must be expiated with great care (Lev. 11:29); and that the burial place of Moses was hidden for this reason, that he might not be raised up by magicians (if this were true, the burial places of the other saints too would have had to be hidden); and that Solomon built the temple with the help of demons, though a huge multitude laboured at that work: what is so fabulous?

(Titus 3:10–11) Avoid a heretical man after the first correction, knowing that such a one is perverse, and sins, being condemned by himself. These are heretics, who attack the Law through the words of the Law; for they build their own sense upon the words of the Law, so as to commend the depravity of their mind by the authority of the Law. For impiety, knowing that the authority of the Law has great power, composes its deceit under its name, so that, since an evil thing cannot be acceptable by itself, it may be commended by the goodness of the name. Therefore, so that men of this kind may be without excuse, he says that they must be corrected once: for if they are corrected more often, they will become more practised in evil. And he who corrects them often seems to drive them on, so that they become more zealous for the ruin of many: therefore they are to be let go, so that, having become more careless, they may perhaps perish alone.

(Titus 3:12) When I send Artemas or Tychicus to you, hasten to come to me at Nicopolis; for there I have decided to winter. After sending the letter he wishes also to see him in person, for affection's sake, and to establish him more fully in ecclesiastical discipline.

(Titus 3:13–14) Send ahead Zenas the expert in the Law and Apollos with care, that nothing may be lacking to them; and let ours also learn to take the lead in good works for necessary uses, that they may not be unfruitful. Each is a man distinguished in wisdom and in works; and therefore he bids that the necessary provisions be given them by those who had believed, so that they might refresh them; because it was right that those who handed on spiritual things should not lack carnal things. For although he calls Zenas an expert in the Law, yet Apollos was perfect in the Scriptures; but because Zenas had been of this profession in the synagogue, he calls him so.

(Titus 3:15) All who are with me greet you. For brevity's sake he indicates that all greet him together. Greet those who love us in the faith. He wishes those to be seen as worthy of his greeting who love him faithfully with the affection of Christ our Lord. The grace of God be with you all. Now he greets in general all the brethren and the household of Titus.
