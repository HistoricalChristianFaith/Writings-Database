Commentary on Colossians

Latin source: the commentary on the Epistle to the Colossians by the anonymous Roman writer called Ambrosiaster (written at Rome about 366–384), from the Maurist text of 1690, printed under the name of Ambrose in J.-P. Migne, Patrologia Latina 17 (Paris, 1845), cols. 421–442, verified against the page images and collated in full with the Maurist edition (Paris, 1690). Migne's misprints are corrected where the Maurist edition he reprints or Ballerini's edition (Milan, 1877) has the right reading, as are his misprinted Scripture references and verse labels. The lemmata are translated from Ambrosiaster's own Old Latin text of Paul, and all other Scripture as he quotes it, from his own Latin; italics mark the lemmata and the other Scripture he quotes. The edition's verse labels are given as (Col. chapter:verse). This English translation is released into the public domain.

Prologue

False apostles were striving to overthrow the Colossians, so that after the preaching of Epaphras or of Archippus they might ensnare the simplicity of their minds with philosophical disputations: that they should not despise the reckonings of the elements, by which human life is, as it were, governed. For this reason the Apostle exhorts them by letter, and warns them not to think that there is any hope in anyone besides Christ, and so be deceived.

Chapter I

(Col. 1:1–3) Paul, an apostle of Christ Jesus by the will of God, and Timothy our brother, to those who are at Colossae, to the holy and faithful brothers in Christ Jesus. Grace to you and peace from God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ. He writes the head of the letter to them in his usual manner, in which he goes on to say that he is addressing his letter to brothers who are both holy and faithful, for whom he earnestly desires that the grace of God may remain through Christ.

(Col. 1:4–6) We give thanks to God and the Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, praying always for you, having heard of your faith in Christ Jesus, and of the love which you have toward all the saints because of the hope which is laid up for you in heaven, which you heard before in the word of the truth of the Gospel, which has come to you, as it has also into the whole world. Since God has thought them worthy to have his Son entrusted to them, he returns thanks to him in his prayers. Then, because of the love which they had toward the saints, they being confident that for this love a firm hope stands with God, by which he is believed to be going to reward those who believe and love one another, Christ being the author, whose name has come to all the Gentiles, so that they may be multiplied in faith in him.

(Col. 1:7–8) And it is bearing fruit and growing, as also among you, from the day on which you heard and knew the grace of God in truth, as you learned from Epaphras, our beloved fellow servant, who is a faithful minister of Christ for you, who also made known to us your love in the spirit. He goes on, in eager words, to say that we grow and bring forth life-giving fruit as an example to the other Gentiles; so that, from their having received the grace of God, they might advance both in number and in work, joining to their faith those who would become their brothers in Christ. For many were drawn by the example of their good works, as Epaphras testifies for them, who ministered the grace of Christ to them in the Apostle's stead, which they at once drank in as a spiritual drink in the love of the Lord, a love which is not in the flesh but in the spirit.

(Col. 1:9–10) Therefore we also, from the day on which we heard, do not cease praying and asking for you, that you may be filled with the knowledge of his will in all wisdom and spiritual understanding, in this: that you walk worthily of him unto all that pleases him, in every good work. The Apostle, hearing of their devoted and ready will concerning the faith of Christ, prays for them, that, having come to know the will of God more fully, they might fulfil by reasonable work the devotion which they had learned on hearing the faith of Christ; lest so faithful a dedication of mind and such strength of faith, while doing something unadvisedly and unskilfully, should lose the fruit of its labour. For that is gladly credited which is done with a devoted mind, and prudently.

(Col. 1:11–13) Bearing fruit and growing in the knowledge of God, taking strength in all power according to the might of his glory unto all patience and long-suffering, with joy giving thanks to God and the Father, who has called you into a share of the lot of the saints in light, who has delivered us from the power of darkness, and transferred us into the kingdom of the Son of his love. He wants the increase of their works to come about with knowledge, so that they may not be ignorant of the hope of their faith; for they are firm and stable then, if they consider what has been promised for faith, and how great the might of the glory of God's majesty is in patience: that he bears with the human race in long expectation, though by its irreverence it not only neglects the Creator but even persecutes those who love him; so that they too may likewise be patient, so as to bear the reproaches and curses of the unbelievers, or, if affliction is brought upon them, await the judgment of God without doubting of it, but rather rejoicing in the promised life, with thanksgivings to God, who deigned to call the Gentiles and to bring them into the promise of the Jews, which is eternal light, that is, eternal life, everlasting truth. For, freed from the condition of darkness, that is, rescued from the underworld, in which we were held by the devil as much through our own offence as through the offence of Adam, who is the father of all who sin, we have been transferred through faith into the heavenly kingdom of the Son of God; so that God might show us with what love he loves us, when, raising us from the depths of Tartarus, he brought us into the heavens with his true Son. For now all believers who are devoted with a fixed mind, on departing from the world, are brought into the heavens with the Angel of the Father's right hand as their guide, which was not granted before death was vanquished.

(Col. 1:14) In whom we have redemption, and the remission of sins. Into the kingdom of this his Son God has transferred us, through whom he redeemed us and set us free; for the remission of sins makes men free, so that, freed from their debts, they cannot be held liable. For without faith in Christ there is no way out of the underworld; because one who is bound by sins cannot go out through the gates of Tartarus. Who is the image of the invisible God. The image of the invisible God cannot be visible, otherwise it is not an image; for what is invisible cannot be painted, nor can one who is visible see the invisible; for what is seen is either a body, or a shape, or some formed substance which the eyesight can take in, yet the whole of it is body: but God, who is foreign to all these, how can he have a visible image? But it is said thus so that the Son may be understood to be such as the Father is; so that, because he is from him and differs from him in nothing in the divinity of nature, he is called his image and form; lest he be believed by the faithless to be another God, although this is contained in the very name; for he is called the true Son of God for this reason, that he may be believed to be from him. But because of wrong understanding this too was added, that he should also be called his image; so that when he appears, whether in human shape or indeed in fire, as to Moses (Exod. 3:2), it may be understood that it is not the Father himself but the Son: and through his being called his image, he may be believed to differ from him in nothing. Hence he says to Philip: Philip, whoever sees me sees the Father also. How do you say, "Show us the Father"? Do you not believe that I am in the Father, and the Father is in me (John 14:9)? This is the unity and substantiality of Father and Son, that they are in one another; so that whoever sees the Son sees the Father also. Now let us see how the Son was seen, so that we may accordingly understand in what way Philip, by seeing the Son, sees the Father also. Certainly it was by understanding the divine works that they saw the Son, not with fleshly eyes. What the Son worked he said was the Father's, to show the unity of power. In the same way he was seen by Jacob also; whence he received the name Israel, that is, a man seeing God (Gen. 32:28). For this reason, then, he was called the image of the invisible God: so that he might be understood to be the one who was seen as God through the understanding; but that the Father has never appeared in any way, as is said in the Gospel: No one has ever seen God (John 1:18).

(Col. 1:16) The firstborn of all creation. He testifies that the Son was begotten before all creation, not created, so as to distinguish his birth from the making of creation: and so that this may become thoroughly clear, he added:

Because in him all things were created in heaven and on earth, visible and invisible, whether Thrones, or Dominions, or Principalities, or Powers; all things were created through him and in him. He signifies Christ as magnificent and glorious not only in this, that he was born before all things came to be: but he also says that all things were created in him, that is, so that the power of all things may be believed to be in him, and so to have proceeded, while all things are made through him: and this is to be the head of the whole creation; because it is through him that they began to be, so that they might exist. And the things that were made by him are said to have been made through him for this reason, that he was born for this: to make the creation; this is to have made in him and through him. For his birth did not profit himself, but us; because he who was in these things had no need to be born. How does he become the only Son of God the Father, how does he himself know the things of God the Father that are unbegotten? For he who proceeded from God was in God, so that God was one: and so it comes about that there is neither diminution of the Father nor advancement of the Son when he is born. And to convey more openly what and how great the power of the Son is, he shows what things exist through him, namely Thrones, Dominions and Principalities, and Powers; so that accordingly nothing at all may be believed to be less in him than in the Father.

(Col. 1:17) And he is before all. He is before all, because he alone was born of God the Father, indeed of the unbegotten substance of God. But these things came to subsist by his power, which he has from the Father through generation; not so that they have communion of substance with him, but they began from nothing at the nod of God the Father, the Son acting. And all things hold together in him. They hold together in him because without him they are nothing; for unless they were subject to him, straying through error, they would become liable, as it were, to perdition.

(Col. 1:18) And he is the head of the body of the Church, who is the beginning, the firstborn from the dead; that he himself may be holding the primacy in all things. Christ is the head of the Church, provided that all, heavenly and earthly, are of one mind, so as to be the Church, that is, of one faith: otherwise, cut off from the head of the whole body, that is, separated from their Creator, they rave in a kind of madness and vanity. Who is the beginning, the firstborn from the dead; that he himself may be holding the primacy in all things. Since all things exist through him, the things that through some negligence had been made perishable had to be re-formed through him once more; lest it should redound to his injury if his work had been restored by another; so that, as he was born before all, so again he should be the first before all from the resurrection of the dead, the former life being restored; for he was born of God before all things, so as to create all things, which were not. But again, having taken flesh, he was born of a virgin, so as to abolish the sin which had entered the world from the virgin Eve. Born, then, and having died, he rose again, so that what he had made at first he might afterwards create anew; because through error they had become as it were dead; so that in all life he might always be first and prince.

(Col. 1:19) Because in him it pleased the Father that all fullness should dwell. The fullness is and remains in him; because he can always do all things, so that nothing is excepted which cannot be accomplished through him; so that he both made, and re-formed, and raised up what had fallen, and gave life to the dead. Hence he says: As the Father has life in himself, so he has given to the Son also to have life in himself (John 5:26); and: As the Father raises the dead and gives them life, so the Son also gives life to whom he wills. For the Father judges no one, but has given all judgment to the Son (ibid., 21–22). What is so just as that he should give to him the judgment of the things which he made through him, so that all the fullness of divinity should dwell in him, through this, as we have said, that he has all that power in himself: that is, that he is perfect God? For whoever does not have all these things is not God.

(Col. 1:20) And through him to reconcile all things unto himself, making peace through the blood of his cross, whether the things that are in heaven or the things that are on earth. This is what he mentioned above: that in order to restore through him all the things which he had made, since through a certain error they had already begun to be at variance with one another, and also with their author (for there is still error in the fleshly heavens), he humbled him by making him incarnate, so that, made man, he might show and teach how the things that are in the firmament and upon the earth could be reconciled to the Creator. And to bring this to completion, he suffered himself to be killed for the sake of men; so that those whom he had taught in the upper world, going out from here, cannot be held by the second death; so that his teaching might have the fruit of the first institution, and all things made through him might live in him as in their author, as Peter confesses in the Acts of the Apostles (Acts 3:15).

(Col. 1:21–22) And you, who were once estranged and enemies of his counsels in wicked works, but now are reconciled in the body of his flesh through death, ought to present yourselves holy and spotless and irreproachable in his sight. He recalls what the gift of God toward the Gentiles is, to show that they are debtors, and greatly so, in thanksgiving to God; for when they were enemies of his counsels, by which he had decreed to visit the human race through his servant Moses, whose teaching and authority they did not receive, being devoted to their idols, or rather to wicked works (for they worshipped their own works, which they had made), the mercy and providence of God nevertheless sought them out; so that they might obtain the grace of God freely, returning to him with a clean conscience; so that on the day of judgment, when nothing of the old error should be found in them, they might be incorporated into their Creator, who, to benefit the human race, became incarnate and was made man, so as to conquer death in his flesh.

(Col. 1:23–24) If indeed you persevere in the faith, grounded, steadfast and unmoved from the hope of the Gospel which you heard, which has been preached in all the creation that is under heaven. Of which I Paul was made a minister, who now rejoice in my sufferings for you, and fill up what remains of the afflictions of Christ in my flesh for his body, which is the Church. He declares that he exults in the tribulations which he was suffering, because he sees his own advancement in the faith of believers. For tribulation is not in vain when it gains for life the one for whom he suffers. These sufferings he says are inflicted on Christ, whose teaching, indeed, they persecute; so as both to weigh down the faithless with the belief of their impiety, and to proclaim to the faithful the love of God, whose Son even now suffers injuries for us. Now, therefore, he wants it understood for what reason he writes to them, when he says: If however you persevere, grounded in the faith; so that they might know that the passion of Christ profits them then, if they remain in the faith they have received. For this is why he declared from the beginning how great and how infinite the omnipotence of Christ's greatness is: to teach that hope must be placed in him alone; because all things are his, and because nothing can live without him, neither in heaven nor on earth. For he is before all, and all things hold together in him, because he himself holds the primacy in all things; so that if anyone thinks that he ought to be devoted to any of the elements or of the angels, or of the Powers, he may know that he errs. For this is the one Son of God, the head of every principality and beginning, whose Gospel has been preached in the whole world, beginning from Moses, who said to the Jews: The Lord your God will raise up for you a Prophet from your brothers, like me. Him you shall hear; for it shall be that whoever does not hear that prophet shall be rooted out from the people (Deut. 18:15–16). Hence the Lord says to the Jews: Moses wrote of me (John 5:46). Therefore this one alone is to be heard and worshipped, and no one is to be reckoned alongside him; because he who holds the head has the remaining part subject to him. For whoever humbles himself to things that are subject is wholly in error; because, not holding the head, from which the whole body is held together for life, he is a mere trunk.

(Col. 1:25–26) Of which I was made a minister according to the dispensation of God which was given to me for you, to fulfil the word of God in the mystery which was hidden from ages of ages and from generations. He says with confidence that he was made by God through Christ a minister of the Gospel among the Gentiles, so that the mystery begun by the Lord, which had been unknown from the ages, might be fulfilled through a fit and effective servant.

(Col. 1:27–28) But now it has been made known to his saints, to whom God willed to show what are the riches of the majesty of the mystery of God among the nations, which is Christ in you, the hope of glory, whom we proclaim. He asserts that the mystery which lay hidden from the ages has now been revealed, that is, shown in the time of the apostles: that the Gentiles too were to be admitted, without circumcision of the flesh, to the faith of Christ, who had been promised to the Jews. For formerly, before the preaching of the faith, Gentiles were commanded to be circumcised if they wished to come to the Law: but now, by the mercy of God, they have been ordered to be received into the faith just as they are. This the Jews thought unworthy: that Christ, in whom was the hope of the Jews, should make the Gentiles too sharers of his glory.

(Col. 1:29) Admonishing every man, and teaching in all wisdom, that we may present every man perfect in Christ: in which I labour, striving according to his working, which works in me in power. He says that this is the true wisdom, which is in the discipline of the Lord, when Christ is acknowledged. The Apostle testifies that he himself does this with great care; so that, instructing every man in this wisdom, he may present him complete to God in the understanding of Christ, so that he may attain the mystery of his birth from God, and of his birth from Mary, and of his whole work in things heavenly and earthly. He says that he has a contest over this against the faithless, with signs of power assisting him, so that through these unbelief may be overcome and suppressed. For whoever has dared to contradict his words with a certain subtlety of diabolical cunning must, corrected by shame, yield to power.

Chapter II

(Col. 2:1–3) For I want you to know how great a concern I have for you, and for those who are at Laodicea, and for as many as have not seen my face in the flesh; that their hearts may receive consolation, when they have been instructed in love unto all the riches of the fullness of understanding, unto the knowledge of the mystery of God in Christ, in whom are hidden all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge. He testifies that he was no less concerned for those whom he had not seen at all in the flesh than for those to whom he preached in person; so that they might know that they were loved by him with no less affection, because he had not seen them, than he loved the others among whom he was; so as to cherish their souls in love, and that they might become ready through the letter, and receive the knowledge of the mystery of God in Christ, observing that these are the riches of wisdom and knowledge, if Christ is acknowledged as one to be adored in the fullness of his deity. The whole mystery, therefore, of the mystery of God is in Christ. For he is the one in whom every creature, unless it has hoped, will perish; so that the power of all things is in him, so that he who knows him seems to have the knowledge of all things whatsoever that exist, and no other besides him is sought to be worshipped; because the strength of all powers is in him; for all have from him. For since he is one, he can do all things: wherefore he is also called the head of all, so that through him all things may be seen to subsist. Fittingly, therefore, all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge are said to be hidden in him. For the whole account of the knowledge of the entire creation, heavenly and earthly, must be in him who is the head of them all, whether of the Angels, or of the sun, or of the moon, or of the stars, or of the other things; all skill of knowledge is in their author; so that he who acknowledges him seeks nothing further, neither wisdom nor power; because he has acknowledged him in whom is perfect power and perfect wisdom. For whatever one has thought he would find elsewhere, he will find fully and abundantly in him. For what is wiser than Solomon? what more prudent than Daniel? To them God gave wisdom above the rest for this reason, to make known to unbelievers that he is the author of all wisdom. Thus Nebuchadnezzar says to Daniel, marvelling at his wisdom: Truly your God is himself the living God, and the revealer of mysteries (Dan. 2:47). Yet it is hidden from every unbeliever that all wisdom and knowledge is in Christ; because they do not read astrology in the Gospels, nor geometry in the Apostle, nor arithmetic or music in the prophets: which things are for this reason despised by our people, because they do not pertain to salvation, but rather send men into error and call them away from God; so that while they apply themselves to these disputations of reasonings, they take no care of their own soul. For what wisdom is so true as to have known what profits, and to have despised what harms? For it does harm, because it hinders the soul from taking care of itself. Not without reason, therefore, has he who has acknowledged Christ found the treasure of wisdom and knowledge; for he has acknowledged what is useful to him.

(Col. 2:4–5) This I say, that no one may circumvent you in subtlety of speech. For though I am absent in body, yet I am with you in spirit, rejoicing, and seeing your order, and supplying what is lacking to the profit of your faith in Christ. He warns them that their understanding should not be led astray by the talk of evil men and the cunning of iniquity; because the wise of the world are eager, with a certain art and with the minutiae of disputations, to ensnare the souls of the simple; so as to draw them away, by the reasoning of worldly things, from the hope which is in Christ. Hence the Apostle says that he is present there in spirit, so that, having reverence for him before their eyes, they might keep themselves from men of this kind. For if the spirit of Elisha the prophet saw the fraud of Gehazi as he went to Naaman the Syrian to receive in his name what had not been commanded him, and saw that Naaman, coming down with a ready mind, had given him more than he had been asked (2 Kings 5:26); how much more could the Apostle see in the spirit the things he spoke of! For there was greater grace in the apostles than in the prophets. And to make them more ready concerning the tradition of the Gospel, he says that he rejoices in the ordering of their way of life; so that, knowing whereby God is pleased, they might become firmer in that through which, being made worthy, they might learn the understanding of it for the advancement of the profit of their faith.

(Col. 2:6–7) As therefore you have received Christ Jesus the Lord, walk in him, rooted and built up in him, and confirmed in faith, as you have also been taught, walking in it with thanksgiving. This he warns and exhorts, that their devotion should not be diminished, and that they should not withdraw from the faith which they had attained, and, overthrown by wicked fables, receive Christ otherwise than they had learned. For many corrupters of the faith were going about to seduce the simple, proclaiming Christ deceitfully and with pretence; and he warns them to avoid these with great caution; so that, stable and firm upon the foundation of the Trinity, in the hope of things to come they might give abundant thanks for the mercy of God.

(Col. 2:8–9) See that no one plunders you through philosophy and empty deceit according to the tradition of men, according to the elements of this world, and not according to Christ; because in him dwells all the fullness of the divinity bodily. He means earthly philosophy, by which those are wont to be seduced who desire to be judged prudent in the world. It is composed of arguments and of the subtlety of certain minutiae, as though it had investigated the reasons of nature, having some things mixed in which may serve as encouragements or lessons for a good life, and ordered sometimes concerning measures, and also concerning numbers, sometimes concerning the qualities and quantities of the elements. Whoever falls into its disputations escapes barely and rarely; for he is held fast by plausible causes and fictitious matters, thinking nothing so true as what is seen and understood in the elements. And because the things that are present and are seen with the eyes seem pleasant and delightful, they seduce a good many, who, in despair of the future, think the spiritual account is to be despised and laughed at, assigning all power to the stars, being subject to carnal reasoning; holding that heavy things are not to be believed able to be raised into heaven, nor light things to come down to the earth, nor anything bodily to be generated without a mixing of things, and so opposing the power and providence of God. For they know that it is read in the ancient divine volumes of the Hebrews that many things were done through Moses which human reason does not grasp; and in like manner in the new books, things done by the Lord, or through the apostles, which carnal treatment forbids to be believed. This tradition, then, or philosophy, he calls deceitful and empty; because it was ordered not by the power of God, but by the weakness of human reasoning, which confines the power of God within its own knowledge, so that he is not believed to act otherwise than carnal reason confines itself; because they assign a diversity of divinity to the individual elements for worship, so as to bind the souls of the unskilled, lest they reach out to the true God. Wherefore he warns that this tradition is to be guarded against; because it is a worshipper of the world, not of God, who is one: nor does it draw towards Christ, but away from Christ, in whom is the perfection of divinity. For all that the Father has, he gave to the Son, when he begot him bodily in the divinity of fullness; so that, since he himself is the head, and his creation is his body, whatever the heavenly creation is thought able to do, all this may be believed to be fully in Christ; so that no one may suppose that anyone else is to be worshipped. For whatever one has thought great in anyone, he will find more of it in him; because all have from him, as the members from the head: but he himself is always full.

(Col. 2:10) And you are filled in him, who is the head of every Principality and Power. He signifies that more is given to believers than can be contained in the elements; because he who is in Christ is filled with the abundance of divinity, which the elements do not have. Thus the Holy Spirit dwells in believers, which could not be granted to the elements; because it was unworthy and unfitting that the invisible should dwell in a visible substance, and the incorporeal in things that are sensible. Let their worshippers, therefore, hear and come, and believe in Christ; so that they may become greater than those whom they now worship.

(Col. 2:11–12) In whom you also have been circumcised with a circumcision not made by hand, in the despoiling of the body of the flesh, in the circumcision of Christ, buried together with him in baptism: in whom also you have risen together through faith in the working of God, who raised him from the dead. Those are circumcised in Christ who, having cut away the worship of all human tradition, are joined to Christ, circumcised in heart, not in flesh, that is, serving in the spirit, not in the flesh; so that they may be converted from earthly things to heavenly, from human things to divine, from error to truth, coupled through love to their head and author. With him they were buried in baptism: and they have risen again through faith in God, of whom they believe that after the example of Christ he will raise them from the dead. For there the old man is laid aside, and the new is put on: he dies to sins, that he may live to righteousness: he renounces the elements, that he may be joined to Christ, holding the example of the Savior, who rose from the dead, as the pledge of the resurrection to come. These things, therefore, he urges, that they persevere in the renunciation of the pomp and the deceptions of Satan. Satan urges the worship of the elements for this reason, to call men away from God, wishing to make them sharers in his apostasy. For because he cannot urge worship under his own name, since he is abhorred for that very name, he tries to fulfil his will under the name of another.

(Col. 2:13–15) And you, when you were dead in offences and in the foreskin of your flesh, he made alive together with him, forgiving us all offences, having blotted out the handwriting which was against us, the decree which was contrary to us: and he took it out of the way, and fixed it to the cross, and stripping off the principalities and powers, he made a show of them with authority, triumphing over them in himself. He says that the Gentiles were dead: first, indeed, because they refused to receive the Law, which was given as a testimony to the Creator and to condemn vices: then, because of the impiety of their sins, they were unworthy of life; and so he distinguished them from the Jews, as men who had been enemies of the one God. But the Jews, living in the confession of the one God, at the coming of Christ learn the mystery of God, which they were awaiting for their justification: so that in that which had crept upon them unawares, since it is impossible to be without sin, they might receive remission. Hence, after the case of the Gentiles, he brings in the Jews too, saying: Forgiving us all offences; so that after the particular he might add the general; because he forgave all men all offences. But because, as the Lord says, He to whom more has been forgiven ought to love more (Luke 7:43): for this reason he admonishes the Gentiles that, considering the causes of their evils, and what grace they have received from God in Christ, they should be solicitous with all devotion for the service of him by whom they have been freed from the various penalty of death. How, then, did he forgive us all offences? Having blotted out, he says, the handwriting which was adverse to us. And what it is to blot out, he added, saying: And he took it out of the way. So, then, he blotted it out when he took it out of the way. And he took it out of the way thus, when he fixed it, he says, to the cross. And to fix it to the cross, he said, is to despoil the principalities and powers publicly, and to triumph over them in Christ. These things, as they appear, are obscure, and therefore they must be made clear with care applied. This is the protection of God, which the Apostle sets forth by signifying various causes. For he recounts many things done whereby the human race might come to pardon; so that he not only forgave us our own offences, but also took away that sin which was from the transgression of Adam (which he called the handwriting in decrees), which did not allow us to rise from the dead; because if he had not taken this too out of the way, those who had received remission would, on departing hence, be held there in the underworld. Moreover, all offences were forgiven for this reason, that they might rise from the dead. For because death is from sin, when sin is taken away the resurrection of the dead comes about: which indeed could not have happened, unless he had fixed sin to the cross. For while the Savior, by not sinning, conquers sin, which held man liable, and moreover, though innocent, is killed by it: thus he crucified sin. For sin conquered is said to be put to death, for the cross is the death not of the Savior but of sin. For an innocent man who is killed in this way makes guilty those by whom he is killed. And by sin let us understand the princes and powers, at whose instigation the first Adam sinned. For this name must be referred to its authors, whose death he signified in the despoiling; for this is their death, that, conquered by the Savior, they are put to death while they are despoiled of the souls which they held in captivity. For they were made guilty: because, while they held souls with this authority, since these had sinned, they themselves were found to be greater sinners, when they killed him who had conquered them by not sinning. And so they were justly despoiled, as he said, publicly, that is, on the cross; which is to be openly triumphed over by God in Christ. For he is triumphed over who is openly conquered, and whose spoils are made public; for he who used to insult others is shown to all laid low. But the decree of which he speaks is not that which the pagans call fate; because they do not assert that that is contrary to all; for they say that some are happy from it, others unhappy. For he says that this decree was contrary to all; for it was a sentence adverse to all men.

(Col. 2:16–17) Let no one therefore judge you in food, or in drink, or in respect of a festival day, or of the new moons, or of the sabbath: which are a shadow of things to come, but the body is Christ's. Since all hope of life, and confidence, is in Christ, he has commanded that we ought to be subject to no superstition or law. Wherefore those who make a din, or who whisper something that does not pertain to the faith of Christ, are to be despised: but he alone is to be followed through whom death was conquered and the resurrection of the dead restored. For some of the Jews are wont to judge the faithful because they eat of foods which were forbidden through Moses, though God has made all things clean: but the Jews, having been judged unworthy to eat of these, were forbidden the better things. Again, they are wont to reproach us because we spurn their festival days, or because we do not observe the beginnings of months, which they call neomeniae (new moons): but above all because we do not keep the days of the sabbaths, so as to pass them in idleness and feasting with luxury: which pertains rather to offence than to grace. Now all these things are the body of Christ, because they are names of the elements through their effects. Hence it is that he says in the Gospel: For the Son of man is Lord even of the sabbath (Matt. 12:8); for all things are through him, which indeed were given through Moses in such a way as to be a shadow or figure of things to come, so that, when the truth appeared, the figure should cease. For just as, when the emperor is absent, his image has authority, but when he is present it has none; so also these things were to be observed in their time before the coming of the Lord, but now that he is present they lack authority. Does anyone acting in the master's stead hold dominion while the master himself is present? If the deputies of prefects become private persons when the prefects are present, how much more ought slaves, when their master is present, to be seen themselves also in service!

(Col. 2:18–19) Let no one deceive you, wishing, in humility of mind and the superstition of angels, the things which he sees, puffing himself up in vain, inflated by the mind of his flesh, and not holding that head, from which the whole body, supplied and knit together through joints and connections, grows unto the increase of God. What above he warned them to guard against under the name of philosophy, he now teaches more openly is to be watched for from those who, devoted to earthly wisdom, assert that those things are to be worshipped which lie before the gaze or appear to it; so that souls held fast by the worship of these may be kept bound beneath the firmament, lest they stretch themselves above the firmament to the higher heavens to adore the God of all. This is brought about by the work of the enemy, so as always to keep souls humbled upon the earth, where he knows that he himself, cast down from the heavens, will always be by the just judgment of God, feigning religion, though it is the greatest sacrilege. For they are puffed up as they survey the motion of the stars, which he calls angels, not by divine authority, but by human superstition, which profits for nothing except for loss: unwilling to hold the faith of Christ, who is the creator and Lord of all. For from him all have their origin; wherefore they are also called his body, if indeed they acknowledge him. For turned to him they are built up again, knit together in his faith, the Gospel supplying all as members of a body in the love of Christ, so that they make increase for God. For when those who through error had deserted him, diminishing the store of his riches while they deny that they are his, return to him, they make increase for God, because they are gained from among the lost. This concerns the pagans: again he speaks of the Jews, because they too serve under the elements, not the elements.

(Col. 2:20) If then you have died with Christ from the elements of this world, why do you still, as though living in this world, make decrees? Everyone who is baptized in Christ dies to the world; for he renounces all the errors of superstitions, so as to worship the faith of Christ alone. For when the things that are added to natural righteousness have been removed, there remains the discipline given by God, through which one lives to God through the hope which is in Christ: but he dies to those to whom he had been subject through error, when he denies those whom he had presumed to be gods. And so it comes about that, through the enmity of renunciation, man and the worship of the elements are said to be dead to each other. For in saying "world" he signifies the error of the flesh; for everything that is seen is assigned to the flesh. Hence it is that John the apostle too says: Do not, he says, love the world, nor the things that are in the world (1 John 2:15); that is, let us love neither the elements, of which the world is compacted, nor the errors which human tradition has invented: but Christ alone, who died for us; so as to join us to his body from among the lost. Those, therefore, who are buried together with Christ are dead to the elements, so as to care nothing for any of these; for they have learned that the things above are to be held, which can give perpetual life. For these things do not even give the present life, and they hinder the things to come from being laid hold of. But he who after baptism is in doubt about some things, thinking that certain of the old things are to be venerated, shows that he lives to the elements, which he decrees are to be worshipped, or doubts are to be despised; such a one will remain within the circles of the world, a stranger to God; because, clothed with the old man, he will not be able to pass beyond carnal things.

(Col. 2:21–22) Do not touch, do not defile, do not taste, do not handle, things which all are unto destruction and corruption through misuse, according to the precepts and doctrines of men. He forbids them in every way from hope in worldly things, because it is empty: that they should not worship them, not assent to them, not think of them, because the things they promise belong to corruption. For their use and worship generate destruction; because the precepts and doctrines are not of God but of men, and in them there is vain hope: seeing that human error invented these things against the Creator; so that, forbidding men the faith of the one God, it might bring in the superstition of many gods.

(Col. 2:23) Which things indeed have a reason of wisdom in the pretence of religion, and in humility of mind for the vexation of the body, not in any honour for the satiety and care of the flesh. Hence they think that they have a reason of wisdom, because they apply the name of religion to human tradition, so that it is called religion, though it is sacrilege; because what is against the author has been invented by a sacrilegious mind. It feigns a name, then, so that it might assert as true things that are false; and it humbles human minds on the earth, lest they raise themselves to the things above: or rather, worshipping earthly things, they fall away from the heavenly things above, and do harm to the body whose head is Christ according to the making of the first reason. For, weighed down by this, they could not be joined upwards to their head, so as to be restored in him, being made honoured: but, as if cut off from the first part of the body, they give themselves wholly to the world to fulfil the sense of the flesh. For human tradition is the fattening of carnal sense, which says that what the providence of the flesh has handed down is sufficient; for it seems to have a reason of wisdom according to the flesh, because every bodily birth is from the elements: and therefore, they say, it is not unfitting to bow down to these by whose governance the human race is ruled, so that a man subjects himself to the things which he sees to be the most brilliant of the world: and spiritual things ought not to be sought; because, they say, it is granted to use these alone as guides; and by this reasoning they neglect the author.

Chapter III

(Col. 3:1) If then you have risen together with Christ, seek the things that are above, where Christ is, seated at the right hand of God. He asserts that those have risen with Christ in baptism who think on the things above the heavens, where the seat of Christ is, where God the Father has given his right hand to his Son, that he may reign and judge; for it is not for a judge to sit on the left. Yet the resurrection has two forms: first, that the soul rises from the earth to the heavens; then, when the body has been received back, that it rises, caught up to meet Christ.

(Col. 3:2) Mind the things that are above, not the things that are upon the earth. He exhorts us to seek the heavens above, which are the dwellings of the Eternal, and to pay no heed to all those things that are in the firmament and under the firmament. For whoever occupies himself with these superstitions will suffer a hindrance; for he will not be able to pass beyond these things to which he has bowed himself, as to masters.

(Col. 3:3) For you are dead, and your life is hidden with Christ in God. To the mind of the world Christ seems dead, and his disciples too, though it is certain that he lives with all of these. Therefore the life of Christ and of his own is hidden from the carnal and worldly, in God, outside the world, in his heavenly kingdom, in which they live.

(Col. 3:4) When Christ, your life, appears, then you also will appear with him in glory. Christ is our life; for we read in the Acts of the Apostles that he is the author of life, he whom the faithless do not believe to be the judge to come (Acts 3:15). But when he comes in heavenly power, and is seen to be what was least believed, then those also who believed in him will appear with him; and it will be made manifest to all faithlessness that he lives with his own in glory. This consolation begets the confidence of hope, so that, secure about the life to come and putting all things after him, we may follow Christ.

(Col. 3:5) Put to death your members which are upon the earth: fornication, uncleanness, lust, evil desire, and avarice, which is the servitude of idols. Since in the epistle to the Romans he signifies that all sins together are a body (Rom. 6:6), therefore now, describing the offences one by one, he calls them members; so that, having received and kept the gospel account of the Savior's fullness, they might give themselves to a good life, abstaining from all baseness and evil desire, so that they may not lose the fruit of divine knowledge. For it does great harm to know the account of the truth and to affirm it while one's deeds in no way follow it. Hence it is that the Lord too says, among other things: But he who knows the will of his lord and does not obey him will be beaten with many stripes (Luke 12:47). Yet he compared avarice to idolatry, to show that nothing is more wicked than it. Under one name he designated two most evil and impious kinds, which he warns must be fled by every means; for idolatry is the first and a very grave crime. For what is so pernicious and so deadly as, to the injury of God the creator, to bestow the name of his honor upon wood and stones or other metals, when men themselves do not suffer a slave to be called by the name of his master, a slave whom not nature, surely, but a certain condition subjects to servitude? On the other side is the crime of avarice, not unlike in wickedness, but different in name. Hence he says to Timothy: For the root of all evils is avarice (1 Tim. 6:10); for it is capable of all offences in order to fill the desire of its greed, which is impossible, always drinking and always thirsting. Not without reason, then, does he show both evils under one name; for they are accomplished almost by one work: because, just as idolatry strives to take glory away from the one God, so that he may not alone have what is peculiarly his, the name of divinity befitting him alone, so also avarice stretches itself out over the things of God, so that, if it can be done, one man alone may usurp for himself God's creation, which God made common to all. Hence God says through the prophet: Mine is the gold, and mine is the silver (Hag. 2:9). Both, therefore, are hostile to God; for both deny to God the things that are his.

(Col. 3:6) On account of which the wrath of God comes. He says that because of the evils mentioned the judgment of God will come, so that vengeance may be taken on those who, despising God and his Law, preferred to follow what is forbidden.

(Col. 3:7) In which you also once walked, when you lived in them. He does not keep silent about how great a benefit the grace of God has bestowed on them through Jesus Christ, so that they may rejoice that they have escaped the wrath which is to come suddenly upon the unbelievers of the whole earth, and may show themselves such that they can obey the precepts of him by whom they have been freed from the second death.

(Col. 3:8–11) But now put away, you also, all things: anger and indignation of mind, malice, blasphemy. Let no foul talk proceed from your mouth. Do not lie to one another. Strip off the old man with his deeds, and put on the new, who is renewed unto knowledge according to the image of him who created him; where there is not Greek and Jew, circumcision and uncircumcision, barbarian and Scythian, slave and free: but Christ is all things and in all. Now he forbids those things which seem lesser sins, lest perhaps, because above he commanded the graver ones to be put to death, these should be thought admissible, or, as though not dangerous, not to be fled; and at the same time so that, having stripped off the old man, that is, seeing themselves undefiled in word as much as in deed, and made new in Christ through the knowledge of the truth, which was once hidden but has now been made manifest to believers through the new law, they may become like the image of him who created them. This image is to be understood in the conduct of a good life, as he says to the Corinthians in the first epistle: As we have borne the image of him who is of the earth, so let us bear also the image of him who is from heaven (1 Cor. 15:49). He, then, is the creator of man whose image he bids us bear in holiness and good works, which descend from the knowledge of the Son of God. For no one can have a good and clean life unless he has someone to whom he may display it, from whom he may expect, if not a reward, at least praise. But with God both praise and reward are hoped for; for the justice of God does not allow that he who has conquered the vices of the flesh should go uncrowned. In this knowledge, therefore, no one is excepted as unworthy, as though he did not deserve to come to the faith of Christ; for all who come become one, blotted out as to what they were, and written down as what they have begun to be; so that, because they are of one faith, in that very thing they may also be of one merit, so that those whom faith joins cannot be distinguished on the ground that they are different in nature and race or condition. For Christ is all things and in all these; for because all are his members, Christ is all things; so that, while their confession about hope in him is one, Christ is in all: and thus all things are his body, so that their profession and their nature agree. Yet this image, which he says is created from the knowledge of the Savior, is one thing, and the image to which the first man was made is another. For this image is in the woman too, when she acknowledges him who created her and, obeying his will, abstains from a base life and perverse conduct; but that image is in the man alone, as he likewise says in the first epistle to the Corinthians: A man indeed ought not to veil his head, since he is the image and glory of God (1 Cor. 11:7). But if the woman too did not veil her head, she too would be the image of God. But it was unfitting that she, having been made subject to the man, should be said to be the image of God; for in Genesis too it says thus: And God made man; to the image of God he made him (Gen. 1:27); so that, as God is one, one man should be made by him: so that, as all things are from the one God, so from one man the whole race should be upon the face of the whole earth. The One, therefore, made one man, who should have the image of his unity. And in man and woman there is that very likeness of the mystery of the Father and the Son. Yet he separated the barbarian from the Scythian, as the slave from the free, not indeed in Christ, in whom all are one, but in the ferocity of their manners and in the savagery of the race. For he wished these to be understood as worse than barbarians before they believe, although the books of the Greeks proclaim their justice, which they followed with nature as their guide; so much so that among them theft is held a great sin; the paths of justice which the Greeks, he says, tracked down with great industry and learning, they praise these men for having grasped by nature. But as all things degenerate, they afterwards began to be more blameworthy than they had been praiseworthy before. For after their women, who they assert were originally called Amazons, began to use the law of men, subjecting all Asia against nature, so it came about that the men too were made rabid after the manner of dogs, savage, wild, uncultivated, more than barbarians, so changed that they were trained to feed on human flesh. What wonder, when the Romans themselves too were far other than they are now? For to their women also, for chastity's sake, the use of wine was unknown, as the use of water is now; for so strong was the authority of discipline that the subject vessel did not do what it wished.

(Col. 3:12–13) Put on, therefore, as the elect of God, holy and beloved, bowels of mercy, benignity, humility, meekness of mind, long-suffering, showing patience to one another, and forgiving one another, if anyone has a complaint against another; as Christ also forgave you, so do you also. So that they may have gain, he exhorts them to do these things which lay up merit with God. Now fear brings about abstaining from sins; for a man fears lest, doing evil, he be subjected to punishments. But these things which he now urges them to do belong to the advancement of merits; for they are of the will, not of necessity. He, then, who abstains from unlawful things has praise; but he who refrains even from lawful things has both praise and reward.

(Col. 3:14–15) But above all these things have love, which is the bond of unity: and let the peace of God exult in your hearts, in which you also were called in one body: and be thankful. Because love is the greatest of all, he exhorts them to be the more zealous for it. For then all the things which he commanded above to be kept can be brought to the effect of rewards, if, with love as their guide, they walk the road of the law; for he works with his whole mind who does anything out of love. Hypocrisy and pretence are not in love; wherefore John the apostle too: Fear, he says, is not in love. For he who fears is not perfect: Perfect indeed love casts out fear (1 John 4:18); hence the Lord too prefers to be loved rather than feared. For this is the love that embraces unity, this is what makes all one body, this is what has true peace, which it guards with a pure heart. For a thing can be called peace and yet not have love; but love always has peace with it.

(Col. 3:16) Let the word of Christ dwell in you abundantly in all wisdom, teaching and admonishing yourselves in psalms and hymns and spiritual songs, singing in grace in your hearts to the Lord. He warns them always to meditate on the things that sound forth Christ, to keep in the heart the things that ever render thanks to God the Father; so that the melodious voice may serve him, the psalm praise him, the hymn extol him. And let it be known to him alone who sees through the hidden things of the mind, from whom the reward too is hoped for. He commanded that he be sung to in the hearts, lest it be done for the favor of men and lose its fruit.

(Col. 3:17) And everything whatever you do in word or in deed, do all in the name of the Lord Jesus, giving thanks to God and the Father through him. Since above he commanded that God be praised in the heart and that thanks be rendered to him, now he exhorts that the name of the Lord Jesus be praised and exalted both in word and in the deed of our whole way of life; so that, when our words are free of reproach and our works are good, the Lord Jesus may be magnified, by whose teaching his disciples are beyond blame. For then we give true thanks to God through him, if we keep the measure of his precepts.

(Col. 3:18) Wives, be subject to your husbands, as is fitting, in the Lord. He commanded that wives ought to be subject to their husbands just as God ordained, saying to the woman among other things: And your turning shall be to your husband, and he shall rule over you (Gen. 3:16); so that by this very custom and discipline she may fear her husband as a lord, from whom she began to be.

(Col. 3:19) Husbands, love your wives, and do not be bitter toward them. He softens the rigor of the man's law, lest anyone, by the power in which he prevails, be excessive in his lordship over his wife; but he ought to have regard to affection, because the woman is his flesh; lest, humbling her too much, he embitter her and she think contrary thoughts.

(Col. 3:20) Children, obey your parents in all things; for this is pleasing to God. Nothing else can please God than what is just and godly. For then children can have God propitious, if they repay due honor to their parents, easy in obeying them, hard to bring to contempt, knowing that children ought never to plot against their parents.

(Col. 3:21) Fathers, do not provoke your children to anger, lest they become faint-hearted. He commands that fathers ought to be moderate toward their children, lest the children, hard pressed by them, sin against them and offend God, which they do not wish. For anger is a thoughtless thing, so much so that sometimes he who is angry does not spare even himself. For fathers ought to remember that they too were once children, and children to consider that they are going to be fathers; so that, the counsel of each side being equally weighed, the children may be subject and the fathers moderate.

(Col. 3:22) Slaves, obey in all things your masters according to the flesh, not serving to the eye, as pleasing men, but in simplicity of heart, fearing the Lord. Since God cannot be served otherwise than with a pure heart, for he sees through the hidden things of souls, so too he commands that men should be served. For he who serves to the eye does something else in secret; but he who serves from the heart is the same outside as he is within. If, then, he is faithful in this obedience, he will earn the favor of the Lord; for he himself said: He who is faithful in the least is faithful also in much (Luke 16:10). For if he has not been faithful to him who punishes in the present, how can it be that he will show faith to him who he hears will judge after death? Therefore in the business of his master according to the flesh he wants the slave to be just and obedient, so that he may be both faithful and obedient in the things of God too. For if he has been fit in the present, where he knows there are none but corruptible rewards, how much more ready will he be in devotion to God, who he knows will give immortal rewards to the faithful!

(Col. 3:23–25) Whatever you do, work at it from the heart, as for the Lord and not for men, certain that from the Lord you will receive the recompense of the inheritance of the Lord Christ, whom you serve. For he who acts unjustly bears that which he did unjustly: and there is no respect of persons with God. To make them prompt in obedience and faithfulness, he promises that they will receive from God the wage of good works in the kingdom of Christ. And that this may be approved and accepted, he wants them to obey as they would the Lord, from whom they expect the wage. But if a slave acts unjustly, he will offend not only a man but God too, whom he despised in the fraud committed against his master according to the flesh. But if he acts well, he will not be, with God, the slave of a man, as though more despised; but he will be rewarded as a free man among men, yet a slave of Christ, which is a name of dignity.

Chapter IV

(Col. 4:1) Masters, provide your slaves with what is just and fair, knowing that you also have a Lord in heaven. Lest temporal masters be lifted up by pride, presuming on their lordship, he softens and restrains their minds, so that, taking the human race into consideration, they may observe that God the author created not slaves and free men, but all freeborn. But this came about through the iniquity of the world: while one man invades another's territory, he then leads freeborn men away captive; hence those taken by the hand (manu capti) were, from this, called by the ancients mancipia, slaves. This chance and condition appears even now: some are redeemed, others remain slaves. With God, however, he is held a slave who has sinned (John 8:34). Accordingly, it was because of sin that Ham heard himself called a slave: Cursed be the boy Canaan; a slave of slaves shall he be to his brothers (Gen. 9:25). The ancients assented to this judgment, so that they defined all the wise to be free, and all fools to be slaves; because the wise man abstains from sins, so that he is freeborn who follows what is right, but he is a slave who through the imprudence of folly subjects himself to sin. Hence Ham too, who foolishly laughed at his father's nakedness, was called a slave because of his folly. He shows the masters, therefore, that they are not truly masters, but as it were in an image; for they are masters of bodies, not of souls. For the only master and author of things, the invisible God, rules both bodies and souls; so that, considering these things, they may exact just services from their slaves: such, surely, as they themselves wish to be exacted from them by the common Lord. For while they themselves do not serve God as is fitting, though they do not deny that he has power over all and that his daily gifts are furnished for human uses through the ministries of creation, from their equals (not to say their brothers) they exact services so heavy that they cannot be borne: not taking to heart that they themselves too, willing or not, are slaves; and let them look to it of what merit.

(Col. 4:2–4) Persevere in prayer, watching in it with thanksgiving: praying at the same time for us also, that God may open to us a door of the word to speak the mystery of Christ (on account of which I am also in chains), that I may make it manifest as I ought to speak. He shows that those who are now wise in the mystery of the Lord Christ, and wary in avoiding the vices of sins, must persevere in prayer, so that they may grow in merits while the temptations of the adversary fail. He holds them so worthy that he judges they can even help him by their prayers to assert boldly the Gospel, for the making manifest of which he had received his embassy.

(Col. 4:5–6) Walk in wisdom toward those who are outside, redeeming the time. Let your speech always be seasoned with salt in grace, that you may know how you ought to answer each one. Since we must walk among unbelievers and join in conversation for the sake of our way of life in the world, this is what he warns: that our very conduct be with wisdom because of the scandal of the Gentiles; lest they receive through us an occasion of blaspheming, and be stirred up to persecute. For what need is there to speak with one whom you know to be intractable and of obstinate mind, ready to do insult? Therefore he gives this warning, so that the words of religion may be brought forth in a suitable place and at a suitable time, with modesty as teacher; or, if such a person is present as makes an uproar, one must keep silent. For one must deal in one way with the powerful of the world, in another with those of middle rank, and in another with the lowly. Again, in one way with the meek, in another with the irascible, to whom one must give way: this is to redeem the time. For when you give way to one who lies in wait for the Lord's words and to the raging boldness of the time, you save yourself the injuries of a hostile time. For if the head, that is, the king, assents to the body, then there is freedom to speak the words of God with meekness, so that men may be provoked to good. Yet to those who make an uproar and are spirited for abuse one must always give way; for you conquer while you give way.

(Col. 4:7–9) All the things concerning me Tychicus, the beloved brother and faithful minister and fellow slave in the Lord, will make known to you: whom I have sent to you for this very purpose, that he may know the things concerning you and comfort your hearts, with Onesimus, the dearest and faithful brother, who is one of you; he will make known to you all the things that are here. He says that he has sent the brothers for this, that the things done by each might become known to the other. Although above he says that he is present among them in spirit and sees what they do, yet there is need of messengers. For a man becomes more careful when he sees someone coming to him to observe his conduct; for he fears more if he perceives that what he does is seen. For although God sees all things, and we know that all things are known to him, yet because we do not see ourselves being seen by him, we do contrary things.

(Col. 4:10–11) Aristarchus, the companion of my captivity, greets you, and Mark the cousin of Barnabas (concerning whom you received a command, that if he comes to you, you receive him), and Jesus who is called Justus: who are of the circumcision. These alone are my helpers in the kingdom of God, who have been a comfort to me. This Aristarchus appears to be a man of good conduct and merit, who is designated a sharer and companion of the Apostle's labors in devotion to the Lord. And to commend Mark, he does not keep silent about who he is, so that, when he is learned to be a kinsman of Barnabas, a praiseworthy man, he may be held in honor. He adds Jesus too, who he says is called by the name Justus. To these alone he bears witness that, being of the circumcision, they were a help to him, assenting to and sharing his judgment, so as to preach that men ought no longer to be circumcised; therefore he calls them his sharers in the kingdom of God. But to preach that one must be circumcised, and that sabbaths and new moons must be kept, is not spiritual but earthly.

(Col. 4:12) Epaphras, who is one of you, a slave of Christ, greets you. He makes the epistle dearer to them when he bears witness to one who is from among themselves. Always anxious for you in his prayers, that you may stand perfect and abundantly fulfilled in all the will of God. He testifies that he desires such salutary things for them, so as to show that the man who is their fellow citizen, and most dear in the affection of oneness of mind, is a sharer in his own devotion for their advancement.

(Col. 4:13) For I bear him witness that he has much labor for you, and for those who are at Laodicea and at Hierapolis. To make them anxious for their own salvation, he shows how great the vigilance of others is on their behalf; for a man becomes more anxious out of shame when he sees another laboring in his cause.

(Col. 4:14) Luke, the dearest physician, greets you, and Demas. Luke was truly dearest to the Apostle, because, putting all things after, he always followed the Apostle: he who is held to have written both the Gospel and the Acts of the Apostles.

(Col. 4:15) Greet the brothers who are at Laodicea, and Nympha, and the church in her house. Since in like manner he was anxious also for those who are at Laodicea, in their epistle he urges that these be greeted, esteeming Nympha the dearer, whose house too he greets; for she seems to have been so devout that her whole house was marked with the sign of the cross.

(Col. 4:16) And when this epistle has been read among you, see that it is read also in the church of the Laodiceans, and that you read the one which is the Laodiceans'. Because the instructions of the Apostle are general, and his epistles were written for the advancement of all the Churches, therefore he commands that this epistle be read to the Laodiceans too, so that through it they might learn what they had to do; and that the Colossians should read theirs, in accordance with the sense stated above.

(Col. 4:17) And say to Archippus: See to the ministry which you have received in the Lord, that you fulfill it. Through the people themselves he admonishes their overseer to be anxious for their salvation; and because the epistle is written for the sake of the people alone, it is therefore addressed not to their ruler, but to the Church. For after Epaphras, who instructed them, this man received their Church to rule.

(Col. 4:18) The greeting itself is mine, by the hand of Paul. Be mindful of my chains. The grace of our Lord Jesus Christ be with you. To confirm the epistle he says it is subscribed with his own hand, reminding them to keep in mind his ordeals, which he was suffering for the salvation of the Gentiles, and to show themselves such that he would not take it ill to suffer injuries for them.
