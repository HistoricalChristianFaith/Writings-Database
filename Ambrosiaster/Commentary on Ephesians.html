Commentary on Ephesians

Latin source: the commentary on the Epistle to the Ephesians by the anonymous Roman writer called Ambrosiaster (written at Rome about 366–384), from the Maurist text of 1690, printed under the name of Ambrose in J.-P. Migne, Patrologia Latina 17 (Paris, 1845), cols. 371–404, verified against the page images and collated in full with the Maurist edition (Paris, 1690). Migne's misprints where the Maurist edition he reprints has the right reading are corrected (4:15, 5:10, 5:23 and two places of punctuation), as are his misprinted Scripture references and verse labels; at 5:13 the text stands as Migne and the Maurists print it, without the non that Ballerini's edition (Milan, 1877) adds from two manuscripts. The lemmata are translated from Ambrosiaster's own Old Latin text of Paul, and all other Scripture as he quotes it, from his own Latin; italics mark the lemmata and the other Scripture he quotes. The edition's verse labels are given as (Eph. chapter:verse); in 2:5–10 they run one verse ahead of the usual numbering and are kept as printed. Psalms keep the Latin (LXX) numbering. This English translation is released into the public domain.

Prologue

The Apostle did not found the Ephesians in the faith, but confirmed them, having suffered many evils for them at the hands of the faithless, as he says: For if, according to man, I fought with beasts at Ephesus, what does it profit me, if the dead do not rise (1 Cor. 15:32)? And since he foresees that his struggle is not without effect for their perfecting, rejoicing in them, he exhorts them in writing to better things from his custody in the city of Rome; for, coming from Jerusalem in custody, he is understood to have lived under a surety, remaining outside the camp in his own hired lodging (Acts 28:16).

Chapter I

(Eph. 1:1) Paul, an apostle of Christ Jesus by the will of God, to all the saints who are at Ephesus, and to the faithful in Christ Jesus. He writes in his usual manner, for he testifies that he is an apostle of Christ Jesus by the will of God. He writes not only to the faithful but also to the saints, so that they may be truly faithful only if they are saints in Christ Jesus. For a good life profits, and is believed holy, only if it is lived under the name of Christ; otherwise it will be a defilement, because it tends to the injury of the Creator.

(Eph. 1:2) Grace to you and peace from God our Father, and the Lord Jesus Christ. He says that the grace of God is with the saints and the faithful, God whom he calls our Father, because from him all things are both founded and restored; and Christ our Lord, because he redeemed us, offering himself for us.

(Eph. 1:3) Blessed be the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, who has blessed us with every spiritual blessing, that is, not with a worldly but with a heavenly blessing, not a corruptible but an eternal one; because the glory of Christians is not on earth but in heaven, and it is in Christ. For every gift of the grace of God is in Christ, so that if anyone, having spurned Christ, thinks that he is blessed by God, let him know that he is in error. Yet God is blessed in one way, and men in another. The name of blessing is indeed one, but it must be understood according to the dignity of the person; for God too is said to make, and men make: but God makes impassibly, by his nod alone, while men accomplish things by hand, by effort, by labor, by sweat. God, then, is blessed when he is extolled with worthy praises; but God so blesses men that he imparts to them the gift of his grace, not according to their merits but according to his mercy. And therefore he said: Who has blessed us in spiritual blessing, to show that a perfect blessing of heavenly things is given to those who believe in Christ, so that they may be full of heavenly glory, borne up above the powers of the heavens. For where I shall be, he says, there shall my minister be also (John 12:26).

(Eph. 1:4) Even as he chose us in him before the foundation of the world, that we should be holy and spotless before him in love. God, foreknowing all things, knows who would believe in Christ, as he says to the Romans: Whom he foreknew, he also called us (Rom. 8:29): not only from the Jews but also from the Gentiles. Those, then, whom God is said to call persevere in faith: these are the ones whom he chose in Christ before the world, that they may be undefiled before God in love, that is, that the love of God may make their way of life holy. For no one obeys another better than he who complies out of love.

(Eph. 1:5) Predestining us to the adoption of sons through Jesus Christ unto himself. He says that God decreed this: that those who believe in Christ should be adopted as sons by God, so that their head might be Christ, the true Son of God.

(Eph. 1:6) According to the good pleasure of his will, to the praise of his glory. He says that God had such good pleasure that the grace given to men should tend to the praise of his glory, so that our salvation might be the glory of God. By which he has graced us in his beloved Son. God has given us grace because we have believed in his Son Christ; for, loving his only-begotten, he bestows divine gifts on those who love him.

(Eph. 1:7) In whom we have redemption through his blood, the remission of sins. He calls for a twofold grace, because he both redeemed us with his blood and did not impute our sins to us, that is, he redeemed and set free.

(Eph. 1:8) According to the riches of his grace, which has superabounded in us in all wisdom and prudence. He adds to grace; because after the benefits mentioned above comes the gift of wisdom and of prudence in spiritual things.

(Eph. 1:9–10) That he might make known to us the mystery of his will, according to his good pleasure, which he purposed in him, in the dispensation of the fullness of times, restoring all things in Christ, which are in the heavens and which are on earth. This pleased God, whose counsel cannot be revoked: to show in Christ the mystery of his will at the time when he willed it to be revealed for the knowledge of creation. And his will was this: that he should then become propitious to all who were in error, whether in the heavens or on earth, when he made Christ manifest as this gift of believers, that sins should not be imputed to those who receive the faith of Christ. Every creature, then, in the heavens and on earth, while it learns the knowledge of Christ, is restored to that for which it was founded. For when it is instructed in the faith of the one God, having known the mystery of the Savior, it is restored from the error into which it had begun to fall, holding for certain that its Creator is the one God and Lord. Hence it is that John too says in the Apocalypse: I saw Michael and his Angels fighting in heaven with the dragon and with his army (Rev. 12:7). This conflict comes partly from error, partly from the presumption of a usurped tyranny.

(Eph. 1:11–12) In whom we too have obtained our lot, predestined according to the purpose of God, who created all things according to the counsel of his will, that we should be to the praise of his glory, we who before hoped in Christ. This is what he says: that the dispensation of preaching was decreed by God for those who from among the Jews believed in Christ. Hence none from the Gentiles was chosen for the apostolate; for it was fitting that preachers should be chosen from those who before hoped for the salvation that had been promised to them in Christ.

(Eph. 1:13–14) In whom you too, when you had heard the word of truth, the Gospel of your salvation, believing, were sealed with the holy Spirit of promise, who is the pledge of our inheritance, unto the redemption of acquisition, to the praise of his glory. It is the praise of God's glory when many are gained for the faith, just as it is the glory of a physician if he cures many. And therefore it belongs to the glory of God that he called the Gentiles, that they might obtain the remedy of their salvation through the faith promised to the Jews, having the sign of redemption and of the inheritance to come, the Holy Spirit given after baptism. For the redeemed have been designated heirs if they persevere in regeneration, so that the first faith may merit pardon, but a holy way of life persevering with faith may merit the crown.

(Eph. 1:15–16) Therefore I too, having heard of your faith in the Lord Jesus, and of your love toward all the saints, do not cease giving thanks for you, making remembrance of you in my prayers. Since, according to the decree of God by which he determined that the Gentiles should become partakers of the promise of the Jews, the Ephesians, having heard the word of God, received the faith of Christ, loving all the saints as the Lord commanded, for this reason he gives thanks to God for their conversion and love, to God who deigned to make the Gentiles one body with the Jews.

(Eph. 1:17–19) That the God of our Lord Jesus Christ, the Father of glory, may give you the spirit of wisdom and of revelation in the knowledge of him, to have the eyes of your heart enlightened, to this end, that you may know what is the hope of his calling, and what the riches of the inheritance of his glory in the saints. It is plain that he says he prays for them for this reason: that they may know the hope of their faith in the heavenly reward; because when they fully know what the fruit of believers is, they will become more eager to cultivate religion. For it seems that there will already be progress of this kind, because if they are so devout before they know the glory of the promise, they will necessarily become more eager when they have come to know it. Yet he calls God the Father of glory, and the God of Christ, although God is the true Father of Christ, and the God of creation. But the Lord himself too says the same: I go to my Father and your Father, to my God and your God (John 20:17). He is therefore the Father of all glory, because from him is all glory, and power, and dignity. He is also the Father of Christ, but in another way, because this one is the true Son, and he is the God of all, but he is not the God of Christ in the same way. For he is the God of Christ in this way: because Christ is God from God; but to the rest he is God as a cause of fear, as he himself says in Malachi the prophet: If I am God, where is my fear (Mal. 1:6)?

(Eph. 1:20–21) And what is the exceeding greatness of his power toward you who believe, according to the working of the might of his strength, which he wrought in Christ, raising him from the dead, and he set him at his right hand in the heavenly places, above every Principality and Power, and Virtue, and Dominion, and every name that is named, not only in this age but also in the age to come. He declares that the pattern of the salvation and glory of believers consists in the resurrection of the Savior, so that from it the faithful may know what has been promised to them. For in like manner they will be raised above the heavens, to be with Christ; as he himself says: Father, I will that where I shall be, these also may be with me, and may see my glory (John 17:24). This is on account of the incarnation of the Son of God, so that even having been made man he may be said to be above all the heavens, and above the whole creation, sitting at the right hand of God, having the name of God by nature, not by adoption; so that those who crucified him may know that he is the Son of God. For the honor which the Father gave him by generation was made manifest then, when, born as man, he suffered, and having conquered death rose again, ascending above all the heavens, whence he had descended. Hence it was said that truth has sprung from the earth (Ps. 84:12). For because the Father has given all judgment to the Son (John 5:22), therefore he is said to sit at the right hand of God, as judge. And indeed he himself says to the disciples: Until now you have asked nothing in my name: ask, and I will do it (ibid. 16:24). Hence he says to the Jews: Henceforth you shall see the Son of man sitting at the right hand of the power of God, and coming with the clouds of heaven (Matt. 26:64). For from the time that he became known, he began to be approached and petitioned, as judge.

(Eph. 1:22–23) And he has subjected all things under his feet, and has given him as head over the whole Church, which is his body and fullness, who is fulfilled all in all. He says that the Father has subjected the whole creation to the Son, so that, because he made all things through him (John 1:3), he himself may be the head and Lord of all. He subjected all things to him then, when he begot him before all things, so that through him he might make the things that were not. In saying the whole Church, he comprehends in sum all that is in heaven and on earth, so that they may be made his members into a new man, while they confess that he is the one through whom they were made by God. For they are subjected to him as members to the head from which they draw their origin, so that all things may be fulfilled in all. For when they have returned to the confession of the one God, bending the knee to Christ, it is fulfilled in all that he may be all; because they are from him: and thus he himself is in them, because they subsist by his power; for those who withdraw from God seem to make him void. Hence he says in another place: That all things may grow unto the increase of God (Col. 2:19).

Chapter II

(Eph. 2:1–2) And you, when you were dead in your offenses and sins, in which you once walked according to the age of this world. He calls them dead because they follow the errors of the world; for whatever withdraws from the profession of the one God is held to be dead, because it does not remain in the root from which it draws its origin. According to the prince of the power of this air, the spirit that now works in the sons of distrust. By the prince of the power, that is, the devil, he signifies that he corrupted the understanding of the world, so that they withdrew from the one God, taking up the opinion of many gods, in order that he might make them companions of his conspiracy, while they are found in the same ungodliness, denying the one God. He calls this prince of error, then, the spirit of the air for this reason: to show that he usurped this region for himself for the exercise of dominion. For the air is one thing and the prince another: but because he holds dominion in the world or in the air, he is called the spirit of the air.

(Eph. 2:3) In which we too once had our way of life in the desires of our flesh, doing the will of the flesh and of its counsels: and we were by nature sons of wrath, even as the rest. He signifies faithlessness when he mentions the desires of the flesh. For the will of the flesh is to take delight in visible things, so that it calls gods the elements which God established as the helms of the world; whereas this name is owed to the one God, from whom are all things, so that just as he has no partner among these in power, so he may have no sharer in the name. But this name is not denied to Christ for this reason: because just as their nature is common, so too will the name be. Yet there is this difference between the Father and the Son, that the Father received this from no one, while the Son received all that is the Father's through generation, so that in power and substance and name the Son differs in nothing from the Father. Hence he says: All things that the Father has are mine; and mine are the Father's (John 16:15). It is therefore neither unworthy nor contrary if Christ too is called God, but God from God the Father, so that the authority of the one God may be preserved. For if anyone thinks that he means something else when he reproaches the will of the flesh, let him know that the Apostle too had led a clean life, because according to the righteousness of the Law he had lived without complaint. But because he persecuted the faith of Christ, therefore we had our way of life, he says, in the desires of our flesh. For every sin is called flesh, above all faithlessness, which is the mother of all wickedness. Good works and chastity will profit no one if he is faithless; for faithlessness stains all these things. Hence it is that he added: And we were by nature sons of wrath, even as the rest. For when an evil will is put under nature, it becomes a nature of wrath, that is, one that must be subjected to punishment, changed not in substance but by an evil will. For what it follows is reckoned to the nature; hence he says in Isaiah: A most wicked seed (Isa. 1:4). And why it is a most wicked seed he added: For you have forsaken the Lord, he says, and have provoked the Holy One of Israel to anger; so that the nature which deserts God is called a most wicked seed. For to degenerate is to withdraw from the Author: and when it offers a bad example to the rest, it is a bad seed; for it produces the worst fruits. But if it returns to the Author, the nature will be good, according to the Apostle, who, changed when he acknowledged his Creator, is called the vessel of election (Acts 9:15).

(Eph. 2:4) But God, who is rich in mercy, because of his great love with which he has had mercy on us. These are the true riches of mercies: that mercy should be shown of his own accord to those who did not seek it, as Isaiah says: I was made manifest to those who did not seek me (Isa. 65:1); for this is to abound in mercy, that it be bestowed even on those who do not ask. Yet this is the love of God toward us: that because he made us, he does not will us to perish; for he loves his own work. For he made it for this reason, that he might love what he made; for no one holds his own labor in hatred.

(Eph. 2:5) And when we were dead in sins, he made us alive together in Christ. Estranged from God we were dead: but by his grace he called us back, that is, he gave us life in Christ. For because he made us through him, through him he has again reformed us in him, that is, so that we may be his members; and he is our head; for we have him, by God's gift, as the author of our life, as Peter the apostle says: But you killed the Author of life (Acts 3:15).

(Eph. 2:6) By whose grace you have been saved. To this gift he adds the Gentiles too, signifying that we have been saved by the grace of Christ.

(Eph. 2:7) And he raised us up together, and made us sit together in the heavenly places in Christ Jesus. He says what I mentioned above: that the saints, even from the Gentiles, will be there where Christ, their head, also is.

(Eph. 2:8) That he might show in the ages to come the superabounding riches of his grace in goodness toward us in Christ Jesus. This signifies that in the age to come the immortal gift of God will appear in the reward of the faithful: what eye has not seen, nor ear heard, nor has it entered into the heart of man, the things which God has prepared for those who love him (1 Cor. 2:9). These are the superabounding riches: that so much is given to believers as the mind cannot search out.

(Eph. 2:9–10) For by grace you have been saved through faith. The grace of faith has been given, that believers may be saved. And this not of yourselves; for it is the gift of God, not of works, lest anyone should boast. For we are his handiwork, created in Christ Jesus for good works, which God prepared that we should walk in them. It is true that every thanksgiving for our salvation must be referred to God, who shows us his mercy, that he might call back to life those who were wandering and not seeking the true way. And therefore we must not glory in ourselves, but in God, who has regenerated us by a heavenly birth through the faith of Christ, to this end, that, exercised in the good works which God has decreed for us now reborn, we may merit to receive the promises.

(Eph. 2:11–12) Keeping in memory, you who were once Gentiles in the flesh, who are called uncircumcision by that which is called circumcision, made by hand in the flesh, that you were at that time without Christ, alienated from the way of life of Israel, and strangers to the testaments, and having no hope of their promise, and without God in this world. He admonishes the Gentiles that, remembering what they had been (for they were carnal, and without hope, having no share in the promise of the Jews in Christ; for Christ had been promised to the Jews, not to the Gentiles), they may give thanks more readily to God through Christ, who in his mercy made them partakers of spiritual things when they were carnal. Yet he says circumcision made by hand in the flesh for this reason: to signify another, spiritual one, not made by hand.

(Eph. 2:13) But now in Christ Jesus you who once were far off have been made near by the blood of Christ. He still commends the gift and grace of God, to whom he wishes us, as those always under obligation, to return thanks; for this reason he recalls that we have been made near to God in the blood of Christ: to show how great an affection God has toward us, who allowed his Son to die for us; so that we too, enduring in faith in him, may not give way to any destructions brought on us for his sake, knowing that he deserves more from us than whatever can be inflicted by enemies: for he, though he is the Lord and needs no one, offered himself for us, so that it might profit not him but us. For even if it did not profit us, we would perhaps be compelled to die for him, as servants for our very lord: how much more when not only his death but ours too brings about its effect for us!

(Eph. 2:14) For he is our peace, who has made both one, and loosing the middle wall of the partition, the enmities in his flesh. The passion of the Savior made peace between circumcision and uncircumcision. For the enmity which was like a wall in the middle, and divided circumcision from uncircumcision and uncircumcision from circumcision, this the Savior loosed, giving a law that neither should the Jew, presuming on circumcision, reject the Gentile, nor the Gentile, trusting in uncircumcision, that is, in paganism, abhor the Jew: but that both, made new, should follow faith in the one God in Christ. For all that the Savior taught he confirmed then, when he rose from the dead. Yet the wall that was in the middle, dividing between the law of deeds and the Gentile world, he signified as a partition or hedge, that is, not a stable wall, nor a strong one; because neither was the Gentile world a firm thing, being as it were invented by man, nor were circumcision, and new moons, and the observance of foods and of sacrifices or of sabbaths stable and fixed; for they were given in such a way that they should cease at the coming of Christ. For although God gave these things, yet he gave them for a time: but the preaching of faith is firm and without successor.

(Eph. 2:15) Making void the law of commandments in decrees, that he might found the two in himself, making peace in one new man. This is what I mentioned above in sum: that the law which had been given to the Jews in circumcision, and in new moons, and in foods, and in sacrifices, and in the sabbath, he made void, that is, he commanded it now to cease, because they were a burden; and thus he made peace. Hence Peter the apostle says in the Acts of the Apostles: Why do you put a yoke upon the neck of the brethren, which neither our fathers nor we have been able to bear (Acts 15:10)?

(Eph. 2:16) That he might present and reconcile both in one body to God through the cross. The enmity removed, Christ by his death first reconciled the peoples among themselves, then made them at peace with God the Father, so that men of one nature, because they had been made diverse through error, might be made members of one new body, whose head is Christ.

(Eph. 2:17–18) Slaying the enmities in himself, and coming he preached peace to you who were far off, and peace to those who were near; because through him we both have access in one Spirit to the Father. The sense is the same; for he slew the enmity in himself when he died for all, Jews as well as Gentiles. For the death of the Savior profits all; for rising from the dead, death, which held all, having been condemned, he showed what hope those who believe in him would have. Hence he commanded the preaching by which all should be called together to God the Father in the unity of the Spirit, the discord having been torn away, so that whether Jews (who are called near for this reason, because the promise was made to their fathers) or Gentiles (who are called far off for this reason, because they did not receive the law of God given through Moses), the former rite having been removed, they might receive the faith of Christ, through which they should be made one.

(Eph. 2:19) Therefore you are now no longer strangers and sojourners, but you are fellow citizens of the saints, and members of the household of God. He admonishes them to be mindful of the benefits of God; because when they were far from the knowledge and fear of God, they were joined by his mercy, so as to become partakers with those who were near to God, made fellow citizens of the sons of God. For just as whatever nation desired to pursue the peace of the Romans was received upon offering gifts, so that they became Roman citizens, as the Tarsians of Cilicia, whence the Apostle says that he is a Roman citizen (Acts 22:26): so too whoever has joined himself to the Christian faith becomes a citizen of the saints and a member of the household of God.

(Eph. 2:20) Built upon the foundation of the apostles and prophets, Christ Jesus himself being the chief cornerstone. That is, set upon the New and Old Testament. For what the apostles preached, the prophets said would come; although he says to the Corinthians: God, he says, has set in the Church first apostles, secondly prophets (1 Cor. 12:28). But these are other prophets; for in that place he is discussing the ordering of the Church, but here the foundation of the Church: for the prophets arranged, but the apostles laid the foundations. Hence the Lord says to Peter: Upon this rock I will build my Church (Matt. 16:18), that is, on this confession of the catholic faith I will establish the faithful unto life; for David too arranged the house of God, and marked the place where it should be built: but Solomon founded it.

(Eph. 2:21–22) In whom the whole structure, fitted together, grows into a holy temple in the Lord, in whom you too are built together into a dwelling place of God in the spirit. This is the sense from which the Lord says: upon this rock I will build my Church. For the Savior took up into himself two peoples, and made them one in the Lord, just as a cornerstone too holds together two walls, made firm in the unity of the house; for the faithful are the surface of the temple of God, living in the spirit, so that they may become heirs of the heavenly dwelling.

Chapter III

(Eph. 3:1) For this reason I Paul, the prisoner of Christ Jesus for you brothers. Since he has shown the disposition of the divine mercy toward the salvation of the Gentiles, which existed before the world, he now declares that by God's provision, Christ sending him, he discharges the embassy of this gift. For the Lord after his resurrection sent his apostles in his stead to preach (Matt. 28:19), directing this one to the conversion of the Gentiles; for thus he said to him: Go, I will send you far away to the Gentiles (Acts 22:21). He has therefore used the Lord's word when he says: Peace to you who are far off, and to those who are near.

(Eph. 3:2–4) If indeed you have heard of the dispensation of the grace of God which was given to us toward you. He wants them to know that by the judgment of God he was sent by Christ to preach to the Gentiles the mystery of the grace of God. By revelation the mystery was made known to me, as I wrote before in brief, as you are able, reading, to understand my prudence in the mystery of Christ. He signifies that the revelation of the mystery of God was shown to him, about which he recalls that he wrote briefly before, that is, in measured fashion, as they were able to grasp the prudence of the Apostle in the mystery of Christ.

(Eph. 3:5–6) Which in other ages was not known to the sons of men, as it has now been revealed to his saints and prophets in the spirit, that the Gentiles are fellow heirs and fellow members of the body and partakers of his promise in Christ Jesus. He asserts that what lay hidden was shown by God in the time of Christ: that the Gentiles were to be partakers of the grace promised in the Law. This, he says, was shown to the holy preachers and prophets, that is, to the apostles and the expounders of the Law, not to the ancient prophets. For although Gentiles were admitted to the Law before, yet they were ordered to be circumcised; and the prophets knew that the Gentiles would be partakers of the gift of God (for they signified this too in many places); but this lay hidden: that without circumcision, and sabbaths, and new moons, and the other such things, they would be partakers of the grace of God, and would receive the faith of Christ apart from the Law, and be saved. This is what he says: That in the spirit the Gentiles are fellow heirs and fellow members of the body, and partakers of his promise in Christ. For he who worships God in the spirit has no need of the work of the Law.

(Eph. 3:7) Through his Gospel I was made a minister according to the gift of the grace of God which was given to me according to the working of his power. He asserts that through the preaching of his ministry the gift became known which God disposed for the Gentiles according to the working of his power. For the working of his power is this, that he raised Christ from the dead, death having been overcome, in which he saved all. Yet the dispensation of his Gospel is this, by which the will of God has been known: that he decreed to save the Gentiles without the work of the Law.

(Eph. 3:8) To me, the least of all the saints, this grace was given. He humbles himself when he calls himself the least, knowing that humility brings advancement. For he says that so great a grace was given to him that he asserts that this gift, which was unknown before in past ages, was granted to him, so that he might make it manifest to the Gentiles. For how can he seem the least of all the saints who, before he was baptized, was called the vessel of election? That I should preach the gospel among the nations, the inestimable riches of Christ (Acts 9:15). He proves that he was chosen to preach the gift of God in the incomprehensible riches of Christ, which he deigned to bestow on the Gentiles when they were already despaired of, so that they might be saved without works through faith. And it is plain that no one sends an excellent and very great gift by an unworthy person. Showing, therefore, how much he merits from God, he nevertheless humbles himself, so that his merit may grow.

(Eph. 3:9) And that I should enlighten all, what is the dispensation of the mystery hidden from the ages in God, who created all things. He says that he was sent to teach the Gentiles in a twofold way the mystery which was in God, from whom are all things: that through him they might learn and understand that Christ was always in God and with God, and that God disposed to save the Gentiles without circumcision and the other commandments of the Law. For the Jews were found unworthy to have so great and such a teacher; for the Jews believed in Christ in such a way that they still kept the Law, which is hateful to God. And indeed the apostles say to their fellow apostle Paul: You see, brother, how many thousands of believers there are among the Jews, and all these are zealots of the Law (Acts 21:20). Hence it came about that the vessel of election was sent to teach the Gentiles, so that they at least might have a full hope of their faith, hoping nothing from the Law, but in Christ alone. For once the mystery was made manifest, such a gift of Christ was given as would have the effect of perfect salvation; for as at great vows the largest festal presents are given, so also, when a magnificent mystery was made manifest, men were presented, for the sake of joy and gladness, with so excellent a gift that it is enough for them for a saving abundance.

(Eph. 3:10) That the manifold wisdom of God might be made known to the Princes and Powers in the heavenly places through the Church. God thought so well of this that he not only gave this teacher to the Gentiles, but also that through him the truth of Christ might become known to the spirits in the heavenly places, who are the Princes and Powers. Powers for this reason, because they are more powerful among the other spirits; and Princes, because they are princes over the Powers; so that, recognizing through the Church, which has been drawn to life in manifold ways, that the mystery of the one God abides in Christ, they may cease from error. For this is what is being done: that the preaching of the Church may profit these also, and that they may abandon their assent to the devil's tyranny, with which he has armed himself in ungodly presumption against the faith of the one God. This sense was set down beforehand in the twenty-third psalm, where God is shown to be the Lord of the universe, the Spirit of God saying through David: The earth is the Lord's, and its fullness, the world, and all who dwell in it (Ps. 23:1). And in what follows: Lift up the gates of your Prince, and be lifted up, eternal gates, and the King of glory shall enter in. Who is this King of glory? The Lord of powers, he is the King of glory (ibid., 7 ff.). It is said, therefore, to these Princes who are in error in the heavenly places, or to his ministers on earth, that they should take away the gates of their prince, that is, of the devil, from their minds, the gates through which the error of asserting many gods entered. For they are temporal, because all deceit is shut in by time: For the way of the ungodly shall perish (Ps. 1:6). But they are admonished, justice exhorting them, to lift up the eternal gates, because truth endures unto everlasting ages. Hence he says in the hundred and seventeenth psalm: This is the gate of the Lord, the just shall enter by it (Ps. 117:20). For it is plain that the just enter through faith into the heavens, into the kingdom of God, which is never corrupted, as Daniel the prophet testifies, saying among other things: Another kingdom shall arise, incorruptible (Dan. 2:44); but the ungodly, because of unbelief, enter Tartarean hell. This King of glory, then, is Christ, who, rising after he had overcome death, appears to be the Lord and the King of glory. For by the strength of his power, by which he subdued the underworld, it is made manifest that he is the Son of God, who preached that the kingdom of the one God is in the heavens and on earth, to the condemnation of the devil's tyranny.

(Eph. 3:11–12) According to the purpose of the ages in Christ Jesus our Lord, in whom we have freedom in confidence through faith in him. He says that Christ was made manifest in the age in which God purposed to show his mystery for human salvation, so that the knowledge of the mystery of God might confer salvation on mortals; so that, as it were, the dedication of the kingdom of Christ might bestow this grace on believers as festal presents. For although it was done long ago, yet it seems new to the creation that is ignorant of it.

(Eph. 3:13) Wherefore I ask that you do not lose heart in my tribulations for you, which are for your glory. Having made manifest the hope which is in the mystery of Christ, he admonishes them not to take offense because of the tribulations which are inflicted on the saints by the faithless. For it pertains to their glory if the disciples have not been offended at the tribulation of their teacher, being made firm by this hope: that the afflictions of the faithful will be raised up by the glory of the age to come; for this is the proving of the faithful, if they keep their minds on the hope of their faith.

(Eph. 3:14–16) For this reason I bend my knees to the Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, from whom all fatherhood in the heavens and on earth is named, that he may grant you, according to the riches of his majesty, power to be strengthened in his Spirit. He says that he prays this with bent knees: that, since he has deigned to call the Gentiles to know the mystery of his will, he may also grant this, because he is rich: that at his bidding they may be strengthened in the Spirit, that is, that the carnal destructions inflicted by the faithless may not disturb them, but that they may be steadfast, awaiting in the future the reward of this thing.

(Eph. 3:17) That Christ may dwell in the inner man through faith in your hearts. In this he asks that they be the more strengthened: that they may not doubt, but rather believe that Christ, whom they do not see with these eyes, dwells in them; that the Spirit who has been given may pour this into them by the gift of God, so that they may be certain about Christ, that he lives, and is the Son of God, and dwells through faith in our hearts, so that, when we have faith in him in our hearts, we may be seen to have him himself. This serves to make us secure of his help, because he does not desert us but is always present because of the faith in him which he sees in us: inasmuch as his Spirit, who is also God the Father's, is given to us for this reason, that in his stead he may keep us safe, if we assent to him, and may also reveal hidden things; through whom, surely, we ought not to doubt that he himself dwells in us. For this is another Paraclete, between whom there is a distinction of persons, not of nature; for he both receives of him and proceeds from God (John 15:26). For those in whom there is unity of nature belong to one another; hence the Lord says: All things of the Father are mine, and all mine are the Father's (John 16:15).

(Eph. 3:18) That, rooted and grounded in love, you may be able to comprehend with all the saints what is the breadth, and length, and height, and depth. He says that Christ, or the Spirit, dwelling in us accomplishes this: that we be grounded in the love of God, bound by his benefits, and be able, with the saints, who are the apostles and prophets, to know God, immeasurable and inestimable, exceeding all things by the majesty of his power. For when he says: Breadth, and length, and height, and depth, he certainly signifies this: that as in a sphere the length is as great as the breadth, and the height as great as the depth, so too in God all things are equal by the immensity of his infinity. For a sphere is enclosed within a definite measure; but God not only fills all things but also exceeds them; for he is not enclosed, but holds all things within himself, so that he alone is held to be ineffable and infinite; and thanks are rendered to him insufficiently, because, though he is so great, he deigned through Christ to visit man, who was subject to sins and death.

(Eph. 3:19) To know also the love of Christ which surpasses knowledge. After the infinite and incomprehensible knowledge of God the Father and his unspeakable mercy, he wants us also to recognize the love of Christ; because it surpasses knowledge, and "human" is understood with it; so that the love of Christ is held to be above the knowledge of men. For who can gather the reason of the mystery of this love: that God should both be born a man for man's sake, and then die for men, the Lord for slaves, the Creator for the creature, the godly for the ungodly? Why did he so love us? Or to have what of ours, he who needs nothing? Is not this love above the knowledge of men? Or who among men will be able to show this love to anyone, although it be unequal, and for this reason it surpasses human devising? This, therefore, he wants us to know: that our understanding cannot attain to, nor see through, the depth of the benefits of Christ toward us; so that accordingly we may always be suppliant and devoted to him, and that we, who can in no way satisfy what we owe, may at least persevere in faith in him even to death. For he, in keeping with the magnificence of his majesty, did for us things that were unworthy of him, so that we too, as far as we can, may serve him. That you may be filled unto all the fullness of God. Because faith is neither whole in the Father alone nor perfect in the Son alone, he therefore added: That you may be filled unto all the fullness of God; so that in confession and thanksgiving honor may be reserved for the Son as for the Father, and that all things which are from God the Father may be believed to have been made and restored through the Son; so that there may be full perfection of the divinity in the profession of believers. For nothing will be lacking to a Christian when he knows God the Father to be immeasurable in all things, and professes also that the love of Christ is ineffable and surpasses human knowledge.

(Eph. 3:20–21) Now to him who is able to do all things, and more superabundantly than we ask or understand, according to the power which works in us: to him be glory in Christ Jesus, and in the Church, and unto all the ages of ages. Amen. He refers this to the person of the Father: so that, because he knows better what things are useful, and what things are not to be asked for, and when they are to be given, and of what kind and how great they need to be, he himself may govern by his providence and power those who believe in him. To him, therefore, is glory in Christ Jesus, because through him he furnishes all things to us; and because the honor of the Father is in the Son, and in the Church, that is, in the people whom he has deigned to adopt for himself. For in the splendor of the sons is the glory of the father, as the Lord says (Matt. 5:16): So let your light shine before men, that they may see your good works and magnify your Father, who is in the heavens. Unto the ages of ages. Amen, that is, truth unto eternity.

Chapter IV

(Eph. 4:1) I beseech you therefore, brothers, I, a prisoner in the Lord, that you walk worthily of the calling with which you have been called. He exhorts them that, going rightly in the precepts of the Lord, they may be able to arrive at the promised glory. For just as a skilled physician beseeches the sick that, by conducting themselves well, they may be able to recover healthful soundness; so too the teacher of the Gentiles exhorts that, by doing works worthy of their faith, they may be able to receive what has been promised to the saints, knowing that the progress of this matter is to his own glory.

(Eph. 4:2–3) With all humility of mind and modesty, with magnanimity, bearing with one another in love, carefully keeping the spirit of unity in the bond of peace. Now he forbids dissension, and warns that love must be pursued, lest they lose the spirit of unity and peace; and thereby, that they bear with one another with patience. For modesty begets progress; because when they tolerate one another, admonishing with gentleness of mind, they are corrected to good effect, and peace will remain in them, through which they will not undeservedly be called sons of God; for the Lord said: Blessed are the peacemakers, for they shall be called sons of God (Matt. 5:9). For agitation or restlessness produces contention, which is wont even to wish to defend the things it knows to be evil, lest it seem to yield. Hence arises discord, which corrupts the covenants of peace.

(Eph. 4:4) One body and one spirit, even as you have been called in the one hope of your calling. He added these things for the sake of peace and concord; so that, because the Church is one body, the people may be of one mind; because that which is believed is one.

(Eph. 4:5–6) One Lord, one faith, one baptism, one God and Father of all, who is above all, and through all, and in us all. He still commends unity, so that, because it is a unity to which we are called, we may disagree in nothing. For if there is one Lord, that is, Christ, as he himself also says in the Gospel: For one is your teacher, Christ (Matt. 23:10); one faith, one baptism, one God and Father of all, who is above all things, and through all, and in all believers: then there ought also to be one mind and one heart of the people; because these things which he enumerates belong to unity, for they agree in all things. Yet he said that God the Father, because he owes what he is to no one, is above all things, and through all. Through all for this reason, that all are from him: and it is necessary that he be above all these things which are from him. And in us all, that is, in the faithful. For he is in us through confession; because we confess him, and because he has given us his spirit, through whom without doubt he is in us: in pagans he is not; because it is denied by them that he is the Father of Christ.

(Eph. 4:7) But to each one of us grace has been given according to the measure of the gift of our Lord Jesus Christ. Now, lest, because the gifts in men are diverse, they should be thought to hinder unity, he clears this up, showing that it is not contrary to it; because in the gifts the diversity is one of offices, not of nature. He says, moreover, that to each is given as much grace as the Lord has deigned to give; yet not without a measure of equity, because he bestows on each according to his strength, so that he may draw as much as he thirsts for.

(Eph. 4:8) Wherefore he says (Ps. 67:19): Ascending on high, he led captivity captive, he gave gifts to men. This is written in the sixty-seventh psalm. Yet he began to bestow these gifts after his triumphs; for, conquering death, he was exalted on the cross, as he himself had said. For he says: When I am exalted from the earth, I will draw all things to myself (John 12:32). He despoiled the underworld when he took away, consenting to him, the captives whom it was holding in that state, taken through the transgression of Adam or by their own sins, and, ascending thence, led them into the heavens (Matt. 27:52). Of these, some, rising in their bodies, appeared to many as a testimony of death overcome; so that those who perhaps would not believe the resurrection of Christ might be made guilty by the resurrection of these men, whom they knew to have been dead; for they saw these, whom they recognized to have lived formerly. Although captives are led unwilling and as strangers, yet the Savior led both his own and the willing. For since by his appearing he stirred those who longed to be set free, he is said to have taken them captive.

(Eph. 4:9) Now that he ascended, what is it, unless that he also first descended into the lower parts of the earth? It is true that he descended for this reason, that he might ascend; and not like men, who descended for this, that they might remain there. For they were held in the underworld by a sentence, a sentence which could not hold the Savior, because he conquered sin. Having therefore triumphed over the devil, he descended into the heart of the earth, so that his showing of himself might be a preaching to the dead, and as many as were desirous of him might be set free. Nor could he fail to ascend, since he had descended for this: that by the might of his power, death having been trampled underfoot, he might rise again with the captives for whose sake he allowed himself to suffer.

(Eph. 4:10) He who descended is the same who also ascended above all the heavens, that he might fill all things. This is what the Lord says: No one has ascended into heaven except he who descended from heaven, the Son of Man, who is in heaven (John 3:13). That indeed is also contained in the psalm; for it says: His going forth is from the highest heaven, and his return even to its height (Ps. 18:7). He, therefore, descending from the heavens to the earth, was born a man: afterwards, having died, he descended to the underworld, from which, rising on the third day, he ascended before all mortals above all the heavens, so that he might show creation that death had been mocked.

(Eph. 4:11–12) And he himself gave some indeed as apostles, and some as prophets, others as evangelists, and some as pastors and teachers, for the perfecting of the saints, for the work of the ministry. The apostles are bishops (Acts 11:26); the prophets, on the other hand, are expounders of the Scriptures; although in the very beginnings there were prophets, such as Agabus, and the four virgins who prophesied (Acts 21:9), as is contained in the Acts of the Apostles, for the commending of the rudiments of the faith (Acts 7:2 ff.): but now interpreters are called prophets. The evangelists are deacons, as Philip was; although they are not priests, yet they can preach the gospel without the chair, as did both blessed Stephen and the Philip mentioned (Acts 8:5). The pastors can be readers, who feed the listening people with reading; because man does not live by bread alone, but by every word which proceeds from the mouth of God (Matt. 4:4). The teachers, moreover, are exorcists, because in the Church it is they who restrain and beat the unruly: or else those who used to instruct in letters and readings the children who were to be instructed, as is the custom of the Jews, whose tradition passed over to us, and which has fallen into disuse through negligence. Among these, after the bishop, he is understood to be the greater who is said to prophesy because he has unlocked the hidden sense of the Scriptures; especially because he brings forth words of future hope; which order can now be that of the presbyterate. For in the bishop are all the orders: because he is the first priest, that is, he is the chief of the priests, and prophet, and evangelist, and the other offices of the Church to be fulfilled in the ministry of the faithful. Yet after churches had been established in all places, and offices ordained, the matter was arranged otherwise than it had begun. For at first all taught, and all baptized, on whatever days or times there was occasion; for neither did Philip seek a time or a day on which to baptize the eunuch (Acts 8:38), nor did he interpose a fast; nor did Paul and Silas put off the time at which to baptize the jailer with all his household (Acts 16:33); nor did Peter have deacons, or seek a day, when he baptized Cornelius with all his house (Acts 10:48): nor did he do it himself, but he commanded the brothers who had gone with him to Cornelius from Joppa to baptize; for as yet no one had been ordained except the seven deacons. So then, that the people might grow and be multiplied, it was granted to all at the beginning both to preach the gospel, and to baptize, and to explain the Scriptures in the Church; but when the Church had embraced all places, assemblies were established, and rulers, and the other offices in the churches were ordained; so that none of the clergy who had not been ordained should dare to presume upon an office which he knew had not been entrusted or granted to him. And the Church began to be governed by another order and providence; because if all could do the same things, it would be irrational, and the thing would seem common and most cheap. Hence therefore it is that now neither do deacons preach among the people, nor do clergy or laity baptize, nor are believers dipped on just any day, except the sick. Therefore the writings of the Apostle do not in all respects agree with the ordering which is now in the Church; because they were written in the very beginnings. For he also calls Timothy, a presbyter created by himself, a bishop; because the first presbyters were called bishops; so that when one departed, the next might succeed him. Indeed in Egypt the presbyters seal, if the bishop is not present. But because the presbyters who followed began to be found unworthy to hold the primacy, the arrangement was changed, a council providing for it, so that not order but merit should create a bishop, appointed by the judgment of many priests, lest an unworthy man should rashly usurp the office and be a scandal to many. In the Law the priests were born of the stock of Aaron, Levites (Exod. 28:43): but now all are of priestly stock, as the apostle Peter says: Because you are a royal and priestly race; and therefore a priest can be made from the people (1 Pet. 2:9). For the building up of the body of Christ. He says that the ordering of the Church is composed after the fitting-together of the human body, in the unity of profession; so that, though diverse in dignity, all may be one in Christ, having one head, Christ, that is, the author of life.

(Eph. 4:13) Until we all meet in the unity of faith and of the knowledge of the Son of God. He says that the ordering remains for as long as it takes for all who are destined to life to be of one faith in the knowledge of Christ. Unto a perfect man, unto the measure of the age of the fullness of Christ. He exhorts them to labor so that the perfection of faith may be grasped: and its principle is this, that faith hold Christ as perfect and full God, and not measure him by a human measure, but hold him as perfect God in the fullness of divinity. For in saying "perfect" he does not signify a man of temporal age and measure: but by this saying he wishes us to become perfect in the fullness of understanding of the divinity of the Son of God.

(Eph. 4:14) That we may no longer be little children, nor wavering, and carried about by every wind of doctrine in the wickedness of men, in craftiness, for the contrivance of error. He wishes us to be grounded and steadfast in the discipline of the catholic faith, which the apostle Peter professed by God's revelation (Matt. 16:16), and not to be tossed about, straying through the pestilent assertions of diverse heresies, invented by human wickedness, so that through them the error of the deceiving enemy may be brought in under the name of truth. For this is the contrivance of falsehood, that it brings in error under the pretext of truth: and not only under the name of truth; but he also warns lest, just as before we received the faith we wandered, lowly in understanding, through diverse errors, so, having become faithful, we should run hither and thither, fragile and doubtful of the promised hope; because, having now become Christians, they ought to be grounded, since there is no ambiguity in our faith; for it is commended by the power of wonders.

(Eph. 4:15) But doing the truth in love, let us grow in him in all things, who is the head, Christ. He says that this is the truth: that, looking to the love of Christ, with which he loved us and gave himself up for us, we subject all things to him, knowing that he is the author of the life of all, so that the members may be subject as to a head; so that, if others, through error or ill will, do not confess that all things have Christ as head, because they were made by him by the will of the Father, we nevertheless who adhere to the catholic faith ought to do this with all devotion and care, so as to bring to this faith not loss but gain, persevering in this assertion; so that the talk of a depraved mind, armed against the truth, may be suppressed.

(Eph. 4:16) From whom the whole body, fitted together and connected through every joint of supply according to the working in the measure of each part, makes the increase of the body for the building up of itself in love. He teaches on what reasoning he said that Christ is the head of all; for from him, he says, the whole body, that is, the entire choir of Archangels and Potencies, of Principalities and Powers, subsists. For although each of these has its own numbers, and they are legions, as the Lord himself said (Matt. 26:53); yet because they are from one and through one, he calls them all together one body. For just as members, though they are of one body, are yet diverse in action and dignity: so too creation was founded by God through Christ in such a way that, from the Cherubim and Seraphim, potencies which are beneath the seat of God, and from the Angels whom the Lord himself calls holy (Mark 8:38), there is as it were a kind of chain, descending in order down to the firmament; so that the whole is both joined and yet distinguished by merits: by merits, I say, not only of offices, but also of natures. For it is not that, because he says all things are joined, they will be of one nature: but whereas in the body the members make things distinct by their offices, not by nature; so in all these it is not members but the natures themselves that make the distinctions. This therefore is what is labored for: that creation be brought back to one mind, and in mind be one body, while the profession of all is one, so that it may agree in the love of the Creator to its own advancement; for it is rebuilding itself into a temple of the Lord, for it had been destroyed by the madness and fury of the devil.

(Eph. 4:17–18) This therefore I say and testify in the Lord, that you do not walk as the Gentiles also walk in the vanity of their mind, darkened in understanding, alienated from the faith of God through the ignorance which is in them. The Creator having been made manifest, and the reasoning shown which creation ought to follow, in the faith of its God alone, he solemnly charges them before God that they go and think no otherwise than they are taught; lest they be found like the Gentiles, who receive neither the faith nor the way of life which the Law commands, having their understanding darkened, so that they do not contemplate the truth of the divine name. For, following vain things, which are idolatry, they are bridled by the devil, lest they walk the way of the one God, so that he may make them companions of his own perdition.

(Eph. 4:19) And the blindness of their heart, who, despairing, have given themselves up to lewdness, unto the working of all uncleanness, and of avarice. He indicates that the Gentiles have blindness of heart through despair of the future resurrection. For, as though they were not to exist any longer, they defile their life with most shameful working, unwilling to submit themselves to the law of faith; because it forbids pleasure for the sake of the hope of the life to come, which these men judge should be laughed at for this reason, that they may seem with right to dishonor themselves and to covet the goods of others with greedy desire, as though after this passage (of life) there were no life.

(Eph. 4:20–21) But you have not so learned Christ, if indeed you have heard him, and have been taught in him, as the truth is in Jesus. It is clear that Christians have not learned despair after this death, but hope as regards both; for to the faithful life is promised, but to the faithless death, which is eternal punishment; so that, whether death or life, it is learned in Christ; because he who follows him will rise through him to life: but he who separates himself from him will through him be handed over to Gehenna; for he will be condemned with him as judge. And since there are some in the Church who, under the name of Christ, do not follow the truth of Christ; therefore: As the truth is, he says, in Jesus; so that if they have been taught in such a way as the truth is in Jesus, then they may be seen to have learned Christ, and to have heard him, and to have been taught in him: for Christ teaches concerning himself what he is, and how great he is to be believed, and what hope there is in him, and what sort believers ought to be. For he who has learned Christ knows that he rose from the dead, that he might be a pattern for the faithful; and therefore there is great hope, in return for this death, for those who love God.

(Eph. 4:22) That you put off, according to your former way of life, the old man, who is corrupted according to the desires of error. He who has heard Christ has learned this: to depart from his former way of life, which he calls the old man because of the past time of ignorance. Man, therefore, is one and the same, who, if he follows his former vices, is called old: but if he walks in newness of life, a stranger to the error of the world, which lies either in deed or in thought, he is called fresh; not, of course, in the substance of flesh or soul, but in understanding and in the way of life. Yet the old man is called corrupted because of his adulterated mind and shameful life.

(Eph. 4:23–24) But be renewed in the sense of your mind, and put on the new man, who according to God has been created in truth and justice. It is just and true to follow the right way: and he follows the right way who, devoted to his Creator, walks in his will, which the Gentile world does not know. For he who is renewed in the spirit of his mind is said to have put on the new man, who is Christ; for he puts on Christ who, reborn in Christ through faith, is an emulator of the life which Christ handed down; so that he may be seen to have been created according to God: for a man is created in that in which he is wise; and therefore the truth and justice of God are unknown to the Gentiles. For when believers acknowledge God as Creator, keeping his commandments, they are created in justice and truth.

(Eph. 4:25) Wherefore, having put away lying, speak truth each one with his neighbor (Zech. 8:16); for we are members of one another. Because we have been created in truth and justice, reborn in baptism, therefore, that we may remain in it, we are commanded to put away lying, having taken up truth, and not to deal with a brother in anything by guile; so that, because we are members of one body, we may support one another's causes in truth.

(Eph. 4:26) Be angry and do not sin. Since one cannot be forbidden to be angry (seeing that anger is a natural motion, conceived from causes, which is wont to serve the progress of the one who offends), he therefore says that one must be angry (Ps. 4:5), but warns that one should not sin through anger. For we are wont to go beyond the measure while we are angry, and we ourselves, who rebuke sinners, are found to sin, when we are angrier and burden the sinner more than he deserves. This then he teaches: that anger itself should be with moderation, and that no one show himself too just toward the offender: because some are wont to sin in such a way that they seem to deserve to be struck with blows as great as they cannot bear: and he who could perhaps have been corrected is taken out of life, not without sin on the part of the one who is angry. Hence it is that Solomon: Do not, he says, be very just; for there is one who perishes in his own justice (Eccles. 7:17). But we ought to be imitators of God, who has a tempered justice; for though it would be just for the wicked to be punished, he bears them with his patience, so that some of them may be corrected. Us ourselves, his servants, he bears with when we sin, nor does he quickly strike anyone.

(Eph. 4:27) Let not the sun go down upon your wrath: do not give place to the devil. He wants anger to pass and not to be kept in the breast, so that, having arisen in the day, it may fall with the day; because if it remains in the mind, it gives the devil occasion for boasting. For it is inevitable that an angry mind thinks evil things, which is what the devil desires; for he finds a mind prepared for evil, and inserting himself into it, he deceives by his own impulse a man made for life; because to think belongs to man, to carry out belongs to the devil.

(Eph. 4:28) He who used to steal, let him steal no longer: but rather let him labor, working with his hands what is good, so that he may have something to give to those who suffer need. That the way of life of the new man may remain, he exhorts that there be no return again to past vices and sins; for it will profit nothing to be called new, when one is proved by evil works to be the old man. So the Christian is commanded not only not to take what belongs to others, but also to supply from his own labor those who suffer want; so that, devoted to good works, he may repay what he had previously taken away, and for the rest lay up treasure for himself for the future; for he who ceases from theft can have pardon, not glory. For what great thing is it, or of what merit, to keep one's hands off what belongs to others? For one advances to merit when one gives of one's own to the needy.

(Eph. 4:29) Let no evil speech proceed from your mouth; but if there is any good for the building up of faith, that it may give grace to the hearers. In a servant of God all things ought to appear good, nor ought his purity to be stained in any part. For what does it profit to have a clean life and a filthy mouth, since the Lord says that each one will be either justified or condemned by his words (Matt. 12:37)? Nor can it seem credible that he lives well who speaks evil; for evil speaking draws one to many vices. For he can speak evil who talks shamefully, and he who disparages the good, and he who chatters with guile, and he who lies. These things produce horror; but good and sober talk gives grace and example to the hearers, so that they may honor God in his words, built up in the faith of Christ.

(Eph. 4:30) And do not grieve the Holy Spirit of God, in whom you have been sealed for the day of redemption. When we act well, the Holy Spirit, who has been given to us, rejoices in us, seeing his admonitions advance our benefit, as was said by the Lord: So there will be joy in heaven over one sinner repenting (Luke 15:10). The Holy Spirit therefore rejoices for our salvation, not for himself, since he has no need of gladness; so, on the contrary, if we are not obedient to him, he is grieved, because he has no progress in us; for he wants us to belong to life. Yet he is not grieved in such a way as to suffer, since he is impassible: but when he says he is grieved, he signifies this on our account; because he deserts us, in that we have offended him by despising his admonitions. His grieving, therefore, is our unworthiness, which prevents us from being called sons of God; for it is he, the Holy Spirit, who sanctifies the sons of God, remaining in us.

(Eph. 4:31) Let all bitterness, and anger, and indignation, and clamor, and blasphemy be taken away from you with all malice. All these things which can bring ruin to souls he records as things to be removed; for through anger comes indignation, through indignation clamor, as it were the voice of one raving, which is foreign to a servant of God: through clamor creeps in blasphemy, which cannot but be evil. This makes life bitter, drawing it to death: and therefore one must beware of all these; for the fear of God ought to temper and set a limit to the causes that arise. But because some can, with anger and clamor held down in the mind, keep their malice for retaliation when an occasion is found, he therefore added that these should be taken away with all malice; because not only is it malice to blaspheme, but also to feign peace in the face and in the mind to meditate discord.

(Eph. 4:32) But be kind to one another, merciful, forgiving one another, just as God also in Christ has forgiven you. By the example of God the Father he exhorts them to become kind and merciful to one another, as the Lord also does: Be, he says, merciful, that you may be like your Father, who is in heaven (Luke 6:36). For if the Lord through his Son has had mercy on servants, how much more ought the servants themselves to have mercy on one another, and to forgive, if one sins against another? These are the precepts of him who has shown mercy: who, if he is despised in these, will without doubt revoke the sentence by which he had given mercy: and so it will happen as the Lord says in the Gospel about him who, having received mercy from the Lord, was caught being ungodly toward his fellow servant (Matt. 18:33).

Chapter V

(Eph. 5:1) Be therefore imitators of God, as most dear sons. This is what he said above: that by this exhortation we are commanded to be imitators of God the Father, so that, if we obey, we may be held to be most dear sons of God. For that son is most dear to his father who is an imitator of his good qualities.

(Eph. 5:2) And walk in love, as Christ loved us, and delivered himself up for us as a victim and an offering to God for an odor of sweetness. Just as he wants us to be imitators of God the Father in kindness and mercy, so also of Christ his Son in love; so that, as he loved us, delivering himself up for us, so we too ought to lay down even our lives for one another. This the apostle John also said: that after the Lord's example we must lay down our lives for the brethren (1 John 3:16). If, then, the death of Christ is to God for an odor of sweetness, God accepted his death gladly. And if this is true, those who crucified him according to the will of God did not sin. But it is not so; for Christ is said to have offered himself to God in that he suffered himself to be killed, persevering in the will of God his Father; for whoever is justly put to death, offering himself to the devil, does his will, so as to deserve to be killed. So too Christ, while he cleaved to the will of God, displeased the devil, and the devil killed him for this reason, because he had offered himself to God by carrying out righteousness. For in the offering righteousness and truth are signified; for it is this that God regards, and this is his acceptable sacrifice. Therefore he who is killed undeservedly pleases God; not because he is killed, but because he preserved righteousness even unto death. For Christ undertook this death for the sake of life; hence it is also called an odor of sweetness to God. Yet to the Romans he says that God delivered him up for us all (Rom. 8:32). Now we must ask how God delivered him up, or how he offered himself to God; for as far as the words go, this seems contradictory. God is said to have delivered him up in that he permitted him to be killed, as the Lord says to Pilate: You would have no power against me, unless it had been given you from above (John 19:11). For power was given him, but to one who was willing; that is, he was permitted to do what he wished, and this is to deliver up: for in letting it pass he delivers up; for if he had been unwilling to permit it, he would not have been killed. He permitted him, then, to be killed, but by those who wished to kill him not under compulsion but of their own accord. They are not therefore exempt from punishment because what they wished was permitted them; for condemnation falls on them on this count, that they willed it. Yet God permitted this for this reason: because he knew that this man would stand against the devil on behalf of many. For it was fitting that the enemy, having no foreknowledge of things to come, should be permitted to do what was against himself while he thought he was doing it as if for himself; so that, charging his lack of foreknowledge to himself, he might be tormented with repentance for his deed. But that Christ offered himself, or that God delivered him up, is one and the same; for the will of both is one.

(Eph. 5:3) But fornication, and all uncleanness or avarice, let it not even be named among you, as befits saints. What he says is plain. Yet how grave a thing is avarice, which we let pass, since it is compared with fornication and uncleanness! And yet it is regarded by us in this way, as some fault of no moment, though it is a grave sin. They cannot, then, be saints in whom anything of these things that he forbids has been found.

(Eph. 5:4) Or filthiness, or foolish talk, or scurrility, which do not pertain to the matter; but rather the giving of thanks. Since some are wont to do something shameful, or to stir up a jest with foolish talk, as if it were not a sin, he forbids this, because the servants of God ought always to appear sober and modest; for through foolish talk one sins. Hence Solomon: The fool, he says, does evil through laughter (Prov. 10:23). And the Lord says in the Gospel that every idle word that men shall have spoken, they shall render an account of it in the day of judgment (Matt. 12:36). Nor can words be judged to go unpunished that are said in order to insult someone; therefore these things do not pertain to any wholesome matter. Wherefore he advises that those things be done and spoken which advance our profit and the praise of God, on whose law we must meditate day and night, as it is written in the psalm (Ps. 1:2). For from their way of life and their conduct the servants of God are to be recognized even when they are silent; so that, as far as the true is distant from the false, so far may the servants of God be distant from the servants of the devil.

(Eph. 5:5) For know this, that every fornicator, or unclean person, or avaricious man, which is idolatry, has no inheritance in the kingdom of God and of Christ. He shows for what reason these things must be put away: because whoever is such will have no part in the kingdom of heaven, which is the Father's and the Son's. Yet in order to teach that avarice is a perilous thing, he calls it idolatry, than which no sin is greater. Let us see, then, why avarice is called idolatry. Idolatry usurps the honor of God and claims it for the creature, when it asserts that his handiwork shares with him in the name of deity, which belongs to the Maker alone. Avarice, moreover, has been made equal to it for this reason, that in like manner the avaricious man usurps for himself and hoards the things that are God's; so that what God granted in common for the use of all, avarice denies, since it gathers these things to itself for this very purpose, that others may not use them; and this causes things to be sold dear, so that the poor cannot live; for if they were not hoarded, the abundance of all things would bring benefit. And indeed among the ancients, because there was no avarice, there was no dearness of prices either. Rightly, then, has it been compared to idolatry, because it harms many. It is likewise hostile to the Creator; for God wills and commands that the poor be relieved from the things he created, and avarice gainsays it. See whether it can exist without the devil.

(Eph. 5:6–7) Let no one lead you astray with empty words; for because of these the wrath of God comes upon the sons of distrust. Do not, therefore, become partakers with them. Empty discourses are vain; and vanity is the worship of idols, which certainly takes its beginning not from God but from error. He teaches, then, that assent should not be given to the words of these men; for they are always clamoring, and with a certain seductive deceit they hold up the custom of antiquity against the faith, judging that the reckoning of present things, not the hope of things to come, is what must be maintained. Therefore vengeance comes upon them from heaven, that they may know that what they refused to believe is true. From this vanity he teaches that the servants of God ought to be kept far apart, lest they become partakers of their punishment.

(Eph. 5:8) For you were once darkness, but now light in the Lord. Darkness is ignorance, because no one sees in darkness; and ignorance is the Gentile world, or the faithlessness from which they have been drawn to faith by the grace of God, being made light, that is, truth; for in the light the things that are are made manifest. Indeed pagans, celebrating their mysteries in darkness in a cave, are made sport of with their eyes veiled. Those of them who have become Christians, having come to know the truth, are called sons of light, because God is light, whose faith they receive in truth.

(Eph. 5:9) Walk as sons of light, for the fruit of light is in all goodness, and righteousness, and truth. He says this so that they may so conduct themselves that they are not undeservedly called sons of light. For the works of light are done openly, without fear; but the works of darkness in secret, because they are unlawful; for every sin fears to be done openly.

(Eph. 5:10) Prove what it is that pleases God. From the magnificence of holiness and goodness it can be weighed in what works God delights; for he himself said: Be holy, as I also am holy, the Lord your God (Lev. 19:2). And the Lord: Be, he says, merciful, as also your Father (Luke 6:36). These things please God; for in holiness there is purification, and in mercy full and perfect righteousness.

(Eph. 5:11) And do not share in the unfruitful works of darkness. He warns that we should not be polluted by the deeds of the wicked through consent; for they are unfruitful toward good, and profitable for evil.

(Eph. 5:12) But rather even rebuke them; for the things that are done by them in secret it is shameful even to speak of. He wants them not only to be strangers to evil deeds, but also to reprove the evil deeds of those who work shamefully in secret. For sins are proven only when they are reproved; so that they may admire the sons of light, whom, when they are justly reproved by them, they will not be able to gainsay, but will rather revere. But all things, when they are rebuked, are made manifest by the light. It is true; for sins are seen at the moment they are rebuked, since he who sins, so long as he is not corrected, does not seem to himself to be sinning. For everything that is made manifest is light. In what is manifest there is no ambiguity, nor can what is openly a transgression be excused. For just as, once the sun has appeared, can it now be denied that it is day? So too, once the sin is made manifest, can the rebuke seem false?

(Eph. 5:14) Wherefore he says: Arise, you who sleep, and rise up from the dead, and you will touch Christ. By this sleep he signifies the stupor of the mind, which is estranged from the true way. And this estrangement is a kind of death, from which he is urged to rise again; so that, coming to his senses, he may acknowledge the truth, which is Christ. The faithless, then, and the vicious, sunk in the mire in despair of life, are admonished to rise again or emerge, and to have part with Christ; that they may pass from darkness to light and from death to life.

(Eph. 5:15–16) See therefore how you walk cautiously, not as unwise, but as wise, redeeming the time, since the days are evil. Since above he commanded that faithlessness and the vices of life be reproved by the servants of God, he now adds that these very things are to be reproved prudently, on account of scandal; so that one must conduct oneself with prudence among the faithless, especially at a time when the faithless reign, relying on a profane king; so that if a Christian sees a restless and wrathful opponent, he should let him be, and not even provoke him to blasphemy and to stirring up a storm; but he should speak where opportunity is given. For just as, when he is asked among powerful faithless men what he is, he ought not to keep silent, nor deny what he is; so also, if there is fear in disputing among them, he must hold back; since indeed it is a loss to sow upon thorns. For this is the part of a prudent man, to know how he should answer each one; for there is one who, though he is faithless, is yet not utterly intractable, so as not to suffer himself to hear the words of faith. There is again one in whom the zeal of faithlessness so burns that the faith must be discussed not only not with him but not even in his presence; for as he clamors and blasphemes, a scandal arises for many. Hence Paul and Barnabas, when they heard men blaspheming against them, shook out their garments and withdrew from them (Acts 13:46).

(Eph. 5:17) Therefore do not become imprudent, but understanding what the will of the Lord is. The Lord Jesus warns that we must be zealous for peace. And the vessel of election: If it can be, he says, as far as lies in you, having peace with all men (Rom. 12:18). And to Timothy: The servant, he says, of the Lord must not quarrel, but be gentle toward all (2 Tim. 2:24). This is the will of the Lord, that what is to be done by his servants should be done with modesty; for disturbance and uproar, or contention with animosity, breed enmity. He added these things, then, on account of what precedes, where he wants the servants of God to correct the transgressions of sinful men; so that, since dissension and brawling are wont to arise from this, they may reprove sinners with moderation and gentleness, lest, being provoked to anger, they rush into strife. And so he warns that this must be seen to prudently, that there may be progress.

(Eph. 5:18) And do not be drunk with wine, in which is debauchery. The sober can have confidence in reproving; for a good way of life strikes fear into the offender; nor will the one who is reproved be able to take it ill that he is rebuked, when he knows the excellent way of life of the one who admonishes him. For where there is drunkenness, there is also debauchery; and debauchery provokes filthiness. Wherefore it is fitting that they be sober, that the rights of a good way of life may be preserved.

(Eph. 5:19) But be filled with the Holy Spirit, speaking to yourselves in psalms and hymns and spiritual songs, singing and making melody in your hearts to God. The Holy Spirit loves a rational life; for if we live well, we shall be filled with him, as he relates, to confess and extol the gift of God with varied melody of voice, so that praise may be sung to God in every tongue. For he in whom the Spirit is always meditates on spiritual things; so that it may not be on the lips only, but may burst forth from the heart, because of the ancient saying of the Lord through the prophet. For he says: This people honors me with their lips, but their heart is far from me (Isa. 29:13).

(Eph. 5:20–21) Giving thanks always for all things in the name of our Lord Jesus Christ to God and the Father, being subject to one another in the fear of Christ. We are commanded to give thanks to God in all his gifts, who has also deigned to adopt us through Christ, his own Son, through whom we have known him, and have learned that God is to be worshipped in the Spirit (because God is spirit), each subjecting himself to the other on account of the fear of Christ, who commanded that humility be practiced (John 4:24).

(Eph. 5:22–24) Let wives be subject to their husbands, as to the Lord; since the husband is the head of the wife, as Christ also is the head of the Church, he himself the savior of his body. But as the Church is subject to Christ, so also let wives be subject to their husbands in all things. Women are commanded by natural law to be subject to men, because the man is the author of the woman; hence, he says, as to the Lord, so they ought to be subject to their husbands. Hence Sarah called Abraham lord (Gen. 18:12); for as Christ is the head of the Church, so also the man is the head of the woman. For the Church took its beginning from Christ, and therefore it is subject to him; so also the woman to the man, that she may be subject. Yet there is a difference in this: that the woman is consubstantial with the man, whereas the Church can share with Christ in name, not in nature.

(Eph. 5:25–28) Husbands, love your wives, as Christ also loved the Church, and delivered himself up for her, that he might sanctify her, cleansing her by the washing of water in the word; that he might present to himself a glorious Church, not having spot, or wrinkle, or any such thing, but that she may be holy and spotless. So also husbands ought to love their wives. Wives are commanded to be subject and to hold their husbands in reverence; but husbands are admonished so to love their wives that they would even lay down their lives for them, having, for love's sake, a zeal for their affection and their discipline, that they may be devout and holy. He loves his own body who loves his wife. By natural reason the woman is a portion of the man's body, and therefore the man loves himself in the woman; just as, if he commits fornication, he sins against himself, because the two are in one flesh. The persons, then, do not divide the substance, so that through the persons a number of natures should arise; rather, they are in the unity of nature.

(Eph. 5:29) For no one ever hated his own flesh, but nourishes and cherishes it, as Christ also the Church. By the example of the Savior he exhorts husbands to love their wives: as Christ nourishes and cherishes his body, so also the husband his wife, because she is his flesh.

(Eph. 5:30) Since we are members of his body, and of his flesh, and of his bones. This is to be understood spiritually; for he said that we are his members for this reason, because he himself is the head of the whole Church; so that, since through him we began to be, he is called our head, as the one from whom the whole body subsists; so that all spiritual creation may be his members, provided that it bends the knee in his name.

(Eph. 5:31–32) For this cause a man shall leave father and mother, and shall cleave to his wife, and they shall be two in one flesh (Gen. 2:24). To commend unity he gave an example of unity; so that, as man and woman are one by nature, so also Christ and the Church may be understood to be one by faith. This mystery is great, but I speak in Christ and in the Church. He signifies that the great sacrament of the mystery lies in the unity of man and woman. Yet this is not what he brings forward; rather, he calls for another cause, not at odds with the mystery mentioned, which he knows pertains to the progress of the human race, that is, to the Church and the Savior; so that, just as a man, having left his parents, cleaves to his wife, so also the Church, having left all error, may cleave and be subject to its head, which is Christ.

Nevertheless you too, one by one, let each love his own wife as himself; and let the wife see that she fears her husband. Since there is one nature in man and woman, the man is therefore admonished to love the woman as himself; but the woman, because she is a person inferior by reason of her condition, not of her nature, is subject to the man and is commanded to fear him.

Chapter VI

(Eph. 6:1–2) Children, obey your parents in the Lord, for this is right: Honor your father and mother. A law is given to children, that, since they owe their existence to their parents as authors, they should obey them; for it is right that they show reverence to those through whom they exist.

(Eph. 6:3) Which is the first commandment of the promise, that it may be well with you, and that you may be long-lived upon the earth. This promise is contained in Exodus (Exod. 20:12), that parents be held in honor, so that it might be well with them and they might reach a more advanced age. Yet how did he call this the first commandment, when it is certain that the first commandment is contained thus: You shall have no other gods besides me. Then: You shall not make for yourself any likeness of the things that are in heaven above, and on the earth beneath. The third: You shall not take the name of the Lord your God in vain. The fourth: You shall observe, he says, my sabbaths. And the fifth: Honor father and mother, etc. (Exod. 20:3 ff.). But because the first four commandments pertain to God, these are understood to be contained on the first tablet, and the rest pertain to man: that he honor his parents, that he not kill, not commit fornication, not steal, not bear false witness, nor covet anything of his neighbor's. These six commandments are seen to have been written on the second tablet, and the first of them is: Honor father and mother. For this reason, then, he said: Which is the first commandment in the promise; so as to distinguish between the commandments that pertain to God and those that pertain to man.

(Eph. 6:4) And you, fathers, do not provoke your children to anger; but bring them up in the discipline and correction of the Lord. So that the Law may be kept, by which it is provided that children honor their parents, another Law is also given by the Apostle to parents: that they so train their children that they do not despise the commandments of the Law; for if they have been nurtured in the fear of the Lord, they will be able to observe what has been enjoined. For if they provoke their children to anger, they themselves do not keep the measure of the Law either; for they seem to force them into being found transgressors. For in anger no one recognizes what is profitable; therefore they ought to learn temperance from the example of their parents, so that they may follow what is profitable. For fathers ought to remember that they too were once children, and certainly did not wish to be exasperated by their fathers. Children too ought to recognize that they will be fathers, and would not wish to be dishonored by their own children. Therefore let both show to one another what they require of one another.

(Eph. 6:5) Servants, obey your carnal masters with fear and trembling in the simplicity of your heart, as to Christ. Since our religion is the most sublime of all and alone powerful, and one that promises the kingdom of heaven to believers, lest pride should for this reason arise in men, all are commanded to discharge what they owe in their present conditions or offices; so that they may the more stir the minds of the faithless toward the worship of God, which they see to be both just and humble. For when masters see that their servants have become better, and have made faithful progress in rendering their services, they render them with all freedom what they deserve. Servants too, when they perceive the gentleness of their masters, desire with all eagerness the faith by whose so gentle reins human duties are governed. For even he who is in servitude, if he has a servant of his own, does not want to be despised by him; and therefore neither ought he to despise his own master: and just as he wants his master to deal with him, so ought he too to deal with his servant, and so he will be able to please God.

(Eph. 6:6–8) Not serving to the eye, as pleasing men, but as servants of Christ, doing the will of God from the heart, serving with kindness as to the Lord, and not as to men; knowing that whatever good each one has done, this he will receive from the Lord, whether servant or free. On account of the fear of God he wants just and faithful services to be rendered to men; for he serves God whose rights he keeps in his services to a carnal master, God who, as he hears, will repay him for his just deeds in the day of judgment and will bear witness before all others. This can benefit even the salvation of the masters; for seeing servants made faithful ministers through the grace of God, which is a difficult thing, they have been able to praise the glory of God.

(Eph. 6:9) And you, masters, do the same things to them, forbearing threats: knowing that both their Lord and yours is in heaven, and there is no respect of persons with him. Since carnal masters are certainly not ignorant that God is the common Lord of all, let them require such services as they would not take it ill to have required of themselves. For with what measure anyone has measured, it shall be measured back to him (Matt. 7:2); for the Lord, a just judge, discerns causes, not persons.

(Eph. 6:10) For the rest, brethren, be strengthened in the Lord, and in the might of his power. After all his admonitions and exhortations, after the order of discipline devoutly handed down and the plan of the mystery made manifest, he wants them to be strong in faith through confidence in the power of God; so that, knowing that the hope of the promise is firm, they may be ready in the service of God, holding the helm upright against the waves stirred up by the recklessness of the enemy. For he stirs up a storm against the servants of God, but it is he himself who suffers shipwreck.

(Eph. 6:11–12) Put on the armor of God, that you may be able to stand against the nations. The armor of God is steadfast faith, by which alone Satan has always been conquered. For your wrestling is not against flesh and blood, but against the Principalities and Powers of this world, against the rulers of this darkness, against the spiritual forces of wickedness, which are in the heavenly places. The Apostle, being certain that the destructions inflicted by the faithless on the servants of God are administered with the devil as their author, whose height is great and impossible for men, warns that against these things the armor of God must be taken up, by whose power alone all the devices of the devil are overcome and destroyed. For it is he whose satellites they are whom he calls the rulers of this darkness, most wicked spirits who dwell in the heavenly places, in the firmament of the world. These are the leaders of error, the rulers of darkness, that is, those set over ignorance and faithlessness. For the rulers of darkness are to be understood as governing toward perversity, as masters of unbelief.

(Eph. 6:13–17) Therefore take up the whole of the armor, that you may be able to resist in the evil day, and stand perfect in all things, having your loins girded in love, and having put on the breastplate of righteousness, and having your feet shod in the preparation of the Gospel of peace. Above all these, taking up the shield of faith, in which you will be able to quench all the fiery darts of the wicked one; and take the helmet of salvation, and the sword of the spirit, which is the word of God. Since we wage war against most savage enemies, cunning in every kind of evasion, we must therefore keep watch with all caution and care, so that whatever they attempt, they may find us fortified and prepared. For it must needs be that God helps those whom he sees keeping watch in prayer and expecting victory from his armor. But in a war of this kind there is need of a sober mind and a pure conscience, because the fight is not against carnal forces of wickedness but against spiritual ones. For against earth-born enemies the body is strengthened with rich feeding, and the mind is fired with drink, so that it may take on the daring to fight back; but against the spiritual forces of wickedness one must fight spiritually, and the armor of sobriety and abstinence must be taken up; so that, filled with the Holy Spirit, we may overcome the unclean and wandering spirits. Thus, then, we shall gird our loins in truth, if we are prepared to resist error. For everyone who wishes to work girds himself, so that, with the hindrance removed, he may work more diligently.

(Eph. 6:18–20) Praying with all prayer and supplication at every time, in the spirit, and watching thereunto with all perseverance and entreaty for all the saints, and for me; that speech may be given me in the opening of my mouth to make known openly the mystery of the Gospel, for which I discharge an embassy in a chain; that in it I may use open freedom, as I ought to speak. If we conduct ourselves rightly, the Holy Spirit remains in us, and we can obtain what we ask. This, then, is to pray in the spirit at all times: to direct prayer to God with a clean conscience and whole faith. For he prays in the flesh who prays with a polluted mind, intending to sin again, not by chance or by being taken unawares, but on purpose. But it is possible not to sin unto death, if one prays perseveringly; so that the soul, with diligent mind, may always be intent on the Law of God, meditating day and night on the things that God loves. Then they can also pray for the saints; for it is not unworthy that the members should be solicitous for one another; nor could it seem rash if fit disciples intercede for their teacher, helping him, so that, setting hindrances aside, he may use freedom to preach, as it was handed down by the Author; lest, pressed by the force of tribulations, he should through very fear blunt the edge of his teaching, its severity lost. He exhorts, then, that prayer be made for him in two ways: that his understanding be filled with the spirit to utter the mystery fully, and that the capacity of boldness in asserting it be given him; and God certainly hears this prayer gladly, for he sees his embassy despised in his Apostle; hence, indignant in his own cause, he will surely not deny its effect. For if it is the custom and the law not to inflict destruction on the ambassadors of men, with what presumption and audacity is not only destruction but even death inflicted on the ambassadors of God! Wherefore he also says: For which I discharge an embassy in a chain. For he was not permitted to speak freely, but, delivering the words of God in subjection and under constraint, he was subjected to bonds, to blows, and to deaths: for he was both stoned and fought with beasts at Ephesus (1 Cor. 15:32).

(Eph. 6:21–22) But that you also may know the things that concern me, what I am doing, Tychicus, the beloved brother and faithful minister in the Lord, will make fully known to you: whom I have sent to you for this very purpose, that he may learn the things that concern you, and comfort your hearts. Through Tychicus the Ephesians learn what the Apostle is doing, and he likewise learns what the Ephesians were doing. Concerning the Apostle there was no doubt, since the things he did were divine; but this was to be known about him: what he was doing among the faithless and their plots. Concerning the Ephesians, on the other hand, this was to be learned: whether they were making progress or not. So that they might obey, then, and listen gladly to the words of Tychicus, he commends him, calling him a most beloved brother and a fit minister of God.

Peace to the brethren, and love with faith from God our Father, and the Lord Jesus Christ. He wishes peace, which is the door of love, to be with the brethren, and love with faith; so that, having entered through peace, they may abide in love, which is from faith. For this is of God the Father and of his Son our Lord Jesus Christ.

Grace be with all who love our Lord Jesus Christ in incorruption. Amen. The Apostle, foreknowing that there would be many holding a view of desperate faith concerning our Lord Jesus Christ, says that eternal grace will abide with those who love Christ, thinking rightly about him and in accord with the will of God the Father; for from those who call him degenerate the grace given them will be taken away.
