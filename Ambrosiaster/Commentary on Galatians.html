Commentary on Galatians

Latin source: the commentary on the Epistle to the Galatians by the anonymous Roman writer called Ambrosiaster (written at Rome about 366–384), from the Maurist text of 1690, printed under the name of Ambrose in J.-P. Migne, Patrologia Latina 17 (Paris, 1845), cols. 337–372, verified against the page images and collated in full with the Maurist edition (Paris, 1690). Migne's misprints where the Maurist edition he reprints has the right reading are corrected (eleven words and two places of punctuation), as are his misprinted Scripture references; at 4:7 the lemma reads jam ("no longer") with Ballerini's edition (Milan, 1877) and the manuscripts, where Migne and the Maurists print tam; at 1:8 (vos) and 2:9–10 (legere, where Ballerini prints egere) the text stands as both editions print it. The lemmata are translated from Ambrosiaster's own Old Latin text of Paul, and all other Scripture as he quotes it, from his own Latin; italics mark the lemmata and the other Scripture he quotes. The edition's verse labels are given as (Gal. chapter:verse). This English translation is released into the public domain.

Prologue

Everyone who believes in Christ and observes the Law of works understands Christ wrongly: like the Symmachians too, who draw their origin from the Pharisees, who, keeping the whole Law, call themselves Christians, defining Christ after the manner of Photinus not as God and man, but as man only. For if they understood Christ to be God, they would hope for nothing from the Law of works, that is, from new moons, the sabbath, circumcision and the distinction of foods; because he who worships the God and Lord of the Law is above the Law, and he who is in concord with the lawgiver cannot be judged by the law. Finally, the Law was not given for the sake of those who worshiped God, but of those who both believed and acted wrongly, to the contempt of the Creator. Hence he says to Timothy: For the law is not laid down for the righteous, but for the unrighteous and for those who conduct themselves wrongly. (1 Tim. 1:9) And Moses says thus: You shall make the children of Israel fearful. (Lev. 15:31). Hence the Galatians too, or those who were subverting them, still having their heart covered by the cloud of error, do not discern the sense of the prophet Isaiah, who says among other things: For a word completing and cutting short in equity, because a shortened word will the Lord make upon the earth. (Isa. 10:23). If then they understood these sayings, they would withdraw from the Law, knowing that from the preaching of John the Baptist the Law now ceases, so that faith alone, cut short out of the Law, suffices for salvation. For in the Law is contained, concerning God the Father, and concerning Christ his Son, and concerning the Holy Spirit, what was predestined in time, which has been made manifest in the preaching; for if the children of Israel were circumcised for the sake of a seal, so that thereby they might appear to be children of Abraham according to the flesh, circumcision ought rightly to cease; because believers have the sign of death overcome, by which they are recognized to be children of Abraham, but according to faith; for to Abraham was promised one who, by his coming, would conquer death. Finally, Abraham was awaiting him, and perceiving in the spirit that he was born, he rejoiced, knowing that he would shortly be brought into the heavens. Hence the Lord says to the Jews: Abraham longed to see my day: and he saw it, and rejoiced (John 8:56); for at the time when the children of Israel were still together in the desert, before they were neighbors of the Gentiles, they were not circumcised. But when they were to mingle with the Gentiles, then they were circumcised by Joshua the son of Nun with rock (Josh. 5:3), which signifies Christ: For the rock, he says, was Christ (1 Cor. 10:4), so as to designate the future circumcision of the heart through Christ, as Moses and the prophet Jeremiah had said: Circumcise yourselves to God, and circumcise the foreskin of your heart (Deut. 10:16; Jer. 4:4); so that, the darkness being cut away through the remission of sins received, they might become worthy to see the glory of God, which, placed under the Law, they had been unable to see because of sin. So, in order both to mark Christ as God and to assert that he had not learned from man the things he taught, he begins thus:

Chapter I

(Gal. 1:1) Paul an apostle, not from men, nor through man, but through Jesus Christ, and God the Father, who raised him from the dead. He testifies that he was not chosen as an apostle by men and sent to preach, as there were some who, chosen by the apostles, were sent to strengthen the churches; nor like others, who were sent by the Jews to disturb the churches, whom he calls false apostles: but that he was sent by the Son of God, whom in this case he seems to deny to be man for this reason, because he gave precepts as God, not as man, and whom he chose, he chose by divine judgment. By this he signifies that he is a fit and firm preacher, since he whom God has chosen is certainly much better than he whom man has chosen. Nor, he says, through man, as those who were compelling these men to be circumcised supposed concerning Christ; but through the Lord Jesus Christ, that is, through him who is God and man. This he at once makes plain when he says: And God the Father, who raised him from the dead. By this saying he condemned two heresies, that of Manichaeus and that of Photinus; because Manichaeus denies that Christ is man, and does not deny that he was crucified; and Photinus does not grant that Christ is God, and yet does not dare to deny that he raised himself, that is, his body, from the dead: so that Manichaeus, when he does not deny the crucified, seems foolishly not to confess him man; and Photinus by a kind of impiety seems to deny that he is God, whom he confesses to have raised himself from the dead. For he reads that the Lord said: Destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up. But he was speaking of the temple of his body (John 2:19). Therefore he himself, raising himself from the dead, signifies that he raised his body from the dead; and nonetheless he is said to have been raised by his Father, because all the power of the Son is from the Father; so that the deeds of the Son are the acts of the Father, from whom are all things.

(Gal. 1:2) And all the brothers who are with me, to the churches of Galatia. After commending his authority, he adds still more to the case, so as to show that the others who were with him were all stirred against them. Although his authority would have sufficed, so that it could not be refuted, yet to make their deed weigh more heavily, by which they had departed from their first faith, he indicates that many with him were inflamed to rebuke their error. For anyone will easily be able to understand that he errs, if he sees himself reproved by many.

(Gal. 1:3) Grace to you and peace from God our Father, and the Lord Jesus Christ. He shows that the human race has been lifted up by the kindness of both, of the Father as much as of the Son. Nor does he signify that the Son is less when he calls him our Lord, nor the Father greater because he calls him our God; for the Father will not be true God unless he is also Lord, nor the Son true Lord unless he is also God. If then the grace of the Father and of the Son toward us is one, why is it, he says, that, thinking less of the Son, you make the Law equal to him, so as to serve partly Christ, partly the Law, when you have read the Lord saying: For the Son of man is Lord even of the sabbath (Luke 6:5)? so that by this they may see how great an insult is inflicted on the Lord by them, when they compare the Lord with a handmaid.

(Gal. 1:4) Who gave himself for our sins. He shows openly that the Law is of no profit, when he says that Christ offered himself to suffer for us, to justify us, whom the Law held guilty; so that, rescued from the Law through faith in Christ, we should no longer be sinners, but, justified by a second birth, should be called sons of God. For when the human race was held in the dominion of the devil, the Savior offered himself to the devil, who was willing, so that, mocking him by the power of his might (for he wished to receive one whom he could not hold), he might take away from him those whom he held liable by an evil right. And so, having despoiled the underworld, he carried a trophy of souls to the Father, from whom are all things; which the Law certainly could not do. Although it was given to be of profit, yet when we sinned it was turned so as to become hostile; and it began to kill, not to give life. But Christ, forgiving offenses, not only gave us life but also joined us to himself, so that we are called sons of God, having been made so through faith. How great an error it is, then, to return under the Law after grace, that is, after receiving liberty to wish to be a slave; as if a man rescued from the sword, forgetful of the kindness, should sell himself again to the gladiator-trainer! For he who leaves his buyer and goes back again to his former master is his buyer's accuser. That he might deliver us from the present evil age. He says this because the Lord yielded himself to death for this reason, that he might deliver us from the error of the world, that is, that, made strangers to the understanding of this world, which, as its age grows old in its courses, declines toward its end, we might be partakers of the age to come, which knows no setting, in the sense of the one God. For this is the evil of the present age, that it does not render fitting reverence to God the Creator and to Christ the Restorer; while human error invents many gods for itself, or while the honor due to Christ is not kept for him by believers.

(Gal. 1:5) According to the will of God and our Father, to whom be glory for ages of ages. Amen. So that what he says, that Christ delivered us, may be firm and full of authority, he adds that he did this with the will of the Father.

(Gal. 1:6) I marvel that you are so quickly being transferred from him who called you through the grace of Christ into another Gospel. Gospel means good news, and by this the old law too is Gospel, except for the Law of works; but rather this one, in which what was promised has been fulfilled, through which sinners are called together to forgiveness. He says, then, that he marvels for this reason, because, falling away from the best and greatest kindness, they had made themselves over to the Law of works, so as to lose what they had obtained, so as to rush from the liberty they had received into slavery, quickly overthrown: whereas the custom is that one passes little by little from evil to good, but these were easily led over from good to the works of the flesh, which are heavy. For it rarely happens that someone flees from gladness to ruin; and by this he wonders at the dullness of those who, placed in security, subjected themselves to empty labor. It is as if a mad sailor, standing in harbor, safe from the storm, should let his ship loose onto the deep, to be tossed by various gales: so these too through folly subjected themselves, so as to suffer the pains of their cut shame and to take up the yoke of the slavery of the Law, though no hope had been preached for this thing. They had been called, then, into another gospel than that which these men preached who had subverted them; yet not another than that which the apostles announced, which these false apostles asserted was another, to bring odium on the Apostle who had instructed them. And to circumvent the Galatians by this deceit, it was necessary for them to say that the Apostle's Gospel was other than what the Savior's reasoning taught, but that this one was true which they preached in concord with the old law. These are those who raised questions with Paul and Barnabas about this matter, as is contained in the Acts of the Apostles (Acts 15:2); for they taught the believers from the Gentiles that unless they were circumcised after the custom of Moses, and judaized, they could not be saved.

(Gal. 1:7) Which is not another, except that there are some who trouble you and wish to convert the Gospel of Christ. It is plain that they were acting so as to convert the Gospel of Christ by changing the traditions, so that under the name of Christ they might be Jews: and this is to convert the Gospel of Christ into the Law.

(Gal. 1:8–9) But even if you, or an angel from heaven, should preach a gospel to you other than what we have preached, let him be anathema; as we have said before, and now I say again: If anyone preaches a gospel to you other than what you have received, let him be anathema. Let no one wonder that the Apostle, who used to tame savagery of manners, is so stirred; for it is for the salvation of the Galatians that he is indignant against the enemies of Christian teaching. For this indignation shows that it is no light sin to turn to the Law after receiving the faith: for he asserts that the Gospel which he had preached to them is so firm and true that he taught that even they themselves, that is, the apostles, should not be heard, should they perhaps be changed and preach otherwise, whose fame, that they were apostles of Christ, had certainly spread through every place. Or if perhaps the devil, feigning himself an angel of God so that he could easily be heard, should appear from heaven preaching against these things, he would be known to be an adversary, and would be held an abomination. If then he ordered that the apostles of Christ, whose reputation for working signs and wonders was so illustrious, and an Angel from heaven, whom a spiritual nature could commend, should be anathematized if they taught otherwise than they had been taught by the apostle Paul, how much more those whom no testimony can commend for leading astray? And he repeats the solemn warning for this reason, to confirm them in the first tradition; for a law repeated makes the negligent more careful.

(Gal. 1:10) For do I now satisfy men, or God? that is, do I satisfy men by persuading you to return to the grace of Christ, or God? Not men, certainly, but God; so that through satisfaction they may return to God, whom they had spurned. Then he immediately added: Or do I seek to please men? If I were still pleasing men, I would not be a servant of Christ. He signifies therefore that those by whom they had been overthrown were pleasing not God but men in subverting the Galatians, as he himself also had done long ago, when he persecuted the Church to vindicate the tradition of the Fathers. For, so that they might be praised by the Jews, they said, according to their will, that the Law must be kept, lest, if they preached the true Gospel of Christ, they should make them their enemies. But this apostle Paul, who taught that one should please God with all devotion, did not fear to offend men, provided he commended the glory of God; for, putting all present things after those to come, he commended himself to hopes, as a fit servant of Christ: but those cannot be fit servants who under the name of the Lord pursue their own gains. But perhaps someone may object: Then those who preached the Law, which God is said to have given, were serving men. How then is the Law said to be fit, whose preaching is related to have been tempered according to the wills of men? Before the coming of the Lord the Law was preached according to the will of God; for its time was owed to it, that it should be kept: but when the Lord promised in the Law came, the Law had to cease, according to the foretold judgment of the prophets. Hence the Lord says: The Law and the prophets until John (Luke 16:16); for it was not fitting that, with the Lord present, the authority of the Law should be kept. And therefore if anyone henceforth thinks that the Law is to be kept, he no longer obeys the will of the Lord, but resists it; for he satisfies those like himself.

(Gal. 1:11–12) But I make known to you, brothers, the Gospel that was preached by me, that it is not according to man; for neither did I receive it from man, or learn it, but through the revelation of Jesus Christ. Now he lays open the principles of the epistle; for he wishes to make the very sense plain, that what he taught, he had not learned from man, lest perhaps there should be doubt about what he taught. Nor had his preaching been fitted to human judgment, so as to seem unworthy of divine authority: but he says that he learned it from the Son of God through revelation, when he was already in heaven after the resurrection, as I mentioned before; and that what he learned agrees with the will and majesty of him from whom he testifies that he learned it. For those things are spiritual which, when kept, lay up merit with God: for those things are carnal which, kept in their time, procured neither merit nor demerit with God; not kept, they did harm; because both those who had not done the Law were under the curse, and those who had done it were not justified with God, since the righteous lives by faith. For those things were commanded to be kept so as to be a burden, not for dignity.

(Gal. 1:13) For you have heard of my conduct formerly in Judaism, that beyond measure I persecuted the Church of God and fought against it, and I advanced in Judaism beyond many of my contemporaries in my race, being more abundantly a zealot of my fathers' traditions. He briefly recalls his purpose, in which he formerly flourished according to the flesh, opposing the Church; but he says that he left this in order to be a servant of Christ. For he indicates that he was more devoted than they in vindicating the Law; not, however, for the favor of men, but thinking this useful with God. For through ignorance, while he pleased men, he thought that he was serving God, having a free conscience; for he did not know that it was no longer the time for asserting the Law, following the doctrine of fathers placed either in error or in ill will; and with all diligence and fervor of mind he strove to seem more eminent than the others of his own age in zeal for the Law, raging so against the teaching of Christ that he spared no man who had obtained the covenant of faith. But when he had learned by God's revelation that this labor was empty, and rather contrary, he recalls that he desisted from it and submitted himself to the faith of grace, so that it might be advancement for him with God. If then this man, who is an Israelite, and who was supported by the dignity of a teacher among the Jews, instructed by Gamaliel, a most prudent man, holding a place with men of the best life, left the Law, how much more ought those who were outside the Law not to have subjected themselves to the Law? And if he rejoices that he has come out from under the yoke of slavery, why is it that they have bound their liberty with the chains of the Law?

(Gal. 1:15–16) But when it pleased him who set me apart from my mother's womb, and called me through his grace, to reveal his Son in me, that I might preach him among the Gentiles, immediately I did not acquiesce in flesh and blood. Since he had confidence in the Law, unaware that it was no longer the time for keeping the Law, and was striving with all eagerness, thinking that he was doing this by God's will, to resist the Gospel of Christ, God, seeing that his zeal was good but that knowledge was lacking to him, was pleased to call him to his grace, knowing that he was now fit to preach the Gospel of his Son among the Gentiles; because if in a very small matter the boldness of his conscience was so swift and faithful, not through flattery of anyone, how much more constant would he be in preaching the gift of God, in hope of the promised reward? By God's foreknowledge, then, he was set apart from his mother's womb, that is, before he was born, or as soon as he was born, God knew that he would be good, as he says to the prophet Jeremiah: Before I formed you in the womb, I knew you, etc. (Jer. 1:5): so too the apostle Paul: knowing what he would be, he went before him, so as to call him before he was converted, because he was necessary. Then he at once took up the dispensation of God's gift, putting aside every carnal care; for he did not seek anyone's counsel, or refer to anyone what he should do, but forthwith preached Christ, that he is the Son of God. For as those who will be bad are often good before, so too those who will be good are usually bad first.

(Gal. 1:17) Nor did I come to Jerusalem to my predecessors the apostles, but I went away into Arabia, and again I returned to Damascus. He says that, having been chosen by God, he was under no necessity of going to his predecessors the apostles, so as perhaps to learn something from them; because God revealed to him through his Son how he should teach. He says this to show that nothing else is to be held than what he teaches, and that men are not to be believed, but God, from whom he testifies that he learned; and by this that those who had led them into the Law were false teachers, because what they taught had not been handed down by God. From Damascus, then, he set out into Arabia, to preach, where none of the apostles was, so that he himself might found churches there, lest, with false apostles creeping in, Judaism should be sown. And from there he again returned to Damascus, to visit those to whom he had preached the Gospel of the grace of God as a novice.

(Gal. 1:18) Then after three years I went up to Jerusalem to see Peter, and I stayed with him fifteen days. It was fitting that he should desire to see Peter, because he was first among the apostles, he to whom the Savior had delegated the care of the churches: not, certainly, to learn anything from him, because he had already learned from the author from whom Peter himself too had been instructed, but out of affection for the apostleship, and so that Peter might know that this license had been given to him which he himself too had received. Coming to him, then, he was received with hospitality, and stayed with him fifteen days, as a fellow apostle of one mind. He makes these things known for this reason, to show that he had concord with the apostles and had dissented in nothing, as the false apostles whispered about him, so as to disfigure him before the churches of the Gentiles and to insinuate their own evils into the simpler, saying that these agreed with the doctrine of the greater apostles.

(Gal. 1:19) Again I returned to Damascus, but I saw none other of the apostles, except James the brother of the Lord. He saw James at Jerusalem, because he had been appointed bishop there by the apostles; he too had formerly been an unbeliever, as the Evangelist says: For not even his brothers, he says, yet believed in him (John 7:5). This James was the son of Joseph, and was called the brother of the Lord for this reason, because Joseph his father was also named the father of the Lord. For so Mary says to Jesus in Luke's Gospel. What have you done to us, son? I and your father were seeking you, sorrowful and sad (Luke 2:48). And in John's Gospel Philip says to Nathanael: Him of whom Moses wrote in the Law, and the prophets, we have found, Jesus from Nazareth, the son of Joseph (John 1:45). In this way, then, the Lord was called the brother of James and of the others, in which he was also named the son of Joseph. For some, led by madness, contend with impious assertion that these were the true brothers of the Lord, born of Mary, while they say that Joseph was called his father without being his true father. For if these were truly his brothers, Joseph will also be his true father; because the one who called Joseph his father also called James and the others his brothers.

(Gal. 1:20) But what I write to you, behold, before God, I do not lie. Being about to speak of what pertains to his commendation, he gives God as witness that the things he is about to say are so, lest perhaps it should seem made up; since it does not befit his person to feign anything. But when he commends himself, he wishes these men to profit; for this commendation profits them, so that they may know that the things said by him are most true.

(Gal. 1:21–24) Then I came into the regions of Syria and Cilicia. But I was unknown by face to the churches of Judea which were in Christ: they only heard that he who once persecuted us now preaches the faith which he once fought against: and they magnified God in me. He says this because, when he was defending Judaism against the faith, like these men by whom the Galatians had been overthrown, he was chosen by God and taught to dissociate himself from Judaism and to preach the new Law: so that those of the Jews who believed, whom he had formerly persecuted, rejoiced in him, giving thanks to God, who made a defender out of a persecutor; so that the Galatians might accordingly know that they had been circumvented, so as to profess themselves Jews under the name of Christ, circumcising the flesh of their foreskin, whereas the law of Christ commands something other than Judaism does; for there is circumcision of the flesh, here of the heart; there one serves under the elements, the new moons and the sabbath, here God alone in the spirit, because one believes with the heart; there the condition is the cause of sin, here liberty from the remission of sins. And so the distance is great.

Chapter II

(Gal. 2:1–2) Then after fourteen years I went up again to Jerusalem with Barnabas, taking Titus also. And I went up according to a revelation; and I set forth to them the Gospel which I preach among the Gentiles, but privately, to those who seemed to be something, lest perhaps I should run, or had run, in vain. Since for a long time his fame had been growing among all the Jews, but he was not seen face to face (for he was going about to found and strengthen the Church among the Gentiles with his usual constancy), and among the Jews an evil opinion of him arose on account of the Law, as if he disagreed with the preaching of the other apostles; and from this a scruple arose for many, so that the Gentiles could be disturbed, lest they were being led by him into something other than what the apostles who had been with the Lord handed down. For on this very occasion the Galatians were subverted by the Jews, who said that Peter handed down one thing and Paul another; hence it came about that, admonished by a revelation of the Lord, he went up to Jerusalem with witnesses of his preaching, that is, Barnabas and Titus, the one from the Jews and the other from the Gentiles, so that if any had a stumbling block concerning him, it might be removed by their testimony, as he opened to them the sense of his preaching. With the apostles, however, he conferred privately, so that they might know that he did not disagree with the rule of the Gospel, that is, so that they might not think that he was running or had run in vain, as some of the Jewish believers supposed. For he could not learn anything from them, since he had been instructed by God; but it was done for the sake of concord and peace at God's bidding, so that the scruple or suspicion might be removed for his brothers and fellow apostles, and so that it might profit the Gentiles, when they recognized that his Gospel agreed with the apostles. Moreover, when they had given a law that those who believed from among the Gentiles should not be troubled, but that they should keep themselves only from these things, that is, from blood and fornication and idolatry (Acts 21:25). Now let the sophists of the Greeks, who claim expertise for themselves and that they naturally excel in subtlety of mind, tell us: what things were handed down to the Gentiles to be observed? Things they did not know, or things they knew? But how can it be that someone learns the things he knows? Therefore those things were shown to the Gentiles to be unlawful which they thought lawful; and accordingly they were certainly not forbidden murder when they are commanded to keep from blood. But they received this, which Noah had learned from God, that they should keep themselves from eating blood with flesh (Gen. 9:4). For how could it be that men steeped in the Roman laws, whose authority in keeping commandments is so great, did not know that murder must not be committed, since indeed the Roman laws punish adulterers and murderers, and false witnesses, and thieves, and sorcerers, and the perpetrators of all other evils? Finally, these three commandments are found to have been given by the apostles and elders, of which the Roman laws know nothing, that is, that they should abstain from idolatry, and from blood, as Noah did, and from fornication. The sophists of the Greeks, not understanding these things, yet knowing that one must abstain from blood, adulterated the Scripture, adding a fourth commandment, that one must also keep from what is strangled; which, I think, they will now understand at God's bidding, since what they added had already been said above.

(Gal. 2:3) But neither Titus, who was with me, (that is, at that very time when I was, he says, in Jerusalem, Titus, who was with me) was compelled to be circumcised. Although we were indeed among the apostles and those who had believed from among the Jews, none of these compelled him, that is, Titus, to be circumcised. And it is understood: Why is it that you are being circumcised, when Titus was not compelled by the apostles, who seemed to be something, to be circumcised, but was received uncircumcised?

(Gal. 2:4–5) But because of false brothers brought in secretly, who slipped in to spy out our liberty, which we have in Christ Jesus, that they might subject us to slavery, for an hour we yielded to subjection, that the truth of the Gospel might remain with you. He denies that Titus was circumcised, because he was a Greek like the Galatians. By this statement he rebukes those who allowed themselves to be circumcised though they were Greeks. But lest perhaps it be objected to him that he himself had circumcised Timothy, he now sets out the reasons why he did what he said should not be done, saying: But because of false brothers brought in secretly, who slipped in to spy out our liberty, which we have in Christ Jesus, that they might subject us to slavery, for an hour we yielded to subjection, that the truth of the Gospel might remain with you (Acts 16:3). The Greeks say the contrary: "Not even for an hour did we yield", and they say that this fits the case; because, they say, one who forbade it to be done ought not to say that he had done it, lest he prove that it should be done. But the Apostle, a divine man, knowing that this could be objected, himself puts it forward, to forestall the slanderers; and he gives the reasons by which he was compelled to do what he did not wish. But because of false brothers, he says, brought in secretly. He says brought in secretly because they entered with guile, presenting themselves as brothers when they were enemies. Who slipped in, that is, who entered with humility, feigning friendship. To spy out our liberty, which we have in Christ Jesus, that they might subject us to slavery. To spy out is to enter in such a way that one feigns one thing and seeks another. So that they might accuse our liberty, which we have in Christ Jesus. This is the liberty in Christ Jesus: not to be subject to the Law. That they might subject us to slavery: that is, they entered with guile and deceit for this purpose, to subject our liberty to slavery, compelling us to be subject to the law of circumcision. For an hour we yielded to subjection; that is, for an hour we submitted ourselves to slavery, humbling ourselves to the Law, so that, Timothy having been circumcised, the guile and the stumbling block of the Jews might cease. For they were ready, as is given to be understood, to stir up a tumult and sedition against him. For there was a cause by which they might have an opening for making a slanderous charge: for Timothy was of a Jewish mother but of a Greek father, whence it came about that as an infant he had not been circumcised according to the Law. But the Apostle, wishing to take him on in order to ordain him bishop, as he did (for all of that place, he says (Acts 16:2), bore him good witness), suffered the plots of the Jews. For they were spying out whether he would receive the uncircumcised son of a Jewish woman, preparing sedition; because, if he argued concerning the Greeks that they were not to be circumcised, just as a letter had also been given by the apostles on this matter (Acts 15:23 ff.), yet he would not forbid the sons of Israel to be circumcised, since the apostles had indicated nothing about them in that same letter; on whose authority the believing Jews raged all the more, because the letter itself did not forbid the Jews to circumcise their sons. Then, he says (Acts 16:3), taking him he circumcised him because of the Jews who were in those places. But concerning the Gentiles they took no offense, whence Titus was not compelled to be circumcised. For an hour, therefore, he yielded, that the truth of the Gospel might remain with the Gentiles, since they do not subject themselves to circumcision. But with the Jews the truth of the Gospel was not, because as believers they circumcised their sons; for in Christ Jesus neither uncircumcision nor circumcision avails anything, but faith working through love (Gal. 5:6). For not only the history but also the wording indicates this, that he yielded. For above he says that Titus, though he was a Greek, was not compelled to be circumcised; and he adds, saying: But because of false brothers brought in secretly. What does this now mean, except that he yielded for an hour because of false brothers brought in secretly? If not, how would the words or the sense follow? He certainly denies that Titus was compelled to be circumcised: but "because of false brothers brought in secretly", what does it mean, except that he yielded? But if because of false brothers brought in secretly he did not yield, then it was not by his will that he did not yield; for he indicated that he would have yielded, if this is so, but that because of false brothers brought in secretly he did not yield. If therefore there was no need to yield, why are they called false brothers, by whose kindness he did not do what was not to be done? So, if this is so, they did the Apostle a kindness, since because of them he did not yield what he had to yield. Why then were they called spies, if they did not want him to do this which he was going to do, but because of them he did not do it? But if he was not going to do it, without doubt he did it because of them: for it is nothing else. For the wording indicates this, that he yielded, and the history cries out the deed. For how could he deny that he yielded for an hour, when it is proved that he circumcised Timothy because of the Jews, which is to have yielded for an hour, and that, purified according to the Law, he went up into the temple (Acts 21)? For that man could say that he had not yielded even for an hour who was never found to have yielded. For either, being about to yield, he did not yield because of the false brothers; or, not being about to yield, he yielded because of the false brothers. So accept one of the two. But those are called false brothers who, as believers, judaized.

(Gal. 2:6) But from those who are reckoned to be something, what they once were matters nothing to me; God does not accept the person of a man. This signifies that the apostles were formerly simple men and unskilled, of no rank in the Law, but that this was no hindrance. For who would accuse one whom God excuses? For the question is not who was what, but what he is. For past things neither condemn nor vindicate. But yet he wishes it to be understood that he was both skilled in the Law and distinguished in life, and accordingly also an outstanding minister in the dispensation of the Gospel. For those who are reckoned to be something conferred nothing on me. He says that he obtained nothing from the apostles, but from God; because he who gave the unskilled the sense of Christian teaching, he himself, he says, deigned to impart to me too, when I was skilled in the law, the sense of a doctrine of this kind. How then could it be that this man should learn from them, whom the grace of Christ, finding him skilled, made more skilled?

(Gal. 2:7–8) But on the contrary, when they had seen that the Gospel of the uncircumcision was entrusted to me, as that of the circumcision to Peter; for he who worked in Peter for the apostleship of the circumcision worked also in me toward the Gentiles. He names Peter alone and compares him with himself, because he had received the primacy for founding the Church; and he says that he himself was likewise chosen, so that he might have the primacy in founding the churches of the Gentiles; yet in such a way that Peter too would preach to the Gentiles, if there were cause, and Paul to the Jews. For each is found to have done both; but yet full authority is recognized to have been given to Peter in the preaching to Judaism, and Paul's perfect authority is found in the preaching to the Gentiles. Hence he also calls himself the teacher of the Gentiles in faith and truth (2 Tim. 1:11); for each one was allotted a dispensation according to his strength. For it was more difficult to draw and persuade to their faith those who were far from God than those who were near.

(Gal. 2:9–10) And knowing the grace of God which was given to me, Peter and James and John, who seemed to be pillars, gave to me and to Barnabas the right hands of communion, that we should go to the Gentiles, but they to the circumcision; only that we should be mindful of the poor, which very thing I also took care diligently to do. By the apostles who were more illustrious among the rest, whom he also calls pillars on account of their firmness, and who had always been with the Lord in his secret moments, worthy to behold his glory on the mountain (Matt. 17:2): by these, therefore, he says, the gift which he received from God was approved, so that he was worthy to have the primacy in the preaching to the Gentiles, just as Peter also had it in the preaching to the circumcision. And just as he gives Peter companions, eminent men among the apostles, so too he joins to himself Barnabas, who was joined to him by divine judgment; yet he claims the grace of the primacy as granted by God to himself alone, just as it was also granted to Peter alone among the apostles (Acts 13:2); so that the apostles of the circumcision extended their right hands to the apostles of the Gentiles to show the concord of their fellowship, so that both, knowing that they had obtained from the Lord the spirit of perfection in the dispensation of the Gospel, might show that in nothing did they pick and choose between one another. This alone, however, he says they agreed among themselves: that, in what they had not been instructed by a precept or gift of the Lord, because it was easy to learn this from the Law, they should command those to whom they preached to be mindful of the poor, so that they might cultivate the mercy through which they were redeemed by God. For this reason, therefore, he makes known these things which took place between himself and the apostles, so that the Galatians may know that what they had received from him is true and approved.

(Gal. 2:11) But when Peter had come to Antioch, I resisted him to his face, because he had been reproved. After the concord of fellowship and the honoring of the primacy, which by the grace of God they conferred on one another, now, a cause of negligence or error intervening, the apostles seem to disagree among themselves, not in their own cause but in their care for the Church. To his face, he says, I resisted him. What is this, except: I contradicted him in his presence? And he added the cause, saying: Because he had been reproved. Blameworthy, certainly, by the truth of the Gospel, to which this deed was opposed. For which of them would dare to resist Peter, the first apostle, to whom the Lord gave the keys of the kingdom of heaven, unless another such, who, knowing through confidence in his own election that he was not unequal, steadfastly disapproved what the other had done without counsel?

(Gal. 2:12–13) For before certain men came from James, he ate with the Gentiles; but when they had come, he withdrew and separated himself, fearing those who were of the circumcision. And the rest of the Jews also dissembled with him, so that even Barnabas consented to their dissimulation. All these Jews who consented to the dissimulation of Peter and Barnabas were of good faith; but because of the stumbling block of those who came from James (for they were zealots of the Law, who would venerate both the Law and Christ with equal right, which is contrary to the teaching of faith), in their presence they did not mingle with the Gentiles. For they feared the onslaught of the audacity of those who were zealous for the Law. And if that alone had been so, it was not blameworthy; but in what the error lay, he shows in what follows. For he himself too certainly yielded to the animosity and audacity of the Jews, fearing lest, through something that is easy, a stumbling block might creep in that would be hard to calm; for he both purified himself according to the Law under compulsion (Acts 21:26), and circumcised Timothy unwillingly (Acts 16:3).

(Gal. 2:14) But when I saw that they were not walking by a straight way toward the truth of the Gospel, I said to Peter before all. He inveighs against Peter alone for this reason, that in him who is first, the rest might learn. If you, though you are a Jew, live like a Gentile, how do you compel the Gentiles to judaize? Now he discloses the cause of the reproof; for there would have been no error if, in the presence of the Jews, he had held himself aloof from the Gentiles, fearing a stumbling block; but here is the whole cause of the reproof: that when the Jews came from James, he not only separated himself from those with whom he had lived like a Gentile, but also compelled them to judaize, on account of his fear of those men, so that the Gentiles did not know which of these things was true. For they knew that he himself had lived with them not as a Jew; but afterwards, hearing from him that the manner of the Jews was to be followed, they certainly hesitated as to what was true. For if this was true, that they should judaize, they held him a transgressor, because he had lived like a Gentile; but if that was better and true, that they should live simply, it was the overthrow of the Gentiles through his cowardice, since, the good having been taken from them, a laborious and ineffectual thing was being handed to them. But the Apostle Paul, when he yielded for an hour, did not also urge this; rather he cried out that he was doing a superfluous and empty thing, because of the fury of the Jews. And to this thing indeed he would not have succumbed, had there not been a cause by which the audacity of very many Jews was vaunting itself. Now Timothy was the son of a Jewish woman, but of a Greek father (Acts 16:1); whence it came about that as an infant he was not circumcised according to the Law. They were lying in wait, therefore, spying out whether he would take on uncircumcised one who had been born a Jew, which they thought unlawful for the race of the Jews, seeking an occasion by which they might hold him an overthrower of the Law; for this reason he yielded for an hour to their fury.

(Gal. 2:15–16) We are Jews by nature, and not sinners from the Gentiles. But knowing that a man is not justified by the works of the Law, except through the faith of Jesus Christ, we too believe in Christ Jesus, that we may be justified by the faith of Christ, and not by the works of the Law. By a manifest argument he shows that they had been deceived into keeping the Law, in which no one is justified before God, since those very men who are Jews by nature, wishing to be justified, leave the Law and flee for refuge to the faith of Christ. Since by the works of the Law no flesh is justified. He says that no man has been justified by the works of the Law; for whoever are righteous have been justified by faith, as Abraham and Isaac and Jacob and the other saints.

(Gal. 2:17) But if, seeking to be justified in Christ, we ourselves too are found sinners, is Christ then the minister of sin? Far from it! How can Christ be the minister of sin, who forgives sins? Therefore, Far from it, he says. But if, wishing to be justified through the faith of Christ, we serve the Law, we profess that we are under sin; because faith draws a man out from the Law, that he may be righteous. Therefore, if we give ourselves to the Law, we are found sinners; because those who are under the Law are under a curse.

(Gal. 2:18) For if I build up again the things which I destroyed, I make myself a transgressor. It is plain that one who comes to Christ leaves the Law; for from a slave he becomes free. But if he returns again to the Law, he becomes his own accuser, because he condemns his own act.

(Gal. 2:19) For I through the Law have died to the Law, that I may live to God. He says this because through the law of faith he died to the law of Moses; for he dies to the law who is set free from it; and he lives to God, whose servant he becomes, bought by Christ. I have been fixed to the cross with Christ. He is fixed to the cross with Christ who, emulous of his footsteps, is captured by no desire of the world, so that, living to God, he may be seen to be dead to the world. For whoever does not do the things that are of the world, or of error, is dead to the world.

(Gal. 2:20) But I live, no longer I, but Christ lives, who is in me. It is not doubtful that in him who is set free from death through faith, Christ lives; for while he forgives the sins of one worthy of death, he himself lives in him; for by his protection he has been rescued from death. But that I now live in the flesh, I live in the faith of the Son of God, who loved me and gave himself up for me. I am not ungrateful to the grace of God. Since the future life has been promised to Christians, therefore he who now in this life lives fortified by the help of God lives in faith in the promised life. For he contemplates its image, as having a pledge of the future life, which has been obtained for us at God's bidding through the love of Christ. He, therefore, is not ungrateful to Christ who perseveres in his faith, knowing that he has a benefit from no one but him, and that it serves to insult him if he compares to him another, whose help he could not have.

(Gal. 2:21) For if righteousness is through the Law, then Christ died for nothing. Nothing is so clear: because if a man could have been justified through the Law, Christ need not have died. But because the Law could not give the remission of sins, nor, triumphing over the second death, despoil it of the captives whom it held on account of sin, therefore Christ died, so that he might provide those things which the Law could not; and accordingly he did not die for nothing; for his death is the justification of sinners.

Chapter III

(Gal. 3:1) O foolish Galatians! who has bewitched you? These are the words of one who is angry and amazed; for they had been so depraved that they did not even recognize that they had been deceived. He has used a preamble; for everyone who is bewitched passes from good into evil, just as these too passed from freedom and security to slavery and anxiety. Before whose eyes Christ Jesus was proscribed, and crucified in you. It is manifest that to the eyes of the foolish Christ seems proscribed, that is, despoiled or condemned; but to the eyes and senses of the prudent he seems not only not condemned, but rather to have himself condemned death by his cross; for the passion of the Lord is our life, and the destruction of death. But because these, having been transferred to the Law, had begun to think unworthily, he therefore complains that to their minds Christ seems condemned; and so he added: Crucified in you. That is, in your understanding he has been crucified, as a man; for he in whom hope is not full is a man. These therefore did not think that full salvation was in Christ, because they made the Law equal to him. But in those who rightly understand Christ, Christ lives in them; in Photinus he has been crucified. For that man is truly dead in whom Christ is said to be crucified; because this is brought against him by way of accusation.

(Gal. 3:2) This only I wish to learn from you: Did you receive the Spirit from the works of the Law, or from the hearing of faith? Are you so without understanding? He still speaks with indignation; for he grieves greatly over their error, because he saw that his building, with which he had built them up through the Gospel, had been destroyed. He was not so moved about the Corinthians, because, although he calls them carnal (1 Cor. 3:2), they had nevertheless not been so far taken in mind as to allow themselves to be circumcised. Therefore these are called both foolish and without understanding, because they were not able to take thought even for the pain of their own flesh. For one is usually induced only so far as what delights, or what does not cause pain. These are therefore the words of one who questions, and what he asks is on the side of one who affirms, when he says: This only I wish to learn from you: Did you receive the Spirit from the works of the Law, or from the hearing of faith? He asks them this, a thing in which they both gloried and which they could not deny, that they had received the Holy Spirit through faith, not through the Law; so that, coming to their senses, they might recognize that the gift of God in which they gloried they had received not from the work of the Law but by believing in Christ; so that, being grateful to Christ at least in this way, they might abandon the Law, which they had learned profited them in nothing. He puts forward this, which at that time could not be denied, that the Spirit of God was in believers; for to commend the rudiments of faith, this gift of God was made manifest to men; as happened at the very beginnings in the apostles and the other disciples, and also in those twelve whom (because in an adulterous baptism under the name of the baptism of John they had been not dipped but defiled) the apostle Paul commanded to be baptized in the name of the Trinity; and when hands had been laid on them, the Holy Spirit fell upon them, and supplied the speech of diverse tongues together with their interpretation; so that no one should dare to deny that the Holy Spirit was in them. For this reason he calls the Galatians without understanding, who, being acquainted with this matter and aware of it, degrading themselves, subjected themselves to the care of the flesh.

(Gal. 3:3) Having begun in the spirit, are you now being finished in the flesh? He says this because, while the ascent is from lower things to the heights of faith, these on the contrary have slipped down from the heights into the depths. For having been made spiritual through faith, when they turned to the Law they had lost the grace of the Spirit which they had obtained; for being finished signifies perdition, that is, of faith.

(Gal. 3:4–5) You have suffered so much without cause, if indeed without cause. He signifies that they had suffered much for the faith from unbelievers; for at that time those who believed were a reproach to the rest, whether to those of their own household or to strangers: for they were pointed at with the finger as if guilty of treason. Hence the Galatians likewise suffered many things, having met with people more frenzied, and the merit of this they had lost by putting themselves under the Law. But because he added: If indeed without cause, he shows that he hopes they can be reformed. He then who supplies the Spirit to you, and works with power in you, is it from the works of the Law, or from the hearing of faith? It is the same sense, to which are added the marks of power, which bear witness to faith, that its hope is trustworthy and reasonable. But no power accompanies the Law to draw men to hope in it; because the things that are seen are not hoped for, but are temporal, like the law of works, which we have shown above.

(Gal. 3:6) As it is written (Gen. 15:6): Abraham believed God, and it was reckoned to him as righteousness. By the example of Abraham, who is the father of faith, he shows that righteousness is imputed not from the works of the Law but through faith; for by believing he was justified: and therefore that they had foolishly and improvidently subjected themselves to the Law, which was not given for justification.

(Gal. 3:7) Do you know therefore that those who are of faith, these are the sons of Abraham? It is true that believers are the sons of Abraham; because if Abraham was the first to be justified by faith, as many as believe after him are his sons, whether from the Jews or from the Gentiles.

(Gal. 3:8) But the Scripture, foreseeing that God justifies the Gentiles by faith, foretold to Abraham: That in you all nations shall be blessed (Gen. 12:3). He says this because Abraham is the pattern of all believers, so that those who believe are blessed in him; for by following his faith they become partakers of the same blessing.

(Gal. 3:9–10) So those who are of faith are blessed with faithful Abraham; for as many as are of the works of the Law are under a curse. It is manifest that the faithful are blessed with the faithful one, just as unbelievers are punished with unbelievers. For those who live under the Law are under a curse, because they are transgressors of the Law.

(Deut. 27:26) For it is written: Accursed is everyone who has not continued in all things that are written in the book of the Law, to do them. This is written in Deuteronomy, that if anyone had passed over anything of the Law, he would be held accursed. And the commandments are so many that it is impossible to keep them. Hence the apostle Peter too says in the Acts of the Apostles: Why do you put on the brothers a yoke which neither our fathers nor we have been able to bear (Acts 15:10)? For these things had been given to a negligent and proud people, so that they would have the Law throughout their whole manner of life.

(Gal. 3:12) But that no one is justified in the Law before God is manifest; because the righteous lives by faith (Hab. 2:4). And therefore he teaches that one must withdraw from the Law, lest the righteousness sought through faith be lost. But the Law is not of faith, but he who does them shall live in them (Ezek. 20:21). The Law is not of faith, because it commands nothing to be believed. For all its precepts are manifest, so that one who keeps the Law may live; if not, he must be punished, because he committed what he knew was to be avoided. It may be objected from the opposite side, and said: If no one is justified in the Law, why is it that he who passes over anything of the Law is cursed? It is true that no one is justified through the Law, but that is before God; he is, however, justified before the world, so that he may be secure in the present life. But if he wishes to be justified before God, so that it may profit him toward merit on the day of judgment, let him follow the faith of God; otherwise, though secure here, he will be guilty in the future. The Law therefore profits, but that which was written on the tablets; for the rest, as he said, were given to be a burden.

(Gal. 3:13) Christ redeemed us from the curse of the Law, having been made a curse for us. Since no one could fulfill the Law, all were guilty under the curse of the Law, so that it might take vengeance on them; but Christ, born a man, offered by the Father for us, redeemed us from the devil. For in offering him, willing, to death for those who were liable to the curse of the Law, he made him a curse in this way, just as in the Law the victim offered for sins was called sin: therefore, offered for the accursed, he was made a curse; for to the Jews too he was made a curse, since he is killed as a sinner. Therefore he did not say: Made for us accursed, but a curse; for he who was offered to death for his own sin became accursed, since he was dying in his own cause. And if you look more closely, you will see that Christ was made the curse of those by whom he was killed; for the cross of the Savior is the sin and the curse of the Jews. For it is written: Accursed is everyone who hangs on a tree (Deut. 21:23). Moses said this in Deuteronomy concerning those guilty of death: that it should suffice for those hanged on a tree to be there until evening, and then to be taken down for burial. For accursed is everyone, he says, who hangs on a tree; so that this should suffice for one guilty of death, that being hanged he died on a tree; lest, if he were hanged longer, his death should carry greater reproach than it ought, and a stain should come upon his family if his death were made public any further. And therefore the Savior, hanged on a tree though innocent, is not accursed, but the curse of those by whom he was hanged. Yet as far as concerns the Jews, offering him to Pilate to be killed as a sinner, they made him a curse, so that he might be killed as guilty though he was innocent, since one worthy of death is accursed according to the Law. For seeing many gathering to his teaching, they were jealous and said: Behold, the whole world has gone after him (John 12:19); and therefore he is said to have been killed for us, and for us in a twofold way; for when they think that he ought to be killed lest he should teach, they achieved nothing; because both his teaching grew, and he made death void in his passion. For descending to the underworld, he set the human race free; for which cause he also allowed himself to be killed, knowing that it was for us, and against the devil.

(Gal. 3:14) That the blessing of Abraham might come among the Gentiles through Christ Jesus: and that we might receive the blessing of the Spirit through faith. He says this because, the Jews having been reproached and cursed, since they flattered themselves on the righteousness of the Law, when the Savior had been killed as a violator of the sabbath, the blessing of the promise to Abraham passed over to the Gentiles through faith in Christ Jesus. Hence the Lord says to the Jews: The kingdom of God shall be taken from you, and shall be given to a nation producing its fruits (Matt. 21:43).

(Gal. 3:15) Brothers, I speak according to man. He says this because he wishes to commend the reason of the divine promise by the example of a human case. A man's testament, yet once confirmed, no one makes void, or adds to it. It is true that the testament of a deceased person, lawfully made, cannot be torn up.

(Gal. 3:16) To Abraham the promises were spoken, and to his seed. He does not say: And to seeds, as of many; but as of one: And to your seed, which is Christ. This is what is written in Genesis to Abraham: And in your seed all nations shall be blessed (Gen. 22:18). This was certainly fulfilled in Christ, in whose name all nations shall be blessed through faith. Therefore the promise was established not in many, but in one, who is Christ. By this argument of his the Apostle marks out as guilty, as it were, of falsifying a testament those who, believing in Christ, also hope for something from the Law; as if salvation were promised not only in Christ but also in the Law. Therefore he proves that the promise of blessing was made in Christ alone.

(Gal. 3:17) But this I say: the testament confirmed by God in Christ is not made weak, so as to make void the promise, by the Law which was made after four hundred and thirty years. He teaches that the promise of blessing was established in Christ alone, so as to show that the Law was given after four hundred and thirty years; for the promise having been established, the Law was given afterwards, which would certainly not weaken the promise, but would signify what was to come, and when it would come.

For if the inheritance is from the Law, it is no longer from the promise: but God granted it to Abraham through the promise. The Jews defend two causes contrary to each other; for in no way and by no reasoning can the Jews be persuaded that the promise to Abraham was made void through the Law, and rightly; but with improvident presumption they defend another thing opposed to it, thinking that justification cannot come about without the work of the Law; though they know that Abraham, who is the pattern of this thing, was justified without the works of the Law through faith alone. But blinded by a certain zeal, they neither understand nor consider that if justification is through the Law, the promise is excluded; and if the promise has been excluded, without doubt the faith of Abraham will be frustrated: which the Jews do not even allow themselves to hear, knowing that the promise to Abraham is from faith. This promise justifies through faith, not through the Law; just as Abraham too was justified by faith. Those therefore are heirs of the promise of Abraham who succeed him, taking up the faith in which Abraham was blessed and justified. The testimony of the promise to Abraham is therefore called a testament, so that after his death they might be heirs in the promise, having been made his sons through faith. This cause ran on up to Christ, who was promised to Abraham, so that those believing after the death of Christ might be heirs of the faith of Abraham through the testament of the blood of Christ. The gift therefore that was promised to Abraham has been paid out to his sons in Christ; although Abraham himself also obtained this gift there where he was found.

(Gal. 3:19) Why then was the Law of works laid down, until the seed should come to whom it was promised? The Law of works, that is, of Moses, was laid down in the middle between the promise once begun and the seed to whom the promise was made, that is, between Abraham and the Savior. It was given long after the promise was made for this reason, that by this very distinction the Law might be seen to be one thing and the promise another; that it might instruct the people of God, separated from the Gentiles, under the fear of God, so that it might become worthy to receive the promise, which is Christ. Yet it is called the Law of works because it teaches what things are to be done bodily, at God's command, concerning sacrifices, firstborn, tithes, and other such things. Ordained through angels in the hand of a Mediator. He calls angels the messengers of God, that is, Moses, and Joshua the son of Nun, and the other prophets up to John the Baptist, of whom God says: Behold, I send my angel before your face (Mal. 3:1). Through these, therefore, the Law, set in order, was ordained by God in the hand, that is, in the power, of the Savior; for he is the mediator or reconciler of God and men (1 Tim. 2:5), to save whom he wills of those who received the Law through the Angels mentioned above. For he himself says: As the Father raises the dead and gives life, so also the Son gives life to whom he wills (John 5:21).

(Gal. 3:20) But a mediator is not of one. Without doubt a mediator, that is, an arbiter, is not of one, but of two; for when two peoples were fighting against each other, always at variance and enemies through the diversity of their teaching, the Savior came as their arbiter, cutting off from both peoples that through which they were in discord, so that they might be at peace. He therefore took away from the Gentiles the number of gods and the service of the elements, which was a stumbling block to the Hebrews; he also took away from the Jews the works of the Law, that is, new moons, circumcision, and the observance of the sabbath, and the distinctions of foods, and the other things which the Gentiles abhorred; and so those who had formerly been enemies were made peaceable. If then these things are so, why is it that the Galatians, through dullness of mind, corrupt this arbitration, having turned to Judaism? For they bring the judgment of the Savior to nothing when, after peace has been made, as if repenting of it, they subject themselves to those things which had formerly been hostile to peace. According to the Galatians' understanding, therefore, Christ is not an arbiter; because, having been led into the Law, they have made one people out of two, and of one people certainly no one is the arbiter. He pursues this same sense to the Ephesians, saying: For he is our peace, who made both one, and breaking down the middle wall of partition, the enmity in his flesh, making void the law of commandments in decrees; that he might create the two in himself in one new man, making peace (Eph. 2:14). If he makes peace in one new man, he has certainly taken away from both that through which they were at variance; and by giving his own law he established for them that through which they might be at peace. This is what the Galatians broke through, having turned to Judaism: But God is one. He says this lest, by preaching Christ as God, whom he calls an arbiter for the one God, he should be thought to have preached against the Law and the prophets. He teaches, therefore, that this arbiter is to be understood through the assumption of flesh, as he says to Timothy: The arbiter of God and of men, the man Jesus Christ (1 Tim. 2:5); but that according to what he is as God, he is not separated from the Father, but is one with the Father. For whether Father or Son, God is one; for God is the name of a nature and of a power, which, as the very meaning of the names teaches, remains undivided in the Father and the Son.

(Gal. 3:21) Is the Law then against the promises of God? Far from it! Far from it indeed, because the Law is not hostile to the promise, since in fact it confirms it. For Moses first says: God will raise up for you a prophet from your brothers (Deut. 18:15). The apostle Peter proves in the Acts of the Apostles that this was said of Christ. For if a Law had been given which could give life, righteousness would truly be from the Law (Acts 3:22). He means this righteousness which is imputed as righteousness before God, that is, the righteousness of faith; because the Law too had righteousness, but for the present, not one that would justify before God. For it could not remit sins, so as to make righteous men out of sinners; but it was given for this, that it might be a terror, calling men to a good life and punishing the irreverent. And therefore the Law was not given such that it could give life, but such that it could condemn.

(Gal. 3:22) But the Scripture has shut up all things under sin, that the promise from the faith of Jesus Christ might be given to believers. He set down this sense also to the Romans, saying: God has shut up all things under sin, that he may have mercy on all (Rom. 11:32). He said this, which I mentioned above, that the Law was given for this purpose, that sinners might know themselves guilty before God. For when their sins had been made manifest, they were shut up, so that they could not excuse themselves, but should seek mercy; so that he who had been promised to Abraham, when he came, might ask of them faith alone, which Abraham had; that they might obtain both pardon for their evils and justification. For God had made this manifest through Isaiah the prophet, saying: He will come from Zion who will rescue and turn away ungodliness from Jacob; and this will be to them a testament from me, when I take away their sins (Isa. 59:20).

(Gal. 3:23) For before faith came, we were kept under the Law, shut up for that same faith which was beginning to be revealed. This is what he says: that before the Savior came we lived under the custody of the Law, lest we should sin, so that when the promise came we might be worthy. For by this hope the Law kept us safe, so as to offer us to Christ at his coming, that is, us Jews.

(Gal. 3:24) So the Law was our pedagogue in Christ Jesus, that we might be justified by faith. It is the same sense: that we were under the care of the Law until Christ came, who would justify us as believers.

(Gal. 3:25–26) But now that faith has come, we are no longer under a pedagogue; for you are all sons of God through faith, which is in Christ Jesus. He who lives under a pedagogue is not in his own power; for one who is small and prone to sin is kept under the care of a pedagogue. But when Christ came, who as it were makes those under age into those of full age, we were freed from the power of the pedagogue and through the washing away of sins were made sons of God, when we had been slaves of sin.

(Gal. 3:27) For as many of you as were baptized in Christ have put on Christ. He says this because believers, when they are changed, put on Christ; when they are called that which they believe.

(Gal. 3:28) There is neither Jew nor Greek: there is neither slave nor free: there is neither male nor female; for you are all one in Christ Jesus. He says that there is no distinction among believers, so that no one is prejudged by what he was before he believed; for to all of these one gift has been decreed. For with God there is no distinction of persons, except of conduct and life; so that men of one faith are distinguished by merits, not by persons.

(Gal. 3:29) If then you are all one in Christ Jesus, then you are the seed of Abraham, heirs according to the promise. All believers are the seed of Abraham, because Abraham is the father of all believers; and if they are sons, they are necessarily also heirs of his promise, so that they may obtain through faith what was promised to Abraham, so that we may be partakers of his justification in the kingdom of heaven.

Chapter IV

(Gal. 4:1–2) Now I say that as long as the heir is a little child, he differs in nothing from a slave, though he is lord of all; but he is under guardians and stewards until the time fixed beforehand by the father. It is true, for the fathers ordained the law and determined up to what age one who is grown must be subject; so that, as long as he is a little child, he cannot use his freedom, nor exercise lordship as an heir; but he is almost like slaves in his condition under guardians or stewards. By this example he proves that those who were under the Law were little children as regards merit, and did not have freedom; for they were subject to the condition of the Law because of sin.

(Gal. 4:3) So we too, when we were little children, were subject under the elements of this world. By the elements he signifies the new moons and the sabbath; for new moons are the lunar days which the Jews observe, while the sabbath is the day of rest. Therefore, before the promise came, that is, before the gift of the grace of God, purifying them, justified believers, we were subject to fellow slaves as to pedagogues, being as it were imperfect and little children through the Law of God; for our freedom was bound because of sin. Yet the Jews hoped for nothing from the elements, as the pagans did; rather, they venerated God the Creator in the creation.

(Gal. 4:4–5) But when the fullness of time came, God sent his Son, made of a woman, made under the Law, that he might redeem those who were under the Law, that we might receive the adoption of sons. The fullness of time is the completed time which had been fixed beforehand by God the Father, when he would send his Son, so that, made from a Virgin, he might be born as a man, subjecting himself to the Law until the time of his baptism; that he might give a pattern of how sinners, washed and rescued from the yoke of the Law by the condescension of God, might be adopted to him as sons, as he had promised to those redeemed by the blood of his Son. Now it was necessary for the Savior to be made under the Law, as a son of Abraham according to the flesh; so that, being circumcised, he might be shown to be the one who, promised to Abraham, was coming to justify the Gentiles through faith, bearing the sign of him to whom he had been promised. Therefore no one ought to be circumcised any longer; for the sign ran only so long, until he should come who had been promised to restore the faith by which Abraham was justified in uncircumcision, so that believers have no need of circumcision. And Abraham was circumcised for this reason, because he believed that he would have a son in whom all nations would be blessed. Therefore the sign ran so long, until he should come who had been promised. But if they believed this, which Abraham believed, circumcision would not have stood; lest perhaps it be objected: If Abraham believed and was circumcised, why are believers not circumcised?

(Gal. 4:6) And because you are sons of God, God sent the spirit of his Son into our hearts, crying: Abba, Father. He began with those to whom he is also writing, saying: And because you are sons of God; and he adds the Jews when he says: God sent the Spirit of his Son into our hearts. Now when he said that the Gentiles who believe in Christ, who had formerly been enemies, are sons of God, there was no doubt that the believing Jews are called sons of God, who, as is well known, had long ago been called by this name. For they had received the name of God as in an image, so that it might afterwards be confirmed through Christ; for without the spirit it was not full. This is what he also says to the Romans: The Spirit himself bears witness to our spirit that we are sons of God (Rom. 8:16). For, that we might be proved to have been adopted by God as sons, he gave us his Spirit, who shows the sign of the Father toward the sons; so that, since we would not dare to say this in our weakness and unworthiness, he prompts us to dare to say: Abba, which is father. Indeed, it was not granted to the Jews of old to say this.

(Gal. 4:7) So he is no longer a slave, but a son. He is not a slave, because he has been taken away from the power of the Law, made a sharer in the blessing of God's own Son. And if a son, also an heir through God. There is no doubt that someone adopts a son for himself for this purpose, that he may leave him as his heir. But an inheritance comes from someone's death: how is it, then, that mortals are called the heirs of him who lives forever? But Scripture speaks in our manner, so that we can understand; for, to show that the father will give to his sons from his goods, it calls these things which he will give an inheritance.

(Gal. 4:8) But then indeed, not knowing God, you served those who by nature are not gods. Since formerly they sinned through ignorance in serving idols, he indicates that they are readily forgiven, as Abimelech says to God in Genesis: Lord, he says, will you destroy an ignorant nation (Gen. 20:4)?

(Gal. 4:9) But now, knowing God, or rather known by God, how are you turning again to the weak and needy elements, which you wish to serve anew? He notes their grave offense; for after the knowledge of God they had begun to follow the things that belong to the pagans. And he adds more: that they did not know God, but rather were known by God. For when these were not seeking God, he called them to his grace; and these, ungrateful, had begun to turn to the things they worshipped at the time when they did not know God: so as to venerate the weak and needy elements, and not Christ with their whole mind, in whom all divinity dwells. He calls the elements weak and needy because they are imperfect; for to rule the world they need one another, but in Christ is perfect salvation.

(Gal. 4:10) You observe days, and months, and times, and years. He now declares which things he called elements. The sun makes the day, the course of the moon the months; and the times are spring, summer, autumn, winter: these four, when their own number is completed, make the year. So those observe days who say, for example: One must not set out tomorrow; for on the day after tomorrow nothing ought to be begun: and in this way they are usually all the more deceived. Those, on the other hand, worship months who scrutinize the courses of the moon, saying, for example: On the seventh day of the moon instruments ought not to be drawn up; again, on the ninth day of the moon a purchased slave, for example, ought not to be brought home: and through these things adversities are apt to come about the more readily. And they observe times thus, when they say: Today is the beginning of spring, it is a festival; the day after tomorrow is the Vulcanalia. And again they say such things as: It is a day-after; it is not lawful to go out of the house. They worship years thus, when they say: On the Kalends of January is the new year, as if years were not completed every day; but in order to recall the memory of that two-faced Janus, they practice this superstition, which ought to be far from the servants of God. For if God is loved with the whole heart, with him being gracious there ought to be no dread, nor suspicion of these things; for whatever is done simply in devotion to God can turn out prosperously.

(Gal. 4:11) I fear for you, lest I have labored in vain among you. The Galatians had been overturned in a twofold way, so that after the grace of God they were turned not only to the Law, but to their former errors. For, misled or overthrown, they had easily turned aside into the Law, to the observance of days and months; for the observance of the sabbath and of the new moons drew them to these things. Therefore he says: I fear lest I have labored without cause among you; for he saw them advancing for the worse in such a way that they might even withdraw wholly from Christ.

(Gal. 4:12) Be as I am, for I too am as you are. He says this so that they may take pains to correct these things; for it is not impossible that they should be as he is, since he too was what they also were, that is, a man; and when he had been in error, persecuting the Church, he was reproved and turned himself about. Brothers, I beseech you, you have done me no harm. Now he deals more gently with them and begins to recall their good things, so that after great reproofs they might be refreshed on hearing of their good works, and might take pains to reform themselves; lest, pressed too long by harsher rebukes, they become exasperated. Yet he says that they have done him no harm; for their offense was harming themselves, since the Apostle had fulfilled the office of the dispensation entrusted to him, that he should preach to them with all diligence, as the Lord says to the prophet Ezekiel: You, he says, preach: if they hear you, you will gain them; if not, you will save your own soul (Ezek. 3:21).

(Gal. 4:13–14) You know that through infirmity of the flesh I preached the gospel to you long ago: and your trial in my flesh you did not despise nor reject. He recalls to their memory the time when he first preached to them the Gospel of God. For at that very time the Apostle was beset with injuries and weakened by beatings; for he seems weak who is humbled and does not resist, for the sake of the Gospel of God. And in this he praises their constancy, that they were not drawn away from the faith: for the injuries of the Apostle were the trial of the Galatians, in which they were found firm, not doubting about the faith. For they could have taken offense and said: What power or hope is there in this faith, when its minister is so humbled? But because they had already steeped their minds in the hopes of things to come, they did not dread present perils for the name of Christ, certain that our faith has its dignity not on earth but in heaven, and that for these tribulations great rewards are repaid. He makes them blush, because after these praiseworthy things they had been circumvented, so that they appeared blameworthy. But you received me as an angel of God, as Christ Jesus. This he praises in them still more, that he was not disfigured in their eyes by his afflictions, but commended, since they knew that he suffered ruin for those who believe. So they received the Apostle as an angel of God, believing that what he preaches is the gift of God: thus deferring to him as though to the author himself, the Lord Jesus Christ.

(Gal. 4:15) What then was your blessedness? He says that they were held blessed among the other churches, because they were not broken by the Apostle's injuries, but grew in faith. For I bear you witness that, if it could have been done, you would have plucked out your eyes and given them to me. He shows that their devotion toward religion was so full that not only was he well received by them, but they were also found strong in faith: and, what goes further to their praise, they offered him almost all their goods to be spent. Hence he grieves the more, because so great a love had been overturned by perverse doctrine.

(Gal. 4:16) Have I then become your enemy, telling you the truth? He says this because it cannot be, he says, that I should be an enemy to you, from whom I have experienced such great kindnesses. But because no one wishes to be reproved when he errs, therefore I seem an enemy to you, since I justly reprove you.

(Gal. 4:17) They emulate you not well: but they wish to shut you out, so that you may emulate them. He signifies that they were overturned by the envy of the Jews; for, seeing them advance so in the Gospel and have such great love for the Apostle, these contrived to deceive them. For they were enemies to the apostles because of the Law, so that, while the name of the Savior remained, they might make them Jews, rejoicing in their overthrow; for they could not bear to hear that the grace promised to themselves had been given to the uncircumcised Gentiles. Therefore they wished them to become their imitators, so that they might be circumcised after their manner.

(Gal. 4:18) But emulate the better gifts, emulate the good always, and not only when I am present with you. The better gifts are the spiritual ones; having imitated these, they will have effect before God. These are the spiritual goods which he wishes them always to imitate, not for a time, as before, or only while he is present, only afterward to turn to something else; but he wishes them to persevere in the good from now on.

(Gal. 4:19) My little children, of whom I am again in labor until Christ be formed in you. At first he had begotten them through faith in baptism; but because, born as if by a miscarriage, they were found deformed and weak, he now, with the pain of counsel, re-forms them into Christ. For, conceiving faith but paying no heed to the meaning of faith, they are said not to have Christ formed in their souls; for if they had understood the grace of God in Christ, they would not have been carried over into Judaism after faith.

(Gal. 4:20) But I would wish to be present with you now, and to change my voice; for I am confounded in you. He wishes to rebuke them in person, so as to change his voice from praise of them to censure, so that they might be confounded in a twofold way, both by their own error and by the shame of the Apostle, which he suffered before those to whom he had preferred them.

(Gal. 4:21–22) Tell me, you who wish to be under the Law, have you not read the Law? For it is written that Abraham had two sons: one by the slave woman, and one by the free woman. This is found in Genesis: that since Sarah could not bear children, for she was barren, she offered her slave woman Hagar to her husband Abraham as a concubine, so that she might adopt for herself what was begotten. So it came about that Ishmael was born of Hagar. But afterward, by the will of God, a son was promised to Abraham by Sarah his wife, who was barren: she bore Isaac to Abraham. Then Abraham began to have two sons, one by the slave woman and one by the free woman.

(Gal. 4:23–24) But he who was of the slave woman was born according to the flesh; while he who was of the free woman, through the promise; which things are spoken through allegory. Ishmael, who was born of the slave woman Hagar, was born according to the flesh, because he was born according to custom; but Isaac, because he was born through the promise, was born not according to custom but according to the providence of God, by his power, seeing that Sarah was both old and barren. For Isaac was born as a type of Christ; therefore he asserts that these things are spoken through allegory, so that the persons of Ishmael and Isaac signify one thing by another. For Ishmael signifies the birth of the Jews, or of those who are slaves of sin; but Isaac that of the Christians, because they are born in freedom; for he who receives the remission of sins becomes free.

For these are the two Testaments. Moses, taking the blood of a calf in a bowl, sprinkled the people, saying: This is the blood of the Testament which God has ordained toward you (Exod. 24:8). This is what he said: One from Mount Sinai, bearing children into slavery, which is Hagar. For the Law was given on Mount Sinai, and Moses, reciting it to the people, called this the book of the Testament, and then sprinkled the people with blood, as I have said. This Law held sinners guilty, and they began to be slaves of sin, made sons of Hagar as of a slave woman.

(Gal. 4:25) Now Sinai is a mountain in Arabia, which is joined to this which is now Jerusalem, in slavery with her children. He says that the case of Hagar signifies the earthly Jerusalem, that is, the Synagogue, which bears children into slavery; for those whom it bears are under sin.

(Gal. 4:26) But that Jerusalem which is above is free: which is our mother. This one, whom he calls our mother, is the rule of the Lord's mystery, through which we are reborn into freedom, just as she herself is free. Therefore she is also called heavenly, because heaven is her seat: and those whom she bears will be there with her, as the Lord says: Father, those whom you have given me, I will that where I shall be, they too may be with me (John 17:24). She is also life, because those who believe and are reborn through her are given immortality. For so it was said of the Lord that what was made in him is life. For as the Father has life, so he has given life to the Son also, and the life was the light of men (John 5:26). This life and light is the mother of the faithful. In Isaiah is found what is set down next:

(Gal. 4:27) For it is written: Rejoice, barren one, you who do not bear; break forth and cry out, you who are not in labor; for many are the children of the deserted one, more than of her who has a husband (Isa. 54:1). He says that this earthly Jerusalem has a husband, because she bears according to the flesh; but that heavenly Jerusalem, which he calls our mother, he calls barren, because she does not bear according to the flesh, nor suffer pains, that is, she is not in labor. But she bears spiritually, without suffering, crying out in joy; for, once deserted (and by whom, if not by Adam, who deserted life and followed death), she now has more children than this one who has a husband, that is, who bears carnally. For Christians are many more than the Jews, as is said in the Apocalypse: And I saw a great crowd, which no one could number, from every tribe and nation (Rev. 7:9).

(Gal. 4:28) But you, brothers, are sons of the promise according to Isaac. That is, they are sons not of the flesh but of God; for Isaac was born as a type of the Son of God: and he wants them to understand by implication that, having been made sons of God, they had turned so as to be sons of the flesh; but if they come to their senses, they are sons of God.

(Gal. 4:29) But as then he who had been born according to the flesh persecuted him who was according to the spirit, so it is now also. Not only Ishmael was born according to the flesh, but Esau too; for he too was born as a type of the Jewish people. Of him it is written in the prophet Malachi: Jacob I loved, but Esau I hated (Mal. 1:3). He persecuted Jacob, who, born as a figure of the Christian people, was called Israel, that is, a man seeing God (Gen. 27:41). Likewise he signified that the Jews too persecute the Christians through envy; whence they had deliberately overturned the Galatians with carnal cunning.

(Gal. 4:30) But what does Scripture say? Cast out the slave woman and her son; for the son of the slave woman shall not be heir with my son Isaac. These are the words of Sarah, who bears the type of the heavenly Jerusalem, bearing Isaac by the promise; so that, the son of the slave woman being cast out, that is, the Jewish people, rejected because of unbelief (for they are slaves of sin, to whom the remission of sins has not been given), the son of the free woman, of that Jerusalem above, may be heir, who is the new people of the kingdom of heaven.

(Gal. 4:31) So we are not sons of the slave woman, but of the free, with the freedom by which Christ has freed us. We were, then, sons of the slave woman while we were liable to sins; but having received the remission of sins from Christ, we have been freed.

Chapter V

(Gal. 5:1) Stand, therefore, and do not be held again by the yoke of slavery. He warns them to stand in the grace they have received, lest, going back to the old things, they lose the gift of freedom which they had obtained.

(Gal. 5:2) Behold, I Paul say to you that if you are circumcised, Christ will profit you nothing. Now he does not keep silent about the force of the open truth and of his own authority, saying: Behold, I Paul say to you, that is, I, the teacher of the Gentiles, whose work you are in Christ, say to you openly: That Christ will profit you nothing, if you are circumcised; for the Lord himself says: The Law and the prophets until John; since then the kingdom of heaven is preached (Luke 16:16). Before the new preaching began, therefore, it was right to be circumcised; but now, since the law of faith has succeeded, those things must be followed which the fresh truth commands; for the old and the new must not be mixed together, on the authority of the Lord, who preaches; for he says: No one sews a patch of fresh cloth onto an old garment; for it takes away its strength from the garment, and the tear becomes greater (Matt. 9:16). And thus circumcision, if added, not only will profit the believer nothing, but will even harm him; for it is a greater evil to become a slave after being free than to be born one.

(Gal. 5:3) And I testify to every man who circumcises himself that he is a debtor to do the whole Law. Some can keep the Law to some extent and not be circumcised: for many of the Romans in Judea kept the Law while uncircumcised. Hence the apostle Paul says to Agrippa, king of Judea: I know, King Agrippa; you believe the prophets, I know that you believe (Acts 26:27). And the centurion, whose slave was about to die, who sent to Jesus asking him to come and heal his slave; to whom the Jews bore witness, saying to Jesus: He is worthy that you should do this for him; for he loves our nation, and he himself built the synagogue for us (Luke 7:4–5). Yet no one who is circumcised fails to keep the whole Law; for he is a debtor, because the Law was given to the circumcised. He said this because they were showing themselves so dull that they deserved to carry all the burdens of the Law.

(Gal. 5:4) You have been emptied out from Christ. Those whom Christ profits nothing have been emptied out from Christ; for they were full of his grace, and when it withdrew, they were emptied. For he speaks to those who, after receiving the gift of the grace of God, preferred to be circumcised. For you who glory in the Law have fallen from grace. The righteousness of the Law consists in keeping the things that were given. And so he who wishes to be justified in these after faith loses grace; for the gift of God came for this purpose, that, the burdens of the Law ceasing, it might justify believers before God.

(Gal. 5:5–6) But we by the spirit, from faith, await the hope of righteousness; for in Christ Jesus neither circumcision avails anything, nor uncircumcision: but faith, which works through love. What he says is plain: that the hope of justification is in faith through the spirit, not through the works of the Law; for in faith God is served spiritually, by devotion of mind and purity of heart. Hence the Lord says to the Samaritan woman: God is spirit; and those who worship him must worship in spirit and truth (John 4:24). And thus neither uncircumcision avails anything, nor circumcision: but faith alone is needed, in love, for justification; for faith must be fortified by brotherly love, so that the believer may have perfection. Accordingly, the Savior answered the scribe who asked what the first commandment was: You shall love the Lord your God with your whole heart and with your whole soul: and your neighbor as yourself. On these, he says, two commandments hangs the whole Law, and the prophets (Matt. 22:37 ff.). So the other things are imperfect, since perfection is contained in these two commandments.

(Gal. 5:7) You were running well; who hindered you from obeying the truth? He testifies that their exercise in the works of faith was good, but that they were held back by the wickedness of evil men, so that they did not complete their course with persevering efficacy. Hence he exhorts them to come to their senses, believing from now on none of those who, by persuading them that the works of the Law must be kept, would not let them obey the gospel truth.

(Gal. 5:8) Your persuasion is not from God, who calls you. It is true, for the Jews were doing these things by human counsel, to put them under the yoke of the Law: not by the judgment of God, who was calling them through his Apostle to grace.

(Gal. 5:9) A little leaven corrupts the whole lump. He says this because those who mix anything whatever of the Law with faith violate faith, so that it has no fruit and is not acceptable. He added this lest perhaps they should think they kept the grace of faith unharmed if they observed a few things of the Law.

(Gal. 5:10) I trust in you in the Lord that you will think nothing else. He says that he trusts concerning them with this hope, because they had entered the way of error not of their own accord but because they were circumvented. Therefore he trusts that, once the true road has been shown, they can easily return. But he who troubles you shall bear judgment, whoever he is. As he who makes one who errs turn back is to be rewarded, as the apostle James says in his epistle: He who makes a sinner turn back will save his soul from death and will cover a multitude of sins (Jas. 5:20); so too he who forces one walking on the right way to turn his road aside to a byway incurs condemnation, whoever he may be. He adds this on account of these men, lest any should claim merit for themselves on the ground that they were sons of Abraham according to the flesh. Indeed, exalting themselves, they say to Jesus: We are sons of Abraham (John 8:33).

(Gal. 5:11) But I, brothers, if I preach circumcision, why do I still suffer persecution? What he says is plain; for he was held as an enemy by the Jews, and they did not cease from persecuting him, because he taught that circumcision no longer stood. Finally, when the Jews pressed him and repeatedly brought false accusations against him, seeing a biased judge, he was forced to appeal to Caesar. Then the scandal of the cross has been made void (Acts 25:11). The preaching of the cross was a scandal to the Jews, because it made void the sabbath and circumcision. But if he admitted circumcision, there would be no scandal, and the Jews would be at peace with us. For they said of the Savior: This man is not from God, who does not keep the sabbath (John 9:16).

(Gal. 5:12) Would that they were even cut off, who are overturning you! This is like that saying in the first epistle to the Corinthians: Whoever does not, he says, love the Lord Jesus, let him be anathema (1 Cor. 16:22). These too, whom he joins to their sentence, are of that same diseased flock: so that those who stripped the Galatians of the grace of God should be cut off from the mercy of the Lord; and he curses them not only spiritually but also carnally, so that, since they were forcing the Galatians to be circumcised, they themselves should be cut off, that their bodily pain might be multiplied.

(Gal. 5:13) For you have been called into freedom, brothers. They were called into freedom because, when they were liable to sins, they received the remission of offenses by the grace of God and were freed. Only do not give your freedom as an occasion to the flesh. He says this lest an occasion of the flesh take away the freedom they had received: for they wished them to be circumcised for this purpose, that they might enter into the condition of the Law. Therefore he warns them not to give occasion by consenting to them. But through the love of the spirit serve one another. He exhorts them that love is to be had not through the affection of the flesh but through the love of the spirit, so that they may be subject to one another.

(Gal. 5:14) For the whole Law is fulfilled in you in one word: You shall love your neighbor as yourself (Lev. 19:18). This is written in Leviticus; it is what was said above, that the perfection of salvation is accomplished through love; for whoever does not love his brother does not love God, says the apostle John (1 John 4:20).

(Gal. 5:15) But if you bite one another and accuse, see that you are not consumed by one another. It is clear that dissension among brothers is the enemy of love; therefore he warns them to be peaceable, advancing in the good. For contentions breed quarreling, whose fruit is the consuming of life.

(Gal. 5:16) But I say, walk by the spirit, and you will not fulfill the desire of the flesh. After he has forbidden them the law of works, in which there are carnal precepts, he now commands them to restrain themselves from the vices of the flesh. For he walks by the spirit who flees sins.

(Gal. 5:17) For the flesh desires against the spirit, and the spirit against the flesh; for these are opposed to each other, so that you do not do the things that you will. He sets forth two laws, as he does also in the epistle to the Romans (7:23), which are opposed to each other: one of God, the other of sin. The latter is signified in the flesh for this reason, that it delights in visible things, being greedy for sins; so that, with these opposing each other, man, standing between them, does not do the things he wills. For the divine law presses upon the law of sin and puts it to flight, counseling man to guard the vigor of his nature, lest he be captured by allurements; the other, on the contrary, acting by ambushes, provokes man with blandishments so that he may spurn the precept of the divine law. When, therefore, man has consented to the law of God, the law of sin contradicts, persuading man not to do what the divine law commands; and conversely the law of God calls man back, so that he may not do what the law of sin suggests. Indeed, man does not see this to be absurd; for he knows that it befits his nature if he does what the law of God commands. Accordingly he rejoices when he does these things; but when he does the things the law of sin suggests, he sees himself base and shudders after the deed. And therefore the precepts of the law of the spirit must be kept, and carnal things fled; for conscience itself accuses a man if he consents to it, knowing that the things which the Law of sin suggests are a horror.

(Gal. 5:18) But if you are led by the Spirit, you are not under the Law. It is not obscure that whoever has the Holy Spirit as his leader does not err; for the law has dominion over those who err, as he says in another place: the law is not laid down for the righteous (1 Tim. 1:9).

(Gal. 5:19–21) Now the works of the flesh are manifest, which are adultery, fornication, impurity, obscenity, idolatry, sorceries, enmities, contentions, dissimulations, angers, feuds, dissensions, heresies, envyings, murders, drunkenness, revelings, and things like these, of which I forewarn you, as I also foretold, that those who do such things will not obtain the inheritance of the kingdom of God. All these and things like them are members of the law of sin, which the Apostle calls works of the flesh; because these errors are from the world, from which the flesh also is. For all sins arise from outside, from the side of the flesh, not from the side of the spirit; therefore they are not unreasonably called works of the flesh.

(Gal. 5:22–24) But the fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, long-suffering, kindness, hope, gentleness, continence, chastity, goodness: against such there is no law. And those who are Christ's have crucified their flesh with its vices and desires. He did not say: The works of the spirit are love, but the fruit of the spirit; for the law of the spirit bears as fruit these things which he mentioned above, so that from them the inheritance of the kingdom of heaven may be sought; for those who are devoted to Christ, these he calls christs, that is, anointed ones. These crucify the flesh, that is, the world, when they condemn those things from which errors arise. Following this sense, the apostle John also says: Do not love this world, nor the things that are in this world. If anyone loves the world, the love of the Father is not in him; for all that is in this world is the desire of the flesh and the desire of the eyes (1 John 2:15). He, then, who does not desire the world and what is in the world crucifies the flesh. For these vices are not to be ascribed to the human body, that is, idolatry, sorceries, heresies, envyings, enmities, angers and the like: but they are called carnal for this reason, that they are from the side of the world, as I mentioned above. Meanwhile he set down no more than ten things which he signifies as the fruit of the spirit, so that in these he may be seen to have embraced everything, in keeping with the tablets of the Testament of God, on which no more than ten words of commandments were handed down.

(Gal. 5:25–26) If we live by the spirit, let us also walk by the spirit. Let us not be pursuers of empty glory, provoking one another, envying one another. He says this because, if we live well and honorably, we ought also to have good morals; for this is to live by the spirit, to have an undefiled life: and thus we walk by the spirit if we are zealous for peace; for this begets love. But empty glory is wanting to win where there is no prize, so that one devotes himself to contention and zeal, knowing that these breed discords and quarrels: But the servant of the Lord, he says, must not quarrel (2 Tim. 2:24). For it makes the fruits of a good life dry, losing the promised hope of a chaste way of life: for a good and chaste life must be adorned with morals and enriched with benevolence.

Chapter VI

(Gal. 6:1–2) Brothers, even if a man has been overtaken by some fault, you who are spiritual, instruct such a one in a spirit of meekness, considering yourself, lest you too be tempted; bear one another's burdens. Now he addresses those who were firmer, so that they would not, from the inflation of a good life, think that one who had perhaps been ensnared by sin should be spurned and cast off; and therefore thought must be taken for such as these. For they must be called forth with meekness to reform themselves; because if they are rebuked too sharply, on authority, they will not suffer themselves to be reproved, but will even begin to defend themselves, lest they seem base; and they begin to become worse. For take a man away from contention and boldness, and you will have him submissive; for humility is wont to bend even the proud. And so that it might be done with all gentleness of mind, he added: Lest you too be tempted; so that, if for this reason alone, he would not spurn the sinner, knowing that he too can sin. And so you will fulfil the law of Christ; for the law of Christ wills all to be saved: and therefore he who takes thought for a sinning brother, that he may return to his purpose, fulfils the will of Christ.

(Gal. 6:3–4) For if anyone thinks himself to be something, when he is nothing, he deceives himself. But let each one prove his own work; and then he will have glory in himself alone, and not in another. It is true, and hidden from no one, that if we consider our acts and our mind, we set ourselves before no one, nor do we readily judge anyone. For he who is lifted up as though he were something errs; for he does not know that one grows through humility. For he does not have before his eyes the words and deeds of the Savior, who, though he is Lord of all, humbled himself (Phil. 2:8), to give us a pattern of what we ought to follow if we wish to grow: and that if we were to raise ourselves up, we would be bowed down, which is what the ignorance of a puffed-up heart does, so that it may seem more commendable among men. For, spurning and despising sinners, he strives to seem righteous, like that Pharisee whom the Lord brands (Luke 18:10). Is not this man truly ignorant, who is greedy for present glory? Does he not truly despair of the things to come, who wants to obtain the reward of righteousness in the present? Why? Because he is not righteous in deed, but feigns righteousness through deceit; for he who wants to be righteous sets another before himself, so that he may be truly righteous. For each one knows his own evils better than another's.

(Gal. 6:5) For each one will carry his own load. At the last he wished to make this known to the proud: that no one is defiled by another's sin, provided he dissents from it, so that no one should be afraid to join himself to a sinner, or to receive one who approaches him, in order to win him.

(Gal. 6:6) But let him who is catechized in the word (that is, who is a hearer of the word of God) share with him who catechizes him in all good things: He says this so that hearers may share with their teachers in all good things. But if they act otherwise than they teach, in these things one must not share with them, but dissent; so that the law may be your guide rather than a man. For he will harm himself, not you; because you observe what he has taught.

(Gal. 6:7) Do not err; God is not mocked. No one doubts that no imposture can be practised on God, but that each one obtains from him what he deserves.

(Gal. 6:8) For whatever a man sows, that he will also reap. For he who sows in his own flesh will reap corruption from the flesh. Here he signifies the carnal vices which are usually committed in the flesh; so that he who has corrupted his flesh may know that he will be liable to corruption. And he corrupts the body who presumes upon things not permitted. But he who sows in the spirit will reap eternal life from the spirit. The Christian man consists of spirit and flesh; for man, in comparison with the holy spirit, is flesh. When, therefore, he gives assent to the spirit, restraining himself from those things which are contrary to it, he sows spiritually, so that he may reap eternal life. For he receives the testimony of the Spirit, and immortality by way of reward. For just as he who corrupts, dishonoring his own body, will belong not to eternity but to corruption, so also he who keeps himself undefiled will obtain eternal life. For this is eternal life: to live without punishment, and to have glory. For if here, for the present, one who is in anxiety denies that he lives, how much more, where he is estranged from God, will he suffer fire as his tormentor!

(Gal. 6:9) But in doing good let us not fail; for in its own time we shall reap, unwearied. He says this because he who is strong for sowing will also be strong for reaping, that is, he will receive just as he does. Therefore he exhorts us to be ready for doing good, and says that we ought not to suffer weariness, secure that we shall receive great things for small, and eternal things for earthly, at the time that God has promised.

(Gal. 6:10) Therefore while we have time, let us work good toward all. The Gentiles have time for working evil; for men without God, how can they work good? But those who have become Christians have time for working good toward all men, so that, after faith has been received, good work may avail for the promised reward. For time is granted to life for this reason, that we may now conduct ourselves righteously, giving an example to the Gentiles. But especially toward those of the household of faith. It is true that we ought to be readier and more inclined to good toward the brothers, because we are members of one another. This thing, then, which is profitable, is what must be served; not circumcision, a thing now empty.

(Gal. 6:11) Know with what letters I have written to you with my own hand. He gives authority to his epistle; for when he says, I have written with my own hand, he wants these letters to be obeyed. For where there is a hand in his own writing, it cannot be called false; lest perhaps those who had been ensnared, being unwilling to be reproved, should make excuses about the epistle, as though it were either false or not the Apostle's.

(Gal. 6:12) All who wish to please in the flesh, these compel you to be circumcised, only that they may not suffer persecution for the cross of Christ. To please in the flesh signifies to please men. For, so that they might live with the Jews favorable, or at least not hostile, those whom he calls false apostles preached Christ in such a way as to teach that the law also must be kept: which the Apostle never feared, but steadfastly did not keep silent about what is true, providing both for himself and for those whom he taught. Indeed: I, he says, am ready not only to be bound, but also to die in Jerusalem for the name of our Lord Jesus Christ (Acts 21:13).

(Gal. 6:13) For not even those who are circumcised keep the law; but they want you to be circumcised, so that they may boast in your flesh. This is like that which was said to the apostle Peter: If you, though you are a Jew, live like a Gentile, how do you compel the Gentiles to judaize (Gal. 2:14)? So these too, though they themselves did not keep the law, living unrighteously, were zealous for circumcision, so that they might thereby seem avengers and lovers of the law: whereas it is plain that he defends and keeps the law who carries out its righteous requirements, and does not pass over the commandment which was given as pertaining to life. Such, then, was the excuse of the Galatians. He says these things so that at least in this way they may understand that they were being mocked as fools and dullards, over whose pains those men boasted.

(Gal. 6:14) But far be it from me to boast, except in the cross of our Lord Jesus Christ, through whom the world has been crucified to me, and I to the world. Just as the false apostles, through hypocrisy, bought themselves off from tribulations for the name of Christ, as though they would profit nothing, being ashamed of them, so the Apostle boasted of them, knowing that there would be a great reward for them: as did his fellow apostles also, as it is written in the Acts of the Apostles: Then, he says, the apostles went out from the council beaten, rejoicing that they had been held worthy to suffer insult for the name of Christ (Acts 5:41). He, therefore, whose every hope is in Christ boasts in his cross, and to him the world is crucified, since he is certain that love of the cross of Christ is life, but that love of the world brings forth death. Therefore they are dead to one another, both the world to the Apostle and the Apostle to the world; while the Apostle covets nothing of the world, and while the world has nothing of its own that it may recognize in the Apostle, as the Lord also says: Behold, he says, the prince of this world comes, and in me he will find nothing (John 14:30).

(Gal. 6:15–16) For in Christ Jesus neither is circumcision anything, nor uncircumcision; but a new creation. And as many as follow this rule, peace be upon them and mercy, and upon the Israel of God. The new creation is our regeneration; it is this that gives progress to those who persevere; for upon these is the peace of God. For he who is at peace with God fears no one's enmity; for he is safe in the protection and mercy of God, which was promised and remains upon the Israel of God, not upon the Israel of man; for the Jews who do not believe are Israelites according to the flesh. He has therefore signified two parts, upon which the peace and mercy of God remain: that of the believing Jews, and that of the faithful Gentiles.

(Gal. 6:17) For the rest, let no one trouble me. He did not want troubles to be caused him from now on, such that he might perhaps be forced to write again: but he wants them now to become more corrected, so that, their errors cut away, he might have rest after such great wearinesses. But if they showed themselves harder, and inflexible to conversion, let them leave him alone, so that he might spend his labors and his efficacy on those from whom he could hope for reward or gain. For I bear the marks of the Lord Jesus Christ in my body. He says this in anger, because he saw that the sufferings which he endured for the sake of believers were ineffective in them; since, for the constancy and faith of his teaching, he did not fear to suffer blows and chains for Christ: but out of love for believers he endured to have the scars of the blows in his body, so that he might preach to them no less faithfully, as the false apostles also did, who had deceived the Galatians. He therefore wants no one to trouble him who is not devoted to this cause; for these marks, that is, the scars of the blows, are testimonies of those believers who suffer, that they are worthy of the future promise.

(Gal. 6:18) The grace of our Lord Jesus Christ be with your spirit, brothers. He does not speak of the grace of the Law, but says that the grace of our Lord Jesus Christ is with the spirit of those whom he calls spiritual brothers; for not all of them had been utterly overthrown.
