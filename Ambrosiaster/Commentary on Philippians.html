Commentary on Philippians

Latin source: the commentary on the Epistle to the Philippians by the anonymous Roman writer called Ambrosiaster (written at Rome about 366–384), from the Maurist text of 1690, printed under the name of Ambrose in J.-P. Migne, Patrologia Latina 17 (Paris, 1845), cols. 403–420, verified against the page images and collated in full with the Maurist edition (Paris, 1690). Migne's misprints where the Maurist edition he reprints has the right reading are corrected (eight words, at 1:21, 2:1–4, 2:7–8, 3:2, 3:3–4, 3:8 and 4:10), as are a misprinted Scripture reference and the verse label he prints over 2:9–11 as 10–12; at 3:17 the lemma stands as Migne prints it (mihi, where the Maurists, Ballerini's edition (Milan, 1877) and the manuscripts read mei; the sense is the same). The lemmata are translated from Ambrosiaster's own Old Latin text of Paul, and all other Scripture as he quotes it, from his own Latin; italics mark the lemmata and the other Scripture he quotes. The edition's verse labels are given as (Phil. chapter:verse); neither Migne nor the Maurists print a lemma for 4:14. This English translation is released into the public domain.

Prologue

The Apostle delivered the word of faith to the Philippians; for he was warned in a dream, as is contained in the Acts of the Apostles (Acts 16:9), to go to them and open their ears to receive the word of the Lord. Having stayed with them, then, for many days, he instructed them in the teaching of the Lord. And because after his departure they were not carried over to something else, as the Galatians were, but remained in the faith, not receiving the false apostles, he praises them highly by letter, rejoicing in the purpose of their heart. In the praise of these is contained the reproach of others: for the Corinthians, though they had heard the Apostle for a long time, were overthrown by various false apostles when he withdrew; hence these are rightly praised.

Chapter I

(Phil. 1:1) Paul and Timothy, servants of Jesus Christ. He is silent about his being an apostle, because he is writing to those who thought rightly about him; therefore he suppresses his dignity, for it was manifest to them: but he confesses his status; because whoever confesses Christ as lord is by these things the more free, and has salvation. To all the saints in Christ Jesus. He writes to the saints according to his custom, but to those who are saints in Christ Jesus, that is, who confess Christ as God and man; for Photinus denies that Christ is God, and Mani that he is man: and therefore they are unclean. So he does not write to those who suppress the truth with their own lies. Who are at Philippi. He indicates to whom he is writing. With the bishops and deacons: that is, with Paul and Timothy, who were certainly bishops; at the same time he also indicated the deacons, who ministered to him. For he is writing to the people; for if he were writing to the bishops and the deacons, he would be writing to their persons, and he would have had to write to the bishop of that place itself, not to two or three, as he also wrote to Titus and Timothy.

(Phil. 1:2) Grace to you and peace from God our Father, and the Lord Jesus Christ. It is manifest that God is father; but that Christ Jesus is our Lord. Does not a lord seem to be more than a father? But that is among men in the world: with God the Father, however, and with Christ the lord there is concord. For here, that is, in the world, a lord is more than a father for this reason: because either he is lord even of the father himself, or the father begets a son not of his own status but of another's; but God so begot that those whom he begot are in his power, so that, though born free, they are nevertheless his servants: when he hands them over to his own Son, he makes them also servants of his Son, so that he is their lord, as he says: Father, those whom you gave me were yours, and you gave them to me; so that in the Father and the Son they are servants of the one God (John 17:9).

(Phil. 1:3–5) I indeed give thanks to the Lord upon every remembrance of you, always in every prayer of mine for you all making prayer with joy, for your fellowship in the Gospel. First indeed he gives thanks to the Lord, by whose will all things turn out prosperously for the faithful. In every prayer of his he is mindful of them with exultation, because they have been made partakers of the Gospel, as the apostle Peter, saying among other things: That you may be, he says, sharers of the divine nature (2 Pet. 1:4).

From the first day until now. For the Apostle foresaw their devotion, and their faith made firm from the very beginnings, and he rejoiced in them, always knowing that they would be an example to many.

(Phil. 1:6–7) Being confident of this very thing, that he who began a good work in you will perfect it until the day of Christ Jesus, as it is right for me to think this for you all; because I have you in my heart and in my bonds, and in the defense and confirmation of the Gospel; since you are all partakers of my joy. Secure about the goodness and justice of God, and not doubting about their faith, he is confident that, aided by the help of God, they will persevere in faith until the day of the Lord, on which those who persist in faith are to be rewarded. Nor ought he to have thought otherwise about those whom he had found always unbending in devotion to God, seeing their course so straight and untiring, so that he would consider them worthy to be partakers of his joy. For men of one faith ought to rejoice together over the hope of future immortality and glory.

(Phil. 1:8) For God is my witness, how I long for you all in the bowels of Jesus Christ. He calls God to witness in order to commend the affection of a righteous man; for one who feels much about someone testifies, to make it credible: and by this he also provokes their longing toward himself, so that those who are longed for may long in return. He longs, then, for all of these not with human desire, but in the love of Christ; not with the affection of the flesh, but of the spirit; so that he may have them as partakers in the understanding of the mystery both of the divinity and of the incarnation of our Lord Jesus Christ.

(Phil. 1:9–11) And this I pray, that your love may abound more and more in knowledge and all understanding, that you may approve the things that are useful; that you may be sincere and without offense unto the day of Christ, filled with the fruit of righteousness through Jesus Christ unto the glory and praise of God. With God as helper, he wishes to instruct them in the order of the Christian discipline, so that, since they are established in faith, they may not be ignorant of the mystery of their belief; and that, knowing how to discern useful things from their contraries, they may adorn the teaching of the Lord with works of righteousness, producing the fruit of immortality unto the abundance of all good things, so that the teacher of the Gentiles may appear glorious in them.

(Phil. 1:12–17) But I want you to know, brethren, that the things which concern me have come to the advancement of the Gospel; so that my bonds have been made manifest in Christ in the whole praetorium, and in all the rest; and that many of the brethren, trusting in the Lord through my bonds, have begun to dare more to speak the word of God without fear. Some indeed both through envy and contention, but some also through good will, preach Christ. Some indeed out of love, knowing that I am set for the defense of the Gospel; but others out of contention proclaim Christ, not purely, thinking that they stir up affliction for my bonds. These things need no explanation; yet, because of our solicitude, lest any of them come into doubt, let us go through them in order more plainly. Since above he declared them partakers of his joy, he therefore makes known, as to dear ones, what progress his preaching has made, so that his sufferings for Christ have become known to all. By this humility he shows himself glorious, so that those who love him may have occasion to rejoice; because the dignity of one whose bonds are made manifest in Christ grows. In the whole praetorium, and in all the rest, that is, in all Judaism, and throughout all the Churches of the Gentiles; for Judaism comes first, because the promise was made to it, as it is said: For salvation is from the Jews (John 4:22). And therefore he says that many of the brethren, having followed his example, dared to speak the word of God with confidence; for his constancy encouraged many, so that they did not neglect to evangelize, having learned from the Apostle's example that God is at hand to protect those who love him; but he says that others too, false brethren, preach the word of God, stirred not by confidence nor by love of God but by the contention of jealousy, as envious men. Many of the brethren, then, proclaimed Christ with a will that was sincere indeed, but differing; for some did this with good will, for the glory of God alone; but others, stirred by love of the apostle, being certain of God's protection. So again, on the other side, he sets down two other kinds, which did not preach with a sincere mind; for some, envying the Apostle, did this out of contention; but others proclaimed Christ with the cunning of pretense, so that they might stir up seditions against the Apostle, as the author of this sect, who had gathered many into his league. For greater envy grew against the Apostle when many were seen to teach from his side.

(Phil. 1:18–21) For what? Provided only that in every way, whether in pretext or in truth, Christ is proclaimed: in this too I rejoice, and moreover I will rejoice. For I know that this will turn out for me to salvation through your prayer and the supply of the spirit of Jesus Christ according to my contemplation and hope; that in nothing shall I be confounded, but in all open confidence, as always, so now also Christ will be magnified in my body, whether through life or through death; for to me to live is Christ, and to die is gain. His first joy is in the brethren who proclaim Christ with good will; then he says that he rejoices also in this, that it had been contrived against him by the deceit of men that Christ should be preached to bring envy and affliction upon him. For he says that what they had prepared for his death profits him for life; for he says that this is his life, if Christ is preached to all, and that he is ready even to die so that this may be fulfilled, knowing that a great reward can be given to him for this wish and its accomplishment: for unskilled malice always plots against itself, without knowing it. To tread down, then, the malevolence of those who, with a deceitful show of uprightness, were laying snares for his life, he testifies that he is fortified by the defenses of God, those whom he certainly calls the companions of his joy helping him by prayer because of their common eagerness, and by the supply of the Holy Spirit, who, against the will of those men, would also open ways to him as he preached, giving grace and confidence to his words, so that he might quell the seditions that had arisen. He says, then, that he is in nothing confounded in the teaching of Christ; both because the hope of the promise is great, and because there is nothing in it base, dishonorable or deceitful, as among the pagans, but open simplicity. For hence it is that with all constancy he does not yield to adversities, so that Christ may be magnified in his body, whether he is killed, or escapes after being beaten. For from this is seen what and how great a hope there is in Christ, when he is ready not only to be beaten and afflicted for him, but even to die. For he says that Christ is his life, whether in the present or in the future, as John the evangelist also testifies, saying: What was made, in him was life, and the life was the light of men (John 1:3–4). For this life he hands over his mortal body, so that he may receive it for eternity: and for this reason he counts it gain to die for Christ.

(Phil. 1:22–26) If, then, to live in the flesh, this is for me the fruit of work: and what I shall choose I do not know. But I am hemmed in between these two, having the desire to be dissolved and to be with Christ, for it is much better; but to remain in the flesh is very necessary for your sake. And having this as certain, I know that I shall remain and continue with you all for your advancement and joy of faith, so that your glory may abound through Christ Jesus in me, through my coming to you again. What he says is manifest: that if he is placed in the flesh, his living is not idle; for he has the fruit of his work. What I shall choose, he says, I do not know. For he is pressed by two causes; for he has the desire to depart and to be with Christ, judging it much better, so that he may now be present with God, so as to enjoy the things longed for and promised; but that he should still be in the flesh he calls necessary for the instruction of believers. And although he considers it much better to be dissolved and to be with Christ, yet he declares that he knows this for certain, that he will remain in the flesh for the advancement of the faithful, so that their glory may abound in the Lord, redounding to the praise of the Apostle, when they see him again. For they make growth in knowledge, and are more fully grounded in faith. How great was the affection he had toward believers, that he does not choose what he says is much better for himself, but wills this, which benefits many, being certain also that this pleases the Lord, that it profits the salvation of many! Who, eager for the third heaven, would not spurn every present care? who, from desire for the upper paradise, would not set all these things aside? who, mindful of the secret words, would not shun human speech? who, after so many deadly perils, would not choose rest? But because he put love before all things, he followed this, which would profit the brethren.

(Phil. 1:27–28) Only conduct yourselves worthily of the Gospel of Christ. He wants them to show a way of life worthy of the Lord's discipline, so that he may not regret having wished to remain in the flesh. So that whether I come and see you, or, being absent, hear of you that you stand in one spirit, of one mind, striving together with the faith of the Gospel, and not terrified in anything by the adversaries, which is to them a display of destruction, but to you of salvation. He wants them to be of one mind in faith and devotion against the adversaries, so that, when absent, he may hear the same things about them which he also saw when present; so that, fearing in nothing the threats or terrors of the adversaries, they may keep the faith, knowing that for those who are afflicted for Christ it profits unto salvation, but for those who afflict, unto perdition.

(Phil. 1:29–30) And this has been granted to you by God for Christ, not only that you believe in him, but also that you suffer for him, having the same contest which you both saw in me and now hear of me. Although he extols the grace of God in all things, yet it redounds to their praise that he says that God granted them to suffer for Christ; for he does not grant or give this except to lovers of Christ. Therefore he says: For Christ it has been granted to you; so that it has been granted by God the Father to those who love Christ that, as proof of their merits, they suffer for Christ, and, following the examples of their teacher, strive with all confidence to triumph over the unbelievers.

Chapter II

(Phil. 2:1–4) If, therefore, there is any exhortation in Christ, if any comfort of love, if any participation of the spirit, if there are any bowels and mercies, fulfil my joy, that you be of the same mind, having the same love, like-minded, thinking one thing; let nothing be done through provocation, nor through empty glory, but in humility of mind each esteeming the other better than himself: each looking not to the things that are his own, but to the things that are others'. He says this because: if there is among you a true exhortation of hope in Christ, with which we comfort one another; if there is the comfort of a common love; if there is participation in the Holy Spirit, through which we are joined in one new body; if there are any bowels, that is, if I am in your heart, as you also are in mine; if mercies, that is, if the affection of devotion is present in love: fulfil my joy. The things he enumerates, he indicates, are to be proved true only then, if the things he enjoins below have been kept: that they be like-minded and humble in spirit, not provoking one another, but rejoicing in love, so that the joy of the Apostle may be full in them. Thus, then, he binds them, so that if they do not keep these things, they may not seem approved in the rest either; so that, since in other things they do not wish to seem unapproved, they may keep these also, that they may be perfect. For without doubt there were among them some who disagreed and were restless, and who were eager for empty glory, wishing to contend and to win with fierceness of spirit, where there is no reward: but it breeds quarrels and breaks love. For what people can there be in which there are only vessels of gold and silver, and not also of wood and of earthenware? But by the practice of good they can be changed and become gold and silver; for the fault is not of nature, but of will.

(Phil. 2:5–6) For have this mind in yourselves which was also in Christ Jesus, who, when he was in the form of God, did not think it robbery to be equal to God. Christ was always in the form of God, because he is the image of the invisible God. But the Apostle treats of the Son of God when, incarnate, he was made man, saying: Have this mind in yourselves which was also in Christ Jesus, that is, in God and man. Who, when he was in the form of God: living, of course, among men, he appeared by words and works to be God. For the form of God differs in nothing from God; for he was called the form and image of God for this reason, that it might be understood that he is not himself God the Father, but that he is what God is. He, then, did not think it robbery to be equal to God; for, knowing that he was in the form of God, he did not steal it when he said: I and the Father are one (John 10:30). And elsewhere in John's Gospel: Therefore, he says, the Jews persecuted Jesus, because he not only broke the sabbath, but also called God his own Father, making himself equal to God (John 5:18). Rightly, then, did he make himself equal to God; for he thinks to commit robbery who makes himself equal to one to whom he is inferior.

But he emptied himself, taking the form of a servant, made in the likeness of men, and found in appearance as a man. He humbled himself, made obedient unto death, and that the death of the cross. Christ, then, knowing that he was in the form of God, showed himself equal to God: but in order to teach the law of humility, he not only did not resist the Jews who were seizing him: But he emptied himself; that is, he withdrew his power from its working, so that, humbled, he might seem to be weakened, his power lying idle. Taking the form of a servant: while he is held and bound and driven with blows, made obedient even to the cross to the Father, to whom he knew himself equal, he did not defend his equality for himself, but subjected himself. He teaches us to imitate this patience and humility, so that we may not only not set ourselves before our equals, but may even bow ourselves down, following the example of our author. Yet he is said not to have received the form of God, but to be in the form of God: but to have received the form of a servant, while he is humbled as a sinner. For servants are made from sin, as was Ham the son of Noah, who was the first deservedly to receive the name of servant (Gen. 9:25). For it does not seem to me, as it does to some, that he received the form of a servant in this way, when he was born a man. For see what he says: Have this mind in yourselves which was also in Christ Jesus, that is, God and man. For before the incarnation he can be called either Christ or Jesus; because both names together signify both the son of man and the Son of God. For what does he say, among other things, of the time before the nativity? And the rock was Christ, and let us not tempt Christ, as some of them tempted (1 Cor. 10:4). Where, then, Scripture wishes to signify either God or man, it sets down one of the two: either Jesus or Christ. For the Son of God, when he had been born a man, was in the form of God; because, while he was seen as a man, he was doing the works of God; so that in the deeds he did it might appear that he was God who was thought to be only a man. For the works signified the form, so that, since his works are certainly not the works of a man, he who was in the work, or form, of God might be understood to be God. For what is the form of God, if not the pattern by which God appears, when he raises the dead, restores hearing to the deaf, cleanses lepers, and the rest? And how is he said to have been made like a man, if he was only a man: and for what reason was he found in appearance as a man, if he were not also God; unless because, though before he allowed himself to be bowed down he was always seen in the power of God, afterwards, weakened, he was found in the appearance of a man? For on the mountain, certainly, he appeared as God (Matt. 17:2): and walking on the sea with his feet, he was seen and understood to be not only man but also God. Then what do those who were in the boat say? Truly this is the Son of God (Matt. 14:33). When this power, then, ceased, he was seen as a man; for he says "as" for this reason, to signify that he is also God. For it is on account of weakness that he says God was made like men. This he discloses in what follows, saying: He humbled himself, made obedient unto death, and that the death of the cross. Hence it is that he was found in appearance as a man; for, holding back his power so that it should not appear in him, he who knows not how to die was seen as a man and was slain.

(Phil. 2:9–11) Wherefore God also highly exalted him, and granted him a name which is above every name; that in the name of Jesus every knee should bow, of those in heaven, of those on earth, and of those below: and every tongue confess that the Lord Jesus Christ is in the glory of God the Father. He shows what and how much humility merits, so that we might seek it the more, having trampled boasting underfoot. Hence Solomon says: God resists the proud, but gives grace to the humble (Prov. 3:34). Wherefore the Lord too: Whoever, he says, exalts himself will be humbled; and whoever humbles himself will be exalted (Luke 14:11). This, then, is humility: if one does not claim for oneself even what one knows to be owed. Indeed that Pharisee (Luke 18:11 ff.), saying true things of himself, is said to have exalted himself; and for that reason he was humbled: but Christ did more, who, though he is God, being made flesh bowed himself down to men. Now let us see how the name which is above every name was granted to Christ, as if the Son of God had been in need, or had been imperfect; for he who is in need is imperfect. For if he was in the form of God, he was on a par with and equal to God; how was it granted to him? For he says it was granted to him who humbled himself, and appeared as a man, emptying himself, made obedient unto death. If, then, he was born perfect, how was he in need? Whoever denies that the Son of God was born perfect of God the Father is an Arian; for before the passion he showed himself equal to God the Father, as I also mentioned above. Hence it is established that he was born perfect; for in his birth he is seen to have obtained all things. For he was born in the fullness of divinity to do all the things that he did; so that he had received the gift before he did the things he was born to do. The gift of the Father, then, is seen to be this: to be the Son; and that his name should be above every name, that is: to be God; for the name of God, but by nature, not by appellation alone, is above every name. Hence it is that in the name of Jesus every knee should bow, of those in heaven, of those on earth, and of those below; as he indicates to the Romans, saying among other things: And of whom is Christ according to the flesh, who is God over all, blessed for ever (Rom. 9:5). And what these "all things" are he discloses in this epistle, saying: things in heaven and on earth and below: for there are no others. Yet to some it seems that the name which is above every name was granted to the man, which fits in no way and by no reasoning. For if Christ the Son of God is himself the same one who is also man, God made man, but remaining God, could not be in need of the things that he had: or if, according as he was man, he was in need of the things that are God's, he himself, the Son of God, being God, would have given himself what was lacking to him according as he was man. This indeed is said out of foolishness. And why is the Father said to have given, when he himself who had been made man lacked nothing? Or could the divinity of the Son not supply to its own body and soul whatever was lacking? But perhaps it is because all things are from God the Father that he himself is said to have given. If, then, all things are from the Father, why should he not be said to have given all things to his Son through generation? And although he begot a Son equal to himself, yet order requires that all things be done in the name of the Father. This the Father granted to the Son after the cross: that all things should be saved in the name of the Son. Indeed the Lord says of the disciples to the Father: Father, those whom you gave me I have kept; for they were yours, and you gave them to me (John 17:12). And to the disciples he says: Until now you have asked nothing in my name; ask, and you will receive (John 16:24). This, then, he received at his birth, so that after the cross it might be made manifest what he had received from the Father when he was begotten. For he received the name from the Father then, when he began to be known by creation; for it comes to be recognized that, on account of the things he did, he had received it before he did them. Indeed before the cross he said: All things have been delivered to me by my Father (Matt. 11:27). How, then, can this be referred to the body, so that the name of God was granted to the body, when it is established that Christ is man and God; since it is neither fitting that God be changed into flesh, nor can flesh be made into that which God is? But perhaps [they say] that he was God by adoption: this too is a pretext; for then Christ will begin to be in part true God and in part adoptive, or else two gods. But Scripture signifies otherwise: for it signifies that it was granted to him who emptied himself, who took the form of a servant, who was made man in the likeness of man, who obeyed the Father. If a man obeyed God the Father, what great thing is it that the Apostle has said? But this is the great thing: that, though he was equal, he obeyed. And what praise is there, if a man was made in the likeness of man, so that a man is said to have been made like a man? But that cannot be; for no one is made in the likeness of anyone unless he is something else before he is made in the likeness of the one he wishes; for he was made like by circumstance, not by substance. And who is it that emptied himself? A man. Then he had something. And how did he receive? But of what could a man, a weak thing, empty himself? And was a man in the form of God? But perhaps the man in the form of God was the Son. Come now, the Son of God, in whose form was he? And if the man was already in the form of God, that is, was the Son of God, he was already perfect before the passion. He did not, then, receive anything after the passion. But let us see what is written of him: Wherefore God highly exalted him, and granted him a name which is above every name; that in the name of Jesus every knee should bow, of those in heaven, of those on earth, and of those below: and every tongue confess that the Lord Jesus Christ is in the glory of God the Father. Let us now consider the words, and so attend to the force of the expression. Certainly the name which is above every name is the name of God. But if this name does not hold by nature, it is not above every name; for a name by appellation lies in the word alone, not in the nobility of nature: and creation does not bend the knee to an adoptive God, but to the true one, not to a fellow creature. And how can it be that a man is in the glory of God the Father? Even if the man were an adoptive God, he cannot be in the glory of God the Father; for this belongs to him who was born of God. For to be in the glory of God the Father is to differ in nothing from God; so that there is one glory of the Father and of the Son through a common substance and power; for this is the unity of nature. What, I ask, is there contrary in it, if the Son is said to have received a gift from the Father, since all that is the Son's is from the Father, from whom all things too are believed to be? And we call the substance of God "nature" for this reason: because Christ was born of it.

(Phil. 2:12–13) Therefore, my dearly beloved, as you have always obeyed, not in my presence only, but much more now in my absence, work out your own salvation with fear and trembling; for it is God who works in you both to will and to work according to good will. He testifies that God helps good endeavours; for he always refers all grace back to God, so that the willing is ours, but the accomplishing is God's. For who would conquer the Princes and Powers, the rulers of this darkness, the spiritual beings of wickedness in the heavenly places, except God alone, since we have them as enemies by reason of envy? For they are indignant that we are called sons of God. What he urges, then, is nothing new: but that, as usual, they be subject to the Gospel, taking care of their own way of life; for a good way of life works salvation.

(Phil. 2:14–18) Do all things without murmurings and hesitations, that you may be blameless and simple, as sons of God, spotless in the midst of a crooked and perverse nation: among whom you shine as lights in the world, holding fast the word of life, to my glory in the day of Christ; because I have not run in vain, nor laboured to no purpose. But even if I am offered up upon the sacrifice and ministry of your faith, I rejoice and congratulate you all. And for this same reason do you also rejoice and congratulate me. So that they may have the fruits of their faith and of their works, he admonishes them to do all things that pertain to Christian discipline without hesitations and murmurings; because if anything is done negligently and without devotion, it does not reach its effect, since it falls under reproach: but that they should work in all simplicity, as spotless sons of God, so that, mindful of their profession, they may answer to it; so that among the distrustful they may appear as bright in life, way of life and conduct as the sun and the moon stand high in splendour among the stars. For as the stars are darkened when the sun appears, so the unrighteous and unfaithful, in the presence of the faithful and the righteous, appear base and disfigured; so that the Apostle may have glory at the coming of the Son of God, since his planting has produced such fruits. For then his labour and its effectiveness will be proved not to have been empty. So even if I am offered up, he says, upon the sacrifice and ministry of your faith, I rejoice. For if he should be crushed by the faithless because he gathered them into his league, he indicates that he is to be congratulated; for he knows that his death has its effect. For it is a great sorrow if one suffers for those in whom one accomplishes nothing. Therefore he testifies that he rejoices in them, and that they ought to congratulate him; so that both the people and their able teacher may share with one another in a common joy.

(Phil. 2:19–24) But I hope in the Lord Jesus to send Timothy to you shortly, that I also may be of good heart when I have been informed about you. For I have no one so like-minded, who would bear a sincere concern for you; for all seek the things that are their own, not the things that are Jesus Christ's. But you know his proof, that as a son with a father he served with me in the Gospel. Him, therefore, I hope to send at once, as soon as I see how things stand with me. And I trust in the Lord that I myself also shall come to you soon. These things are plain and need no interpretation; for he indicates that after this epistle he will send Timothy, through whom he may learn reliably what is being done concerning them. For he was like-minded with the Apostle; that is why he is also marked out as more concerned for them, not like certain men who, while they wished to commend themselves, were negligent of the discipline and way of life of the brethren. For they did not preach with a devoted mind, but for their own gain. And because Timothy was an excellent man, he says that he held him proved; for he was with him as a dearest son, serving the Gospel of Christ. He calls him son because he had been ordained by him: he says that he is sending him ahead, and that he himself will also go to them shortly.

(Phil. 2:25–27) But I thought it necessary to send to you Epaphroditus, my brother and fellow soldier, and your apostle, and the minister of my work; since he longed for you all, and was impatiently anxious, because you had heard that he had been sick. For indeed he was sick near to death: but God had mercy on him, and not on him only, but on me also, lest I should have sorrow upon sorrow. All these things are plain; for he indicates that he will send Epaphroditus to them with this epistle. For both were sorrowful in their longing for one another on account of his sickness, both the people and Epaphroditus; so that on seeing him they might be refreshed by his recovered health, and he might be free of the anxiety he bore to see them. For he had been made their apostle by the Apostle, when he sent him to them to exhort them: and because he was a good man, he was longed for by the people. Why is it, then, that with the Apostle present, who raised the dead, this Epaphroditus was sick even to death? Did the Apostle not pray for him, and not obtain that he be healed more quickly? But signs are done for the sake of unbelievers; for this sickness befell him not to his loss, but to his increase. For the trials of believers are various: for one is tried through sickness, another by the loss of dear ones, another through loss of money, another through tribulations, to see whether he is found steadfast in necessity, not seeking help from the opposite side, so that he may make an increase of merits. The Apostle's request, then, was not despised, but better provision was made for him for whom he was asking. But what does this mean, that, whereas in another epistle he warns that one ought not to be saddened at the departure of the brethren, in this one he indicates that he would have been saddened if Epaphroditus had departed from the body? He had said that, if he had departed, he would have had sorrow upon the sorrow that he had over his sickness; for he was necessary to the Churches. For to the Thessalonians: See, he says, that you be not saddened concerning those who sleep, as also the rest who have no hope (1 Thess. 4:12). But he had said that he would be sorrowful at the departure of Epaphroditus because he needed his comfort and help; not, however, so as to mourn him as one lost.

(Phil. 2:28) Being anxious, therefore, I have sent him, that when you see him you may rejoice again, and I may be of better heart. He indicates that Epaphroditus and the people of the Philippians had a mutual affection, so that on seeing him they might rouse themselves again to gladness, and the Apostle's heart might rejoice; because he becomes of better heart who sends someone to revisit his dear ones.

(Phil. 2:29–30) Receive him, therefore, in the Lord with all joy, and hold such men in honour; because for the work of Christ he came near even to death, handing over his soul to destruction, that he might supply what was lacking to you regarding my service. Although the love between Epaphroditus and the people was undivided, yet he is also commended by the Apostle, so as to make him dearer still: above all on account of those who were perhaps not so warm in their affection toward him; for in every people there is diversity. Hence it is that he also recalls his battle, that he did not hesitate to die for the work of the Gospel: that is, he was not deterred by the threats and afflictions of the unbelievers, but persisted in exhorting the faithful, so as to fill up what was lacking to the people in the handing on, according to the Apostle's dispensation. He, then, who does not draw back his soul from death hands it over to destruction as regards the present life, as the Lord also says: For whoever wishes to save his soul will lose it (Matt. 16:25). This losing is the destruction; because he who hands over his soul for Christ loses it as regards the world; yet he will preserve it for the future.

Chapter III

(Phil. 3:1) Finally, my brothers, rejoice in the Lord. To write the same things to you is indeed not irksome to me, but for you it is necessary. He shows the concern which he bears for their progress, because he is unwearied in writing to them the things that are necessary for their benefit.

(Phil. 3:2) Beware the dogs, beware the evil workers, beware the concision. He means those who, envious of the Gentiles, had overthrown the Galatians by the depravity of their talk, persuading them to be circumcised. He teaches that they are to be avoided and spurned in every way, like dogs that first bark and then, with a savage bite, work evil upon the flesh.

(Phil. 3:3–4) For we are the circumcision, who serve God in spirit, and glory in Christ Jesus, and have no confidence in the flesh; although I might have confidence even in the flesh. It is clear that believers themselves circumcise their heart, so that, the mist of error being cut away, they may behold and acknowledge the Lord of creation. This is to serve God in spirit and to glory in the Lord Jesus Christ, who gave the law of the spirit, so that the glory of the flesh might be emptied. Yet the Apostle also had this confidence, so that, if he wished, he could glory in the circumcision of the flesh; but knowing that this glory is empty, he rejected it.

(Phil. 3:5–7) If anyone seems to have confidence in the flesh, I more: circumcised on the eighth day, of the race of Israel, of the tribe of Benjamin, a Hebrew of Hebrews, according to the Law a Pharisee, according to zeal persecuting the Church of God, according to the righteousness which is from the Law, having lived without blame. But the things that were gains to me, these I have counted losses for Christ. As far as concerns the observance of the Law, and the keeping of its righteousness, and devotion to the sect of the Pharisees, and, in keeping with zeal for the tradition of the fathers, the persecuting of the Church, he had no equal; for in the other things those who were likewise Jews by nature were his partners. But the things that seemed gains according to human understanding were found, in comparison with Christ, to be losses. Why losses, unless because it was no longer the time for the Law to be kept? For all these things were empty, and hindrances to approaching grace; and because these are carnal, but ours are spiritual. And he says that he is of the tribe of Benjamin in order to exalt his lineage above those who trace their origin from the patriarchs, the sons of Jacob by his concubines, or on account of those who were proselytes. Hence he says: I more, by circumcision of the eighth day, of the race of Israel, to show that he was first among the Jews, but that he despised this for the sake of the hope promised by Christ. The Hebrews, however, were named after Abraham; for a letter was changed for the sake of the sound, so that they were called not "Abraei" but "Hebraei": just as among the ancients, when "medidie" was said and it was absurd, a letter was changed so that "meridie" was said. For if their origin is from Abraham, their name too ought to be drawn from him, not from Heber, who is in the sixth generation above Abraham (Gen. 11:14). For if in Abraham the human race began to be formed anew, and he himself is the father of faith, from him come both the name and the Hebrew tongue. For the first tongue, which was without a name, when it is restored to him, takes its name from him. For there is no reason why it should draw its name from Heber; for Abraham was a Chaldean, and yet the Hebrews do not have the Chaldean tongue.

(Phil. 3:8–11) But indeed I count all things to be losses because of the eminence of the knowledge of Christ Jesus my Lord, for whose sake I have counted all things losses, and count them dung, that I may gain Christ, and be found in him not having my own righteousness, which is from the Law, but that which is through the faith of Christ, which is from God, the righteousness upon faith, to know him, and the power of his resurrection, and the fellowship of his sufferings, conforming myself to his death; if in any way I may arrive at the resurrection which is from the dead. Since above he was speaking of the race of Abraham, and of the Law and its righteousnesses, and of the merits of those who observe it, reckoning all these things a loss in comparison with the promises of the Savior, he now adds not only these things: But even all things to be loss because of the eminence, he says, of the knowledge of Christ Jesus my Lord. After the Law and its righteousnesses, what will all these things be, unless it means that the knowledge of Christ is to be put before the very life of body and soul? For if, as he says to the Colossians, in Christ are hidden all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge (Col. 2:3), all things whatsoever that exist are to be reckoned of no account, so that one may succeed in attaining to the excellence of this wisdom and knowledge. Sufficient, therefore, nay overflowing, for salvation is the righteousness which is from faith, not from the Law, which has been granted by the grace of God from the knowledge of Christ. For the knowledge of the mystery of his incarnation, and passion, and resurrection is the perfection of life and the treasure of wisdom; for where there is wisdom, there is also life, because there is no life where there is folly. Thus Solomon: The fool, he says, does evil through laughter (Prov. 10:23). For he who understands that Christ was made man and suffered for his sake, and, having conquered death, rose again, despises and spurns all things and follows him by whose protection he sees himself freed from death, seeking occasions to deserve well of him, so that he may be able to make him some return in at least something, and so deserve to obtain still greater promises in the resurrection.

(Phil. 3:12) Not that I have already received, or am already justified, or am already perfect: but I follow, if I may apprehend, in which I also was apprehended by Christ Jesus. Since in the whole epistle he testifies that he rejoices in them, and praises their obedience and faith, lest perhaps, being men, they should be lifted up with conceit because they were already worthy, he now declares openly, in his own person, that something is still lacking to him for fulfilling justification, so that they may strive to add to it in good works. For if this man, who is endowed with such great dignity, still confesses that perfection is lacking to him, how much more should these understand that they must labor to obtain the merits of justifications! Therefore he added: But I follow, he says, that I may apprehend, in which I also was apprehended by Christ. For while he was wavering in error and in the turmoil of his mind, distrusting the way of the Lord, the mercy of God apprehended him on the road, as one fleeing the grace of Christ, as he was going to disturb and afflict the Church, to this end, that he might learn the gift of God and the grace which he was rejecting, and might know that for its sake even to die is gain; and therefore that he could do no harm to believers, but to himself, if he did not believe, he who in ignorance was persecuting a thing of salvation. For this, then, he was apprehended, that is, sought out by the foreknowledge of God, he was found and learned the mystery of Christ, so that, making increase of merits throughout his whole life, he may apprehend the appointed prize, having deserved well of him who, as he learned, was slain for his sake.

(Phil. 3:13–14) Brothers, I do not count myself yet to have apprehended. But one thing: forgetting indeed the things that are behind, and stretching myself out to those that are before, I press on toward the mark, to the palm of the heavenly calling of God. This is what he said above, for he indicates that he labors for this reason, that he may advance daily for the better, always stretching himself out toward greater things, so that, forgetting the things that are past, he may pursue better ones: not because they are bad, but because they are small for laying up merit. But one must advance for the better, so as to arrive at the appointed heavenly prize; for he declares that for the sake of so admirable and invisible a prize one must certainly strive, and not lightly.

(Phil. 3:15) As many of us, then, as are perfect, let us be of this mind. Above he denies that he is perfect, but with respect to the promise, so that something may still be added to his merit; in comparison with the rest, however, who attend to divine things more negligently, those are to be called perfect who, applying diligence, walk the road of perfection. This, then, must be their mind: that they advance for the better. And if in anything you are otherwise minded, God will reveal this also to you. He says this so that, if they should devise anything further for the cultivation of an improved way of life, they may consider it to be the gift of God, lest they be puffed up by it.

(Phil. 3:16) Nevertheless, to what we have attained, that we mind the same thing, and walk in it. Lest some presumption should arise from this, and something be supposed that had not been revealed by God, he therefore added, saying: To what we have attained, that we mind the same thing; that is, not to mind things outside the rule of discipline in the way of life of faith, but to mind this, which is common and modest in the truth of the Gospel.

(Phil. 3:17–19) Be imitators of me, brothers, and observe those who walk so, as you have our pattern. He wants them to be watchful, lest they be caught by the subtlety of wicked men, and to take note of each one, how he both lives and teaches, so that they may join themselves to those in whom they have recognized the mind of their teacher. For many walk, of whom I often told you, and now tell you even weeping, enemies of the cross of Christ: whose end is destruction, whose God is their belly, and their glory is in their shame, who mind earthly things. Those whom he indicates are of the number of those who had overthrown the Galatians. For, walking with guile, they were subverting the Churches of God, preaching under the name of Christ that Judaism was to be practised, as though Christ had been promised to them. He mentions them with grief and tears because they were obstructing the salvation of believers, raising questions about foods to be eaten and not to be eaten, as though salvation lay in food, or the belly were God, which they thought, according to the Law, was delighted with clean foods, glorying in their circumcised shameful parts; this is to mind earthly things. But he who minds spiritual things glories in faith, in hope, in love. It does not belong to this passage, however, that he said on account of gain or of gluttony: Whose God is their belly.

(Phil. 3:20–21) But our way of life is in heaven; whence also we await the Savior, our Lord Jesus Christ, who will transfigure the body of our lowliness, conformed to the body of his glory, according to his working, by which he is able also to subject all things to himself. Those who care for spiritual things are said to live in heaven, though they themselves are still on earth; just as the wicked Princes and Powers, though they themselves are still in the firmament, are nevertheless on earth in their way of life. The acts of Christians, then, are in heaven, where also is the hope of their salvation, which is Christ, whom every believer awaits for the transfiguration of the body of flesh, so that what is from the earth, clothed with a spiritual garment, he may make conformed, at the coming of his majesty, to the body of his glory, according to his working, that is, according to his power, by which he is able to subject all things to himself: that is, because through the resurrection of the dead and the change into glory the power of the Lord will appear made manifest, subduing all the Princes and Powers, so that he himself may be manifested to be God and Lord of all.

Chapter IV

(Phil. 4:1) Therefore, my dearest and most longed-for brothers, my joy and crown, so stand in the Lord, most beloved. He admonishes them to persevere in the same faith; for his joy at present is their constancy, and in the future his crown. For when the disciples are victors in the contest, the teacher will be worthy of a crown.

(Phil. 4:2–3) I ask Euodia, and I beseech Syntyche, to mind the same thing in the Lord. And I ask you also, true yokefellow, help those women who labored with me in the Gospel, with Clement and the rest who are my partners in the works, whose names are in the book of life. He exhorts Euodia and Syntyche to increase their prudence, with the help of those whom he mentions, so that, as they had made progress in the works of the Gospel, they might make progress also in the knowledge and wisdom of God; and he declares that all of these, by the merit of faith, are held in the book of life.

(Phil. 4:4) Rejoice in the Lord always; again I say, rejoice. The Apostle, glad at their works and faith, to heap up his own eagerness, earnestly wishes that they, advancing in this zeal, should always rejoice in the Lord; for this is why he repeats it, to show that he rejoices in them with true affection.

(Phil. 4:5–7) Let your modesty be known to all men; the Lord is near. Be anxious in nothing, but in all things by prayer and supplication, with thanksgiving, let your requests become known before God. And may the peace of God, which surpasses every mind, guard your hearts and your understandings in Christ Jesus. Now he wishes to gain others by their example; for when their modesty, that is, their reasonable way of life, has been made manifest, their works will shine, and there will be no lack of those who imitate their good, so that they may obtain increase not only from their own deeds but also from those of others; so that, as it is said (Ps. 127:2), they may eat the labours of their fruits. For when the fruit of their works gains others for good work, the labours of those who have been gained will profit those by whose example they began to imitate good works. The Lord, he says, is near. Be anxious in nothing: that is, they ought to be prepared, and to watch in prayer and supplication, giving thanks to God, and, setting aside the care of the world, to hope for and keep before their eyes what the Lord has promised; whence he teaches that thanks are also to be given to him; for when the Lord is at hand, the care of the world ceases. For why should they be anxious about a thing that will soon perish? And therefore, let your requests, he says, be made known before God. This making known comes about through assiduity and watchfulness in prayer. Then at last, with the defences of his peace, which surpasses every mind, may he guard your hearts in Christ Jesus. He said this for the reason that he who has peace with God does not fear any hostile mind; for everyone who loves God in Christ Jesus is safe by the peace of God. For who would not fear one whom he knows to be a friend of God?

(Phil. 4:8–9) For the rest, brothers, whatever things are true, whatever magnificent, whatever just, whatever holy, whatever lovable, whatever praiseworthy, if any virtue, if any praise of discipline: think on these things. The things which you have both learned and received, and heard and seen in me, do these, and the God of peace will be with you. In conclusion he sets out the things that remained, for the imitation of perfection, so that, both thinking and doing the things which not only do not fall under blame but are even worthy of praise, and following the pattern of the Apostle, they may have the God of peace with them. Our God is truly the God of peace, who exhorts to peace unasked, as one mighty, and not out of fear, as one weak, so far as even to forgive the sins committed against him; though he could certainly, by the terror of his power made manifest and of his ineffable greatness, compel even the unwilling, not to peace, but to subjection. But peace of that kind belongs to the world, not to God, whose very nature is peace.

(Phil. 4:10) But I rejoiced in the Lord greatly that at last you have flourished again to this, that you are mindful on my behalf, as you were indeed mindful; for you were occupied. Now he shows his eagerness made more ready, because in the matter in which they had become more negligent they applied diligence and repeated their former work, having become mindful of their Apostle, so as to send their fruits to him to be laid up in the heavenly granary, where neither moth nor rust destroys (Matt. 6:19).

(Phil. 4:11–13) I do not speak as if on account of want; for I have learned to be sufficient in the things in which I am. I know both how to abound and how to be humbled (everywhere and in all things I have been instructed), both to be filled and to hunger, both to abound and to suffer want: I can do all things in him who strengthens me. He testifies that he rejoices in this work of theirs not for his own sake, but that, with God's help, he is content above all with their own progress; so that he would neither be saddened by want nor exult in abundance, knowing that abundance often lifts a man up, and want endured acquires heavenly riches. Yet he declares that their works toward him were kind; for it was of benefit to the necessities of many.

(Phil. 4:15–17) And you also know, Philippians, that in the beginning of the Gospel, when I set out from Macedonia, no Church shared with me in the account of giving and receiving except you alone; for when I was in Thessalonica, you sent once and again for my needs: not that I seek a gift, but I seek the fruit that increases to your account. He recalls these things for this reason: that, hearing that their works are held in remembrance and praise, they may not doubt that they are credited before God, but may rather apply zeal for the faith.

(Phil. 4:18) Yet I have received all things, and abound; I am filled, having received from Epaphroditus the things that were sent from you, for an odor of good sweetness, an acceptable sacrifice, pleasing to God. It is not idly that he admonishes that it ought to be done, nor on any other ground that he exhorts to what he professes has been done, except that it seemed to have been done either more negligently or less abundantly than it had been their custom to do it; so that, remembering their former works, they might know that they had done less than they had begun. For although he does not disapprove of what was done, but indicates that they had been more devoted in this work before, yet, he says, it is welcome, and God credits it, as a pleasing sacrifice. Thus the Lord says: Give alms, and behold, all things are clean to you (Luke 11:41). How welcome a work it is, then, that it cleanses away every stain and filth of man!

(Phil. 4:19) And may my God fill all your desire according to his riches in glory in Christ Jesus. He made a distinction for this reason, saying: My God, so that, in accordance with what he granted to his merits, help might be present to their desires also, in the abundance of the greatness of his goodness, in the glory of Christ Jesus. It is the glory of Christ Jesus when, by the will of God, the desires of Christians are fulfilled according to the discipline of the Gospel.

(Phil. 4:20) But to God and our Father be glory for ever and ever, amen. Here he made no distinction, but says generally: To God and our Father be glory. He is God because of fear, but Father for the sake of honor, and because from him is every origin; for he himself said in Malachi the prophet: If I am God, where is my fear? and if I am Father, where is my honor (Mal. 1:6)?

(Phil. 4:21) Greet every saint in Christ Jesus. Greet in Christ Jesus him who sanctifies himself, so that he may be held worthy of Christ, who said: Be holy, because I am holy, the Lord your God (Lev. 19:2).

(Phil. 4:22) The brothers who are with me greet you. The greeting of brothers affords mutual comfort, for it presents, as it were, images of those who are parted. All the saints greet you, especially those who are of Caesar's household. He indicates that these have a readier affection, with regard to those who are of Caesar's household, and he shows these to be superior also to the brothers mentioned above, because he calls these saints as well.

(Phil. 4:23) The grace of our Lord Jesus Christ be with your spirit, amen. Since questions arise about the person of the Savior, here, to affirm the solidity of the divinity in him, he wishes for the protection of his grace upon our spirit.
