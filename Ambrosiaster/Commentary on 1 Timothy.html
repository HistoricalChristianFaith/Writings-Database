Commentary on 1 Timothy

Latin source: the commentary on the First Epistle to Timothy by the anonymous Roman writer called Ambrosiaster (written at Rome about 366–384), from the Maurist text of 1690, printed under the name of Ambrose in J.-P. Migne, Patrologia Latina 17 (Paris, 1845), cols. 461–484, verified against the page images and collated in full with the Maurist edition (Paris, 1690). Migne's misprints are corrected where the Maurist edition he reprints or Ballerini's edition (Milan, 1877) has the right reading, as are his misprinted Scripture references and verse labels. On 5:18 the comment reads debet esse with Ballerini's edition and the critical text, where Migne and the Maurists print esset. The lemmata are translated from Ambrosiaster's own Old Latin text of Paul, and all other Scripture as he quotes it, from his own Latin; italics mark the lemmata and the other Scripture he quotes. The edition's verse labels are given as (1 Tim. chapter:verse). This English translation is released into the public domain.

Prologue

Timothy was the son of a believing Jewish woman, his father being a Greek, on whose account he could not be circumcised as a little child. Through his mother's zeal he learned the sacred letters, and, applying himself to them, he established a good life for himself (Acts 16:5). When the Apostle wished to take him on, knowing that he would be a fit steward of the truth of the Gospel, he was compelled for the sake of the Jews to circumcise him, since they insisted that one who had been born of a Jewish mother ought not to be taken on uncircumcised for the office of teacher, because of the testimony of his race. So, now that he has been made bishop, he instructs him by letter how he ought to order the Church.

Chapter I

(1 Tim. 1:1–2) Paul, an apostle of Christ Jesus according to the command of God our Savior, and of Christ Jesus our hope, to Timothy, genuine son in faith. Grace, mercy and peace from God our Father, and Christ Jesus our Lord. He shows that he was made an apostle by the command of the Father and of the Son, so that he might preach salvation in God the Father and hope in Christ his Son; so that, since no one denies that salvation is in God, no one should doubt either that hope is in Christ Jesus, whom he would recognize, raised from the dead, as having given the example of this hope. He writes to Timothy in this way so that he might be secure about his own ordination, because he was chosen to teach by the command of the Father and of the Son. He calls him a true son, but in faith, as he says to the Corinthians: In Christ Jesus, he says, I begot you through the Gospel (1 Cor. 4:15). This is the true and firm generation, which knows no setting, knows no diseases and pestilence, and which is ignorant of hunger and thirst: but it has learned to lack nothing in the kingdom of God; because by the gift of God there will be a glorious immortality in the kingdom of God and of Christ, whose grace, mercy and peace he desires to remain with him.

(1 Tim. 1:3–4) As I asked you to remain behind at Ephesus, when I was going into Macedonia, that you might charge certain persons not to teach otherwise, nor to attend to fables and endless genealogies, which provide questions rather than the edification of God, which is in faith; so do. A son ought not to have been asked by a father; but out of the affection of love, and to show him a pattern of humility, the bishop entreats his fellow bishop not to allow the Jews to teach the people otherwise than was handed down by the Apostle; that they should not be delighted with the fables which the Jews are accustomed to tell about the generation of their origins, about Abraham and Isaac and the other patriarchs, and about circumcision and the things that were afterwards handed down by Moses; that they should not be ensnared into observing these things, which had been given carnally. For what need is there to make mention of generations, which are known to be endless through descent, and of what was done under each of them, and to what extent; and not rather to seek salvation by the short way through faith? For from this questions arise, when it is discussed what was done, and when, and how, and why: a thing which hinders salvation.

(1 Tim. 1:5–7) For the end of the precept is love from a pure heart, and a good conscience, and unfeigned faith, from which some, straying, have turned aside into vain talk, wishing to be teachers of the Law, understanding neither what they say nor concerning what they affirm. Since love is the greatest of all, he said that it is the end, that is, the consummation of perfection, of all the precepts; provided, however, that it is from a pure heart and a good conscience. For true love cannot have a bad life nor a feigned faith; in these the whole Law is fulfilled, and the Prophets (Matt. 7:12). But some of the Jews, ignorant of the mystery of the Law and of the prophets, that they were handed down to be kept until Christ, went astray, thinking that, once Christ had been preached, the law of deeds too, in new moons and circumcision and the distinguishing of foods, ought to be kept. Therefore he says that they turned aside into vain talk, because they wished to teach what they did not know.

(1 Tim. 1:8–11) But we know that the Law is good, if one uses it lawfully, holding this as certain, that the law is not laid down for the righteous, but for the unrighteous and disobedient, for the ungodly and sinners, for the wicked and profane, for parricides and matricides, for fornicators, murderers, those who lie with males, kidnappers, liars, perjurers: and whatever else is opposed to sound doctrine, which is according to the Gospel of the glory of the blessed God, which has been entrusted to me. He does not deny that the law which was given through Moses is good; yet in such a way that its sense be known, and that it be received as it was given. For he knows the Law, and for what cause it was given, who, once Christ has been preached, abandons it in that part in which it is called the law of deeds; but in what concerns the love of God he uses it, asserting and affirming the things which were spoken by the Savior in the Law and the prophets. For he uses the Law lawfully who can distinguish the things that were given for a time from those that are perpetual: and knows that the things which pertain to the mystery of Christ ought no longer to be kept for the future, as though he who has already come were believed to be still to come. If then all things are taken in their own places, they are good: if not, they will be bad for one who asserts them perversely; for it is a sin not to expound each thing in its own place. Moreover, because the righteous have no need of the law, that is, those who, having received remission of sins, have been justified, so that they no longer live under the Law, from which they have been freed by the gift of God through faith in Christ; so that for the rest, keeping the law of nature, which receives the knowledge of the Creator and exhorts us not to sin, they may await the day of judgment, in which the righteous are to be rewarded, who keep the natural law; and if the human race had held it as its guide, the law in letters would not have been given through Moses. But because it was held in contempt, as though it were of no authority, Scripture was established, so that it might be known that it was to be kept, both because of the present time and because he who established it will in the future inquire by whom it was neglected and by whom it was kept. Thus he says that the Law was given not only because of evil acts and the vices of the body, but also because of the faithlessness with which they spurned the Creator; so that they might learn to live well and to give thanks to the Maker.

(1 Tim. 1:12–14) And I give thanks to him who strengthened me in Christ Jesus our Lord, because he judged me faithful, putting me into the ministry, because I was formerly a blasphemer, and a persecutor, and insolent; but I obtained mercy, because I acted in ignorance, not yet believing. And the grace of our Lord superabounded with the faith and love which is in Christ Jesus. Because every unbeliever suffers a weakness of mind, constrained by carnal reasoning so as not to believe the spiritual, he therefore says that he gives thanks to Christ, because the appearance of his majesty strengthened the feebleness of his mind; for he thought impossible what was preached about the power of the Lord, and his twofold birth, and resurrection, and hope. But once strengthened, he was seen to be such a one as was worthy and able to be a steward of the mystery of the Lord; so as to confirm as true through the Law the things which before, out of love of the Law, he judged incredible, and to rejoice in the persecutions which before he inflicted, as if justly, on believers, grieving because he had done it, glad because he was suffering. For he had learned so great a hope in him whom before he denied that he rejoiced more in suffering than he had before exulted in persecuting. For he did not do this out of malice, but out of error, which can easily be pardoned when a man comes to his senses. But he who knows that the things he denies are true does this not out of error but out of malevolence; his wound is incurable; for he is driven by envy to call false what he knows to be true.

(1 Tim. 1:15–16) A humane saying, and worthy of all acceptance, that Christ Jesus came into this world to save sinners, of whom I am the first. But for this reason I obtained mercy, that in me first Christ Jesus might show all magnanimity and patience, for an example to those who are to believe in him, unto eternal life. What is so welcome and so pleasant as to preach pardon to sinners? For who is without sin, that he should not receive this gift with all thanksgiving, setting God before him as its author and praising him in Christ: who, to wash man clean of sins, descending from heavenly things to earthly, took the flesh of sin; mingled himself with earthly things, to make him heavenly; suffered himself to die, to restore him, rescued from death, immortal to paradise? Who would not extol without end these benefits which God has bestowed on man, the Lord on his servant, the Maker on his creature; so that he was greater still in his benefits to men, after he had made them, than when he made the things that were not? For, abandoned by them, and despised, and assailed with insults, he added to his benefits without being asked, that he might be more welcome and more to be preferred in restoring than when he made; to show that he is the one who can do all these things. So then Christ brought help to man in such a way as neither to violate his own purity nor to lose his power. For he willed to be born a man, holding it for certain that he would in no way be violated by the contact of the flesh; he mingled himself with the human way of life, knowing that he could not be corrupted by sins; he suffered himself to be killed, not unaware that death could not lord it over him, but that he would crush it by his own power. These things, therefore, he did to teach that he can do all things. For this humility does not tend to his diminishment but to his glory: and not only his, but also that of those who believe in him; for it is a dignity to be the servant of one who is powerful. Though before the Apostle many of the Jews had certainly believed, of those who killed the Lord himself, yet the Apostle, to humble himself, still grieving over his unbelief, calls himself the first of all sinners. For since beyond the others he steeped himself in the mystery of the Savior, coming nearer to knowing his magnificence, he accuses himself the more, as one who recognized so great a good late, and not of his own accord, but forestalled by his mercy, so that he himself should be a witness to the divinity of him whom before he reckoned a dead man. This is his accusation of himself, but the praise of the Savior, who, for an example to the other believers, called his persecutor to grace; so that through his patience and magnanimity in him he, who had shown himself so benevolent to an enemy, might show the others what he would be to those who run to him.

(1 Tim. 1:17) Now to the King of the ages, immortal and invisible, to the only God be honor and glory for ever and ever, amen. These words pertain to the person of God the Father, whom he therefore calls alone immortal and invisible, because all immortality is from him, so that it belongs in truth rather to him alone; for whoever is immortal has it not from himself but from him. In a similar way it is read in the Gospel too, the Lord saying: That no one is good but the one God (Mark 10:18). And again he himself says: A good man out of a good treasure brings forth good things (Matt. 12:35). Yet no one is good but the one God, because he himself is the fountain of goodness, from which the rest are given to drink so that they may be good. He is also the only invisible God, because he has never been seen by anyone; for he does all things through the Son, whom we therefore say was seen; so that we signify that, although otherwise than he is, he nevertheless appeared. He therefore proclaims honor and glory in truth to God the Father, by whose godliness and providence Christ freed sinners from death.

(1 Tim. 1:18–20) This precept I commend to you, son Timothy, according to the prophecies that went before concerning you, that by them you may wage the good warfare, having faith and a pure conscience, which some, thrusting it away, have made shipwreck concerning the faith: among whom are Hymenaeus and Alexander, whom I have handed over to Satan, that they may learn not to blaspheme. After he has recalled what and how great are the benefits of God the Father and of Christ his Son towards sinful men, whom, though they deserved to be punished, he not only pardoned but also justified and adopted as sons for himself, he shows in what follows in what order he should imbue the people with ecclesiastical discipline, admonishing holy Timothy, mindful of his election, to fulfil that to which he was predestined. For he was predestined when he was taken up by the Apostle for this, to be ordained, judged worthy to be a future bishop, that he might fulfil the warfare of the Gospel in faith and a pure conscience; for he who teaches faithfully cannot have a bad conscience. For he displays the faith of his preaching then, if he abstains from all things contrary to it; because by the example of good works he provokes many to belief, trusting in the hope of the preaching; for he who spurns faith is without doubt of a bad conscience. For he is proclaimed either a hypocrite or an open blasphemer, as were Hymenaeus and Alexander, who, deserting the faith, became shipwrecked, that is, stripped of the truth, or deprived of life. For what is truth, if not life? He says that he has handed them over to Satan, that they might be amended on account of their blasphemy. Now this handing over is this: the Apostle, moved by their blasphemies, pronounced sentence on them; and the devil, who is ready for this, to receive into his power those who are turned away from God, having heard the sentence, seizes them; so that they might understand that they were bound with punishments for this cause, because they had blasphemed. Hence it is that in the Gospel it was said among other things: now sin no more, lest something worse befall you (John 5:14), showing that on account of sin infirmities are sometimes inflicted on bodies. And that these are done with the devil as minister, the Lord declared, saying among many things: But this woman, since she is a daughter of Abraham, whom Satan had bound for eighteen years, ought she not to be loosed from the bond on the sabbath day (Luke 13:16)?

Chapter II

(1 Tim. 2:1–4) I exhort therefore, first of all, that entreaties, prayers, petitions and thanksgivings be made for all men, for kings, and for all who are placed in a higher station; that we may lead a calm and quiet life in all godliness and chastity. For this is good and acceptable before God our Savior, who wills all men to be saved and to come to the knowledge of the truth. This is the ecclesiastical rule, handed down by the teacher of the Gentiles, which our priests use, that they make supplication for all, entreating for the kings of this world, that they may hold the nations in subjection, so that, set in peace, we may be able to serve our God in tranquillity of mind and in quiet. Praying also for those to whom high power has been entrusted, that they may govern the commonwealth in righteousness and truth, with an abundance of goods supplied, so that, the disturbance of sedition being removed, gladness may follow; for bread strengthens the heart, and wine gladdens the mind (Ps. 103:15). Petitioning, moreover, for those who are in various need, that, rescued and set free, they may praise God as the author of their safety. Rendering thanksgivings also for the things which are bestowed on us daily by the providence of God for our life; so that in all these God the Father may be praised, from whom are all things (1 Cor. 8:6), and his Son, through whom are all things; so that, all that is hostile and inimical to this empire being laid to rest, we may be able to serve God in the disposition of godliness and chastity; for disturbance and captivity preserve neither godliness nor chastity. For how is he godly who is naked? And how chaste, when he is not in his own power? So that these things may be preserved, then, quiet is necessary; so that services pleasing to God may be rendered, who wills all men to be saved and to know the truth. This is, he says, eternal life, that they may know you, the only true God, and Jesus Christ whom you have sent (John 17:3). If God, who is certainly called almighty, wills all men to be saved, is his will not fulfilled? But in every utterance a sense and a condition lies hidden; hence the apostle Peter says: Every Scripture needs interpretation (2 Pet. 1:20). For God wills all men to be saved, but if they come to him; for he does not so will it that the unwilling should be saved: but he wills them to be saved if they themselves also will it. For certainly he who gave the law to all excepted no one from salvation. Does not a physician set himself up in public precisely to show that he wishes to save all, provided, however, that he is sought out by the sick? For it is not true salvation if it is bestowed on one who is unwilling: nor can he rejoice in the salvation he has received who received the medicine against his will, if that can even happen; not to say that the medicine cannot have its effect unless the sick man has applied his mind to it; because this medicine is not bodily but spiritual, and it profits neither the doubtful nor the unwilling. For it is faith that gives salvation, and unless the mind has received it with its whole will, it will not only profit nothing, but will even do harm. For the grace of faith has this power: that it pours a divine remedy into those devoted to it, but on the undevoted it confers a disease through which the whole man perishes.

(1 Tim. 2:5) For there is one God, and one mediator of God and men, the man Christ Jesus. He signifies that the will of God and of Christ is one in saving men; hence he confesses that God the Father, because all authority is from him, is one, and that the one mediator of God and men is Christ Jesus. For, sent by the Father and made arbiter, he reconciled God and men, by reforming them to the knowledge of him; for since the Father and the Son are one, not in person but by the indistinction of their nature, the Son of God, in order to be the arbiter of God and men, took flesh and was born a man; so that the mediator of God and men might be the man Christ Jesus, that is, not without divinity; because the man was in God, and God in the man: and so that he might be mediator from both, and might reconcile both, he did this first in himself. For one who guards the unity of God could not be called mediator of God and men as though there were another God who should call man, at odds with God, back into favor: but a man who would not transgress the law that was given was made arbiter between him who sinned and him who knew not how to sin; so that the latter might pardon, and man might for the rest remain in the faith of God.

(1 Tim. 2:6–7) Who gave himself as a redemption for all, whose testimony was given in its own times, in which I was appointed a preacher and apostle (I speak the truth, I do not lie), a teacher of the Gentiles in faith and truth. Since man, though reconciled to God, was still liable to the death of the underworld, so that he might be able to ascend to God, to whom he had been reconciled, the Savior, in order to bring his arbitration to effect, suffered himself to die against right; so that, descending to the underworld, he might condemn death for the impiety it had committed against him, taking away from them those whom it held; so that for the rest whoever had his sign could not be held by it. He showed the testimony of this at the time when he rose; for the destruction of death is the resurrection of the dead. This hope this Apostle of the Gentiles was sent to preach, so that they might learn from him what hope there was in Christ; for this is why he was called teacher, that he might teach faith and truth.

(1 Tim. 2:8) I will therefore that men pray in every place, lifting up holy hands without anger and disputing. He admonishes that there ought to be a peaceable mind in prayer, so that the prayer may be brought to effect; for hands are holy when the heart is clean. Yet he commands that one pray in every place in such a way that a fitting place be understood, certainly not an unsuitable or filthy place; since indeed the Lord forbids praying in public: but he admonishes this, lest they think that one should pray only in the church (Matt. 6:5–6).

(1 Tim. 2:9–10) Likewise also women in ordered attire, with modesty and purity, adorning themselves not with twisted hair, or gold, or pearls, or costly clothing: but, as befits women professing godliness, through good works. He admonishes that women ought to pray in humble attire, not in ostentation; for one who wishes to be heard ought to bow himself down, with pomp put away from him, so as to call forth the mercy of God. For proud attire neither obtains what it asks nor causes right things to be believed of the wearer. For who among the prudent would not shudder at a woman ostentatiously adorned? How much more God, her author, who sees the body that was made free by him bound up in metals! For humble attire befits a good profession, so that one may be reckoned nothing other than what is seen: but she who prefers to be seen with pomp in the house of God attains what she wants, certainly not on account of God but on account of men, namely that she should appear glorious, while she will attain nothing from God but a stain. For the more splendid she appears to men, the more she is despised by God.

(1 Tim. 2:11–12) Let a woman learn in silence with all subjection. For it is not permitted to a woman to teach, nor to have dominion over a man, but to be in silence. He commands that a woman ought not only to have humble attire, but also that authority should be denied her and that she should be made subject to the man; so that both in attire and in obedience she may be under the power of the man, from whom she draws her origin.

(1 Tim. 2:13–15) For Adam was created first, then Eve: and Adam was not seduced, but the woman, being seduced, came to be in transgression (Gen. 1:27). But she will be saved through the generation of children, if she remains in faith and love, and holiness with purity (Gen. 3:6). He ranks the man above the woman because he was created first; so that the woman is inferior, because she was created after the man and from the man. He adds another point too, that the devil seduced not the man but the woman, while the man was deceived through the woman: and for this reason no boldness is to be granted to her, but she ought to be in humility; because through her death entered into the world. She will be saved, he says, through the generation of children; if, however, she remains in faith, and love, and a pure life. For he calls those children who are regenerated through faith in Christ; for if these persevere in their generation, the woman will be set free with them, rising from the dead, not that she will be set free by them, but through their generation; for when those who are destined to eternal life believe, the resurrection from the dead is reached, and she will be saved, as they believe and lead a chaste life. For the resurrection is held in suspense for so long, until all who are predestined to salvation believe.

Chapter III

(1 Tim. 3:1) A humane saying: if anyone desires the episcopate, he desires a good work. Since a bishop ought to be of good life, therefore whoever longs for the episcopate desires a good work. For in longing for this he is seen to seek a good life; provided, however, that he does not turn aside from the things that are set down in what follows, lest he be seen to have done this out of ambition or desire for money.

(1 Tim. 3:2) For a bishop must be irreproachable. He describes what sort of man a bishop ought to be, so that no one should seek the episcopate unless he is faithful, and one who is able to abstain from all things contrary to it, so that he may not be unworthy of his profession. The husband of one wife. Although it is not forbidden to have a wife who is second in number, yet for anyone to be worthy of the episcopate he ought to spurn even lawful things because of the sublimity of that order itself; for he who is desirous of that see ought to be better than the rest.

(1 Tim. 3:3–4) Sober, chaste, orderly, hospitable, teachable, not given to wine; not a striker, but modest; not quarrelsome, not harsh; but ruling his own house well, having his children in subjection with all chastity. He says that a man who keeps these good things which he enumerates ought to be made bishop. For these are the signs of the episcopal dignity. But if anyone, pursuing the better things, has dedicated both his body and his soul to God, so as not to join himself in marriage, he will become so much the more worthy. For he set down the lower thing for this reason, that no one might be in doubt about the better.

(1 Tim. 3:5) But if anyone does not know how to rule over his own house, how will he apply diligent care to the Church of God? It is plain that a man can be proved fit to be a future ruler only then, if he has first governed his own house rightly; for whoever is faithful in the least things is faithful also in great ones (Luke 16:10).

(1 Tim. 3:6) Not a neophyte, lest, lifted up into pride, he fall into the judgment of the devil. It is true that those who are raw in the faith are apt to be carried away by pride, especially if they receive an order; for such a man is puffed up by novelty and by power, thinking himself more worthy than the rest. For seeing an honour conferred on him in the first year of his birth, he thinks that he has been called not so much for his own advancement as for that of others, as though he were giving a benefit to religion rather than receiving one from it; and lifted up by this, he runs into the snare of the devil. For having found the occasion of his pride, Satan casts him down headlong. And therefore it is read in Solomon: God resists the proud (Prov. 3:34). Hence the same Apostle also says in another epistle: Whoever thinks himself to be something, when he is nothing, deceives himself (Gal. 6:3). And the Lord: Whoever, he says, exalts himself will be humbled (Luke 14:11).

(1 Tim. 3:7) But he must also have good testimony from those who are outside. Following the mind of the Lord, he says that the future bishop must have good testimony from outsiders; because whoever is faithful in the least things is so also in great ones, as the Lord says. And if you have not been faithful in another's mammon, who will give you what is your own? So that there may be no doubt that this man, who is found by no means faithful in worldly matters and in human conduct, will not be fit in divine things either; but if he has kept faith with men, much more will he with God. Lest he fall into reproach and the snare of the devil. If a bishop does not have the fear of God before his eyes, but rather accommodates himself to the times, God abandons him as an unbeliever and a false prophet, and he will appear disfigured and stained to all; because he does otherwise than he preaches, accusing himself by his own words, because he has been assigned to the flock of Satan; for he proves that he preaches true things only then, if he also tries to carry them out. Therefore he who receives the house of God to rule must be chosen with great care; for if fit stewards of earthly things are to be sought, how much more of heavenly ones!

(1 Tim. 3:8–10) Deacons likewise chaste, not double-tongued, not given to much wine, not occupied with shameful gains, holding the mystery of the faith in a pure conscience. And let these also be proved first, and so let them minister without crime. He who gives such great precepts about choosing deacons, who are known to be the ministers of the priests, what sort of men does he wish bishops to be, if not, as he himself says, irreproachable, having no public business, no shameful and base gains? For he knows that men engage in trade for this reason, to get gains, and that they profess nothing other than what they do. But he says that these are shameful gains, if under a godly profession one is eager for gain; for such a man is found out as shameful, since he makes a show of being pure. Yet after the bishop he has subjoined the ordination of the diaconate. Why, if not because the ordination of bishop and presbyter is one? For each is a priest, but the bishop is first; so that every bishop is a presbyter, yet not every presbyter a bishop; for he is bishop who is first among the presbyters. Thus he indicates that Timothy was ordained a presbyter; but because he had no other above him, he was bishop. Hence he also shows how he is to ordain a bishop; for it was neither right nor permitted that an inferior should ordain a greater; for no one gives what he has not received.

(1 Tim. 3:11) The women likewise chaste, not eager for discord, but sober, faithful in all things. Since he commands that a holy man be made bishop, and equally a deacon, he certainly does not wish the people to be unlike them; since the Lord says: Be holy, because I also am holy (Lev. 19:2). And therefore he wishes even the women, who seem inferior, to be without crime, so that the Church of God may be clean. But the Cataphrygians, seizing an occasion of error, because after the deacons he addresses the women, maintain with vain presumption that they too ought to be ordained deaconesses; though they know that the apostles chose seven deacons. Was no woman found suitable then, when we read that there were holy women among the eleven apostles? But in order that the heretics may seem to build up their opinion from the words, not from the sense, of the Law, they strive with the words of the Apostle against the sense of the Apostle; so that, whereas he commands that a woman ought to be in silence in the Church, they on the contrary claim for them even the authority of ministry in the Church.

(1 Tim. 3:12–13) Let deacons be husbands of one wife, ruling their children well, and their own houses; for those who have ministered well acquire for themselves a good standing, and much confidence in the faith which is in Christ Jesus. The things he had said less fully about the ordination of the diaconate he now subjoins. He shows that they too ought to be husbands of one wife, so that those may be chosen for the ministry of God who have not gone beyond what God has established. For God decreed one wife for a man, with whom he is blessed; for no one is blessed with a second. If they govern their children well, and their houses, that is, their home-born slaves or their household servants, they will be able to become worthy of the priesthood, and to have confidence before God, so that they may know that they can obtain what they ask, restraining themselves henceforth from the use of a woman. For to the Levites or priests of old it was granted for this reason to have wives for use, because for a long time they were at leisure from the ministry or the priesthood (for there was a multitude of priests, and a great abundance of Levites, and each served the divine ceremonies at a fixed time according to the institution of David (1 Chron. 6:31–32); for he established twenty-four classes of priests, that they might serve in turns: whence Abijah had the eighth class, in whose turn Zechariah performed the priesthood, as is contained in the book of Chronicles (1 Chron. 23:4 ff.)), so that at the time when it did not fall to them to serve the altar, they took care of their houses; but when the time of their ministry drew near, having been purified for some days, they came to the temple to offer to God. But now there must be seven deacons, and some presbyters, so that there may be two in each church, and one bishop in a city; and for this reason all ought to abstain from intercourse with a woman, because they must be present in the Church every day, and have no interval in which to be lawfully purified after intercourse, as the men of old had. For the offering must be made every week, even if not daily for strangers, yet for residents at least twice in the week; though there is no lack of those who are baptized almost daily when sick. For it was granted to the men of old for this reason, because for a long time they were not seen in the temple, but were private persons. For if he commands common people to abstain for a time for the sake of prayer, so that they may be free for prayer, how much more Levites or priests, who must pray day and night for the people entrusted to them! They ought therefore to be cleaner than the rest, because they are God's agents.

(1 Tim. 3:14–15) These things I write to you, son Timothy, hoping to come to you shortly; but if I delay, that you may know how you ought to conduct yourself in the house of God, which is the Church of the living God, the pillar and firmament of the truth. As far as his own knowledge went, the case was that he would see him quite soon; but because something else is wont to please God, whose providence is known to him alone, therefore, lest perhaps, he says, I delay, I write to you so that you may know how to order the Church, which is the house of God; so that, while the whole world is God's, yet the Church is called his house, whose ruler today is Damasus. For the world is in transgression, troubled by diverse error; therefore the house of God, and the truth, must be said to be there where he is feared according to his will. When his servants professed concerning him what he himself had deigned to teach, lest perhaps it be denied that the words were his, he sealed these things with mighty works which can be done by no one else; so that unbelief, convicted by them, may perish without excuse. For the signs and wonders are the firmament of this truth.

(1 Tim. 3:16) And indeed, by the confession of all, great is the mystery of this godliness. He denies that any of the faithful fails to marvel at the mystery of the truth preached in the Church, which he calls godliness; for to those who acknowledge his magnanimity and clemency he grants eternal and divine gifts, besides the remission of sins, which he bestows here in the present. Which was declared in the flesh, justified in the spirit, seen by angels, preached to the Gentiles, believed in this world, taken up in glory. He says that the mystery of God's mystery, which is Christ in the flesh, has been made manifest, which lay hidden in the spirit. For the Son of God, born a man, who was in secret with God, declared himself to the creation by work and preaching; and this is to have been justified in the Spirit: that it became clear by his works and power who he was who was in the flesh; and then that he appeared to angels, no longer in the form of lowliness, but in power. For he was seen lowly in aspect through the flesh only as long as it was until, death having been vanquished, he should rise from the dead, and the majesty of him should be seen who, born as a man, was not wholly man; so that the angels, recognizing the mystery which before lay hidden, declared in the flesh, bending the knee to him as to God, might give thanks, because they have learned the truth which was preached to the Gentiles, that they might be freed from error; in which the world makes progress, so that it may not be condemned. It was believed by the world. For the mystery of God was given to the world to be believed for this reason, that its faithlessness might not be able to merit pardon for its error, as the Lord says of the Jews: If I had not come and spoken to them, they would not have sin; but now they will have no excuse for their sin (John 15:22).

Taken up in glory. Because what they despised as weak and unsightly was taken up in glory; for after the cross, his person and his power having been made manifest, the Savior openly ascended in glory into the heavens, with the clouds serving him.

Chapter IV

(1 Tim. 4:1–5) But the Spirit says plainly that in the last times some will depart from the faith, giving heed to deceitful spirits and doctrines of demons, in the pretence of speakers of falsehood, having a cauterized conscience, forbidding to marry and to abstain from foods, which God created to be received with thanksgiving by the faithful and by those who have known the truth; because every creature of God is good, and nothing is to be rejected that is received with thanksgiving. For it is sanctified through the word of God and prayer. What the Holy Spirit revealed to him as to come, for the instruction and caution of the churches, he does not keep silent, so that the churches, being forewarned, may be watchful lest they be circumvented by men of this kind. For what could he have foretold with a plain voice so clear and so open, for avoiding or rather condemning the men of this pestilent doctrine, than to call their assertion deceitful, and a feigned doctrine of demons; so that, when the words of this assertion are heard, it might be known that this doctrine was composed by the devil, the doctrine which the Holy Spirit had foretold, which asserts false things about the incarnation of the Savior, and which is now marked in the Marcionites, although they have all but failed, in the Patricians, or above all in the Manichaeans (On the Patricians: Philaster, and Augustine, heresy 61)? For these both deny that Christ was born, and forbid marriage, and teach that one must abstain from foods. Their conscience he calls cauterized, that is, corrupted by pretence; because as a cautery spoils the hide and inflicts a brand, so too deceit marks out for perdition the conscience which, through the guile of malevolence, knows one thing and professes another. For he says that these men are no longer changed so as to be saved; because as a cautery cannot be changed, so neither can their mind be amended; for nothing is worse than pretence. For they are such as the Jews also were, those who, though they understood that Christ worked by the power of God, said that these things were done by him in Beelzebub. For in order to treat the maker of man ill, though they are not sorry to have been born, they nevertheless forbid marriage, and take away flesh from Christ; and to advance in doing him injury, they teach that one must abstain from the things he instituted for our uses, so that, by the very fact that these are despised, they may seem to be evil and devised by an evil author. Hence it is that the Apostle shows that all things were made good by God, provided they are taken with thanksgiving; for they will be unclean to him who does not praise the Creator in them. For those things cannot do good to one who takes them in such a way as to treat their author ill.

(1 Tim. 4:6) Setting forth these things, therefore, to the brethren, you will be a good minister of Jesus Christ, nourished in the words of faith and of the good doctrine which you have attained. What is so true, what so clear and provident, as that the Christian discipline be handed on uncorrupted, so that its fruit may be praised as fertile and firm on the Lord's threshing-floor; that is, that on the day of judgment it may not be judged reprobate and fit to be burned with fire? This is the part of a good teacher and of a fit minister of our Lord Jesus Christ; since he says that he was nourished in the words of faith and of good doctrine, which he declares he attained from infancy. For he who had been thoroughly imbued with all the books of the Hebrews, on receiving the faith, made a perfect teacher.

(1 Tim. 4:7–8) But refuse profane and old wives' fables, exercising yourself unto godliness. For bodily exercise is useful for a little, but godliness is useful for all things, having the promise of the present life and of the future. Profane words are understood to be adverse ones; and old wives' fables are certain ravings. These things are wont to be drawn into contention by the voices of certain clamorous men, so that a sort of treatise is held on an empty matter, whose effect is nothing but vanity. He warns, therefore, that one must turn aside from the things that are contrary, and in like manner also from those that are old-womanish, put together out of a certain vanity; for there are some who in the heat of their mind throw themselves into disputations of this kind. But he urges that attention be given to godliness, because it brings great profit. For those who are devoted to mercy, making a return to their aged parents, will both not lack the help of God in the present life, and in the future will have immortality with glory. But bodily exercise he calls useful for a little; for to fast and to abstain from foods, while the authority of the Creator remains intact, profits not much, unless godliness be added to it, whose work is aided by the prayers of many toward winning the favour of the Lord. Thus the saying of the Gospel testifies that those who are generous to the brethren, and dutiful in various ways, advance to eternal life (Matt. 25:41); but those who hold back from this work, to eternal fire. Bodily exercise is nothing other than the reins of the flesh. If anyone, then, since mercy is a great thing, yet gives way to the slipperiness of the flesh, what will become of such a one? Without doubt he will be beaten; because that ought to be done, and this by no means left undone (Luke 11:42). But if he had only bodily exercise, he will suffer everlasting punishments, as the Lord promised in the Gospel (Matt. 25:41); for the whole sum of our discipline lies in mercy and godliness. Yet the carnal have one bodily exercise, and the spiritual another; for the carnal nourish their bodies with fattening and feasts, so that with swift movement they advance to perdition; but the spiritual temper their bodies with fasts and abstinence, so that, made sluggish toward desires, they may be worthy of the resurrection to come. For the one chastises the body because of the hope to come; but the other, through despair of the future, cherishes it, taking care of it for the time being, so that it is moved the more quickly to sin.

(1 Tim. 4:9–10) A faithful saying, and worthy of all acceptance. For to this end we labour and suffer persecutions, because we hope in the living God, who is the giver of salvation to all men, especially to the faithful. It is plainly fitting, and to be counted to our credit, to suffer persecutions for the hope which is in God; because in the present life hope is perishable, but in the promise of God it is firm and stable. For he himself is the salvation of all; because from him and through him all live, as far as the present life is concerned; but as far as the promised salvation is concerned, the faithful. For to those who know him he has promised that, after this life, which is ended by death, he will give another, which will flourish in immortality. Therefore, then, he says: Especially to the faithful.

(1 Tim. 4:11–12) Command these things, and teach. Let no one despise your youth, but be a pattern of the faithful in word, in conduct, in love, in faith, and in chastity. Timothy, as far as his age was concerned, was young; as far as his character and way of life, he was an elder and grave. He admonishes him thus for this reason, that his progress might in all things be an example of good works; so that in a young man there might be seen a discipline wonderful and grave, so that his acts might excuse his age, and he might be held not as a youth but as an elder; and those older in years might blush, unless they showed themselves grave in way of life and character after his pattern; while the young would have one of their own age as their teacher. A wonderful young man was the most holy Timothy, by whose example undisciplined youth is to be restrained, and the irreverence of elders punished.

(1 Tim. 4:13–14) Until I come, attend to reading, exhortation, and doctrine. Do not neglect the grace which is in you, which was given to you through prophecy, with the laying on of hands of the presbytery. He wishes him to be intent upon and devoted to the exercise of reading, so that with all care and diligence he may strive at this, to exhort and to teach. Exhortation is when with a mild word we call some forth to good work; but to teach is when we impart to the minds of the hearers the things that are hidden. For if the ruler of the people turns a blind eye to these things, he neglects the grace given to him; for a leader is made for the people to this end, that he may bear the care of its salvation, by admonishing, by teaching, so that his ordination may bear fruit: for God has appointed him for this very purpose, that he may advance the salvation of his people. Whoever, then, being placed in this authority, is negligent will be held a despiser of God, called least in the kingdom of heaven; for neither will he be able to do, who does not deign even to teach (Matt. 5:19). Yet he signifies that the grace of the ordainer is given through prophecy and the laying on of hands. Prophecy is that by which one is chosen as a fit future teacher; but the impositions of the hand are mystic words, by which the one chosen for this work is confirmed, receiving authority, with his conscience as witness, so that he may dare to offer sacrifice to God in the Lord's stead.

(1 Tim. 4:15–16) Meditate on these things, be in them, that your progress may be manifest to all. Attend to yourself and to doctrine, persist in them. For by doing this you will save both yourself and those who hear you. He admonishes him to be a pattern to all, not only in good works, but also in the exercise of doctrine. For he signifies that the progress of his exercise ought to appear, not in doctrine only, but also in deeds; for a good tree makes good fruits (Matt. 7:17): so likewise good doctrine cannot have evil works; so that when good works have been seen, they may be understood to be the fruits of divine teaching, and the hearers may be able to advance. For they understand that the things said are true and to be feared only then, when they see them observed with great reverence by the very one who teaches. This profits the ruler of the people in two ways: for he will receive the reward both of his own good works and of his hearers'; just as, if he has been negligent, he will have to render account as much for himself as for them.

Chapter V

(1 Tim. 5:1–2) Do not rebuke an elder, but exhort him as a father, younger men as brothers, old women as mothers, young girls as sisters, in all chastity. Out of respect for age he teaches that one older in years is to be summoned to good work with gentleness, so that he may more readily accept the admonition; for, once admonished, he may fear being rebuked afterwards, which is shameful for an elder. For among all nations, certainly, old age is honorable: hence both the Synagogue and afterwards the Church had elders, without whose counsel nothing was done in the Church. Through what negligence this has fallen into disuse I do not know, unless perhaps through the sloth, or rather the pride, of the teachers, since they alone wish to seem to be something. Younger men he judges are to be admonished as brothers, with the affection of love, so that, seeing that they are admonished for love's sake, they may more readily correct themselves, especially since they see that his works do not differ from his preaching. Old women, however, as mothers, so that, while they see themselves taught with gentle speech, they may not take it harshly, since they profit in an honorable way: for it is harsh for elders when they are rebuked by the young. This he wishes to be softened by a certain moderation, so that there may be profit both for the one who speaks and for the one who hears. Young girls he advises are to be taught as sisters, so that they may readily accept the discipline of a good way of life. For when she sees that the things which pertain to a good life are being instilled into her not with command, she consents, showing herself humble to the one who admonishes her. For by blandishments are usually obtained the things that cannot be obtained by authority; for animals, when they are jealous over their own limbs, nevertheless yield to blandishments.

(1 Tim. 5:3) Honor widows who are truly widows. He says those widows are to be honored who are widows of set purpose: who, though they could marry, in order to be better and worthy of God, rejected second marriages, knowing that wedlock is blessed once, imitators of the most holy Anna, who served God with fastings and supplications night and day, having known the bed of one husband only (Luke 2:36).

(1 Tim. 5:4) But if any widow has children or grandchildren, let her learn first to manage her own house with godliness, and to render to her parents a return of gratitude in turn; for this is acceptable before God. He says that the widow is first to be proved in this life and way of life, so that only then may she be worthy to be called a widow of the Church, one who submits to ecclesiastical discipline not for the sake of gain rather than of protection. For merits ought to go before, so that the honor due may be seen to be granted to her when she comes forward; for she who has dealt with her own dear ones with godliness, and has governed her house well, has kept the law of God, and has become worthy of the Church's remuneration.

(1 Tim. 5:5) For she who is truly a widow and desolate hopes in God, and persists in prayers by night and by day. A widow who has children or grandchildren is not readily to be admitted to the stipends of the Church; because if she has governed her house with godliness and care, a return must be made to her by her own, just as she too rendered it to her kin: but if, as commonly happens, impiety has been at work, so that the old woman is despised by her own, whom she treated with godliness, it is fitting that she be taken up by the Church, because she has lived faithfully in the law of the Lord. The widow, therefore, who is desolate and despised by her own, hopes in God; for, seeing that she has support from no quarter, she is devoted with her whole mind to God, from whom alone she awaits help for her life and salvation.

(1 Tim. 5:6) For the widow who lives in delights is dead while living. He signifies that a widow, because she is called a widow, ought to be such that she is free for prayers in order to win the favor of the Lord, serving God night and day, so as to show that she despised second marriages for this reason, that she might apply herself to the discipline of the Lord. For if under the name of widow she is free for delights or luxury, she is to be held dead while living; because it is a kind of imposture to do one thing and profess another: such women wish to have not the service and devotion of God but the honor of a widow, whereas the very profession of the name indicates this: that, having spurned a husband, she chose God, to serve him with her whole mind, for the sake of the life that is promised.

(1 Tim. 5:7) And command these things, that they may be irreproachable. He wishes the widow to have such diligence in her acts and conduct that she cannot be reproached; for, removed from the hope of a worldly way of life and from the duty of caring for a body, she has betaken herself to God for this reason, that she may lead an approved life. For when the occasions that usually open the way to sins are taken away, a good way of life can remain unharmed.

(1 Tim. 5:8) But if anyone does not have care of his own, and especially of those of his household, he has denied the faith and is worse than an unbeliever. This widow, who was negligent toward her own dear ones, so as not to bring them up in the discipline and correction of the Lord, he says is not to be honored; because, like the faithless, she has not obeyed the law of the Lord, so that she is held to be worse. For he must needs be worse who, living under the law and the fear of God, is a despiser, than he who is not subject to the law; for this one does not know, but that one despises knowingly.

(1 Tim. 5:9–10) Let a widow be chosen not less than sixty years old, who has been the wife of one husband, having testimony in good works, if she has brought up children, if she has received guests in hospitality, if she has washed the feet of the saints, if she has ministered to those suffering affliction, if she has followed every good work. If a widow is commended by the prerogative of past works, he teaches that she is to be chosen; for by the judgment of the Lord her name is entered in the ecclesiastical records, since her former life declares her worthy. For, steeped in divine services and wearing away her life in them, she is to be received into the bosom of the Church, to which she long ago vowed herself in lawful services.

(1 Tim. 5:11) But avoid young widows; for when they have spent their life in delights, they wish to marry in Christ. He forbids young widows to be received into this profession, for faith ought not readily to be placed in a slippery age; especially since the examples of certain women go before, who after their profession, through the allurement of a life of delights, have the name, not the works, of widowhood, and having turned back are enticed by marriages. For he who thinks that he leads a life in delights in Christ by no means remains innocent; for luxury draws him to its own members, so that he is made subject to sin; for delights are the doors of sin. For it is not unknown where he is heading who loves a dissolute life.

(1 Tim. 5:12) Having condemnation, because they have made void their first faith. He shows what awaits such women, so that they may not readily venture what is hard to fulfill: or, if the mind is given to this devotion, let her keep it to herself for the meantime, because she has learned that it is rash to profess what ought not yet to be entrusted to a youthful age. For he becomes liable to condemnation who turns aside from this, which he had rightly done. For as praiseworthy as it is if one fulfills what is believed to be beyond one's strength, so much the more will one deserve punishment if one dares, through the presumption of rashness, to impose upon oneself what one cannot bear: nor ought he to have any claim to pardon who has been found unfaithful from the very beginning.

(1 Tim. 5:13) And at the same time, being idle, they learn to go about: and not only idle, but also wordy and curious, speaking what they ought not. Certain virgins or widows, recommended by their profession (for the name of this thing is glorious), are welcome in the houses of the rich, and they make it their business to comply with their wishes, walking about and seeking out things to carry to them with which they may be delighted, so that, being idle, they may be rewarded with gifts: and by a bad habit they seek more by tales than by what they work with their hands, so that they go out puffed up and dressed up, acting against their profession. This thing usually turns against them even in this life; for that is revealed which they think will always be hidden. For what does it mean, that they speak the things they ought not? For they are close to marriages, sharers in many secrets, flatterers, complainers about servants.

(1 Tim. 5:14–15) I will therefore that the younger women marry, bear children, be mothers of families, give no occasion to the adversary for the sake of reviling. For already some have strayed after Satan. Since what he said above about such women is contrary and unlawful, and will lead them to death, for that reason he gives counsel and commands that those things be done which are lawful. For it is better to have the care of one's own house than to commit adultery in another's house: and it is far more expedient to marry than, under a good and godly profession, to walk notoriously in evil: and it is a greater thing to seek one's living with one's hands in modesty than shamelessly to wait in idleness for other people's goods. For thus Satan finds an occasion for overthrowing ill-advised souls, when those who profess godly things conduct themselves unjustly; for nothing is so dangerous as when deeds contradict a profession. For Satan is not jealous of open evil, because he rejoices in it: but this one whom he sees deceiving, he works to make manifest as his own son, not God's: which deed does not displease God.

(1 Tim. 5:16) If any faithful man or any faithful woman has widows, let him minister to them sufficiently, so that the Church is not burdened with their maintenance, so that it may suffice for the true widows. What wonder if Apostolic power could foresee the future? For what he said is plain to see, since the widows of the faithful now greatly burden the Church, and of those who seem rich in the world; because, when the women cease from the work of wool-making on account of old age, they withdraw from them both clothing and food, not all of it, however, but in part, so that through that very want they may turn to the Church in order to live: whereas they ought not only to give generously to their own needy but also to feed and clothe other poor women. This does much harm; for when it is given to these, to the rest either less is given, or nothing, though it ought rather to be given to them, who are worthy to be ministered to at the Church's expense. And thus it is burdened, when it is compelled to clothe many, though it ought to clothe few: and the people, which could gladly and often minister to a few, is compelled to let more go, so that it either murmurs or comes to their aid more slowly. For whereas he commands that a widow be chosen who was the wife of one husband, now there are found among them not only wives of two or three husbands, but even women who have lived more shamefully: which sometimes happens through negligence, sometimes through the power of the one who introduces them.

(1 Tim. 5:17) Let the presbyters who rule well be held worthy of double honor: especially those who labor in the word and teaching. Good and faithful stewards ought to be judged worthy not only of exalted honor but also of earthly honor, so that they may not be saddened by want of means, but rather rejoice in their faith and teaching. For he becomes more earnest if he is not humbled by poverty: and authority grows in him when he sees that even in the present he receives the fruit of his labor; not so that he may abound, but so that he may not be in want.

(1 Tim. 5:18) For Scripture says: You shall not bridle the ox that treads out the grain (Deut. 25:4); and: The workman is worthy of his wage (Luke 10:7). The wage of one who preaches the kingdom of God must be such that by it he is neither saddened nor puffed up.

(1 Tim. 5:19) Do not receive an accusation against a presbyter. Since the honor of this order is exalted (for such men are vicars of Christ), for that reason an accusation against this person ought not readily to be admitted. For it ought to seem incredible that this man, who is a prelate of God, has conducted himself criminally, just as it is credible that an actor is utterly base.

(1 Tim. 5:20) But those who sin rebuke before all, that the rest may have fear. Although a charge against a presbyter ought not readily to be believed, yet if it is proved, or it is manifest that he has conducted himself irreverently, he commands that he be rebuked publicly, so that the rest too may be terrified; and this profits not only the ordained but also the people; for when they see a man of rank rebuked because of his error, they must needs take heed for themselves.

(1 Tim. 5:21–22) I testify before God, and Christ Jesus, and the elect angels, that you keep these things, doing nothing without prior judgment, inclining to the other side. Lay hands hastily on no one, nor share in other men's sins. Keep yourself chaste. He lays down his testimony before God the Father, and Christ his Son, and his elect angels, whom the Lord in the Gospel calls holy (Mark 8:38). These, therefore, are elect, because there are others, reprobate, who are not God's angels but the devil's, assenting to his apostasy. Under this testimony, therefore, he orders those things to be kept which pertain to the ordering of the Church, and commands that nothing be done without prior judgment: lest anyone readily receive ecclesiastical dignity unless his life and conduct have first been examined, so that, approved as worthy, he may be appointed minister or priest; so that the chief of the people may not afterwards, if the man is seen to be blameworthy, perhaps regret that he did not judge beforehand with clarity what he deserved, whom he ordained without due approval; nor is one whose sins come under suspicion to be ordained, lest the ordainer be stained by his vices and offenses. If, therefore, the offenses are entirely hidden, the ruler will be free of blame, because his conscience is free. For suspicion makes one fearful; for he sins if he does not test the man, and ordains him so. For he who is to be ordained ought to be proved better than the rest; for it is not enough if he is without crime, because the merits of his good works ought to go before him, so that he may be held worthy of ordination. A bishop who keeps these things will show himself chaste for religion: and for this he will obtain a reward in the future.

(1 Tim. 5:23) Do not drink water any longer, but use a little wine for your stomach's sake and your frequent infirmities. Now he gives a special counsel, that he should govern himself by wholesome teaching; for God wishes to be served prudently, not so that men become weak by their own excess and afterwards seek the help of physicians. For one must use moderation, so that, if it can be done, the service begun may be advanced step by step rather than diminished through want of consideration. For intemperance makes the soul itself restless, so that, when it is anxious about infirmity, it is not so wholly given to divine services. For improvidence brings loss; the soul set in tranquillity lifts the whole heart up to God, entreating with undivided counsel the things that are to be asked.

(1 Tim. 5:24–25) The sins of some men are manifest, going before to judgment, but some they also follow after. In like manner good works also are manifest: and those that are otherwise cannot be hidden. He wishes it to be understood that there will be no excuse for those who cling to men whose sins are openly known, so that, when they ought to be avoided, they are rather held in honor. This, therefore, he signifies: that the man of God should separate himself from these, especially the steward of Christ, who also has the authority to rebuke them; and if they promise to amend themselves, they are to be retained. But he shows that the evil works of others follow after them; because while living they are reckoned good, but when dead they are uncovered, so that those who earlier showed themselves fit are afterwards declared impostors. By the reasoning above, just as evil deeds, so also good deeds are manifest; for a good life and modest acts cannot be hidden, which, when godliness is joined to them, make men perfect: but just as some evils are hidden for the present, so also are some good things; for no one can know everything in men, but from what he sees and hears he tests and judges. For he weighs whether the things that are said fit him of whom they are said, whether good or bad: but whatever has been hidden from men will become manifest on the day of judgment. For this reason, therefore, these things were said, that such men may know that they have a graver case than the rest. For he is worse who, though he is evil, shows himself good, than he who is openly evil.

Chapter VI

(1 Tim. 6:1) Let as many slaves as are under the yoke count their masters worthy of all honor, lest the name of the Lord and the teaching be blasphemed. He wishes that through the services of their slaves the masters should give thanks to God; for when through the discipline of God the slaves have shown them faithful service, perhaps the masters too will subject themselves to this same discipline.

(1 Tim. 6:2) But those who have faithful masters, let them not despise them, because they are brothers: but let them serve them the more, because they are faithful and beloved, who receive the benefits. If he commands that profane masters are to be served with all solicitude, how much more the faithful! For a man proves himself subject to the fear of God then, if he has obeyed a faithful and temporal master with his whole mind. Let him not think that his master is to be despised because he is called a brother on account of the common faith; because he will be the more deserving of rebuke who has thought a faithful master fit to be despised.

(1 Tim. 6:3) Teach and exhort these things. He wishes the teaching of the Lord to be made manifest, so that its detractors may see that there is nothing in it to be reproached; and that, considering at the same time what shameful things lie hidden in their own rites, they may also blush, through which they will be without excuse.

(1 Tim. 6:4–5) If anyone teaches otherwise, and does not acquiesce in the sound words of our Lord Jesus Christ, and in the teaching that is according to godliness: he is proud, knowing nothing, but sick about questions and fights of words, from which arise envies, contentions, blasphemies, evil suspicions, conflicts of men who are corrupted in mind and who have been without the truth, supposing godliness, and the worship of God, to be gain; withdraw from such men. He commands that those who dissent from the catholic discipline are to be spurned, especially those who, though convicted and corrected, refuse to acquiesce through the disturbance of a depraved mind, who delight and feed on contentions. For the proud man does not suffer himself to be overcome, seeing that he bursts into conflict to seek favor, which is to be avoided by gentle and good men. For contention is never without strife, and it would be a small matter if strife alone arose; but blasphemies and enmities arise too, which is foreign to the servants of God. And to keep away from those who, corrupted in mind, are deprived of the truth is judged useful; for they suppose the worship of God and godliness to be gain, hoping for nothing of the future, but providing for how they may get through the present, like the worldly sects too, which are known to have been invented for the sake of present gains; they are not unaware that some gladly hear this, that one must flourish in this life; and therefore one must withdraw from such men.

(1 Tim. 6:6) But godliness with sufficiency is great gain. He says that there are great riches and great acquisition in this, if one of the faithful is content with what is his; for he is godly both in himself and towards others who, acquired by the mercy of God for the life to come, thinks that what he has suffices him for the present, not hesitating to give generously from that very thing. For every avaricious man is ungodly, harming both himself and others.

(1 Tim. 6:7–8) For we brought nothing into the world, and truly we cannot take anything away; but having food, and that with which we may be covered, let us be content with these. He declares with open voice that mortal men strive in vain after plenty and wish in vain to become rich, since they know that they have nothing of their own in the world. To what does human greed profit, except that it stands as its own enemy? For he brought nothing into the world; he will be able to take nothing away. Why does he strike anxiety into himself and seek to amass? He will go out from here such as he came. This Job too writes, and confesses in his suffering, saying: Naked I came out of my mother's womb, naked I shall return to the earth.... blessed be the name of the Lord (Job 1:21). But if the avaricious did not hoard to their own ruin, a few would lay up what could profit many, to the advantage of those very men as well.

(1 Tim. 6:9–10) For those who wish to become rich fall into temptation and a snare, and into many useless and harmful desires, which plunge men into ruin and destruction. For the root of all evils is avarice, which some, coveting, have strayed from the faith, and have thrust themselves into many sorrows. Nothing is so harsh and so pernicious as when a churchman, especially one who is in a high place, is eager for the riches of this world; because he harms not only himself but others too. For he gives men a contrary pattern; for many imitators of him must needs arise, to their perdition. For the more honorable he is in rank, the more he persuades others to imitate him, especially in this thing, to which there is a ready slope in this life; for avarice can commit all evils. It is the root of all evils for this reason, that, in order to fulfill its desires, which is impossible, it perpetrates evil deeds, and homicides, and obscenity, and whatever crime there is; it is not secure in the present, because it is always covetous, and in the future it is condemned.

(1 Tim. 6:11) But you, man of God, flee these things. He exhorts and commands the man of God to cut himself off from this, so that he may both be secure here and be rewarded in the future with divine prizes. For not undeservedly is he to be crowned who spurns this thing, which persuades many.

(1 Tim. 6:12) But pursue righteousness, godliness, faith, love, patience, tranquillity of mind. Contend in the good contest of faith, lay hold of eternal life, to which you were also called, and you have confessed the good confession before many witnesses. Since avarice is an iniquitous thing, he bids the man of God, so as to keep apart from it, to pursue righteousness and godliness and the other things he mentions; for he will not be able to avoid it unless he cleaves to these things, which are opposed to avarice. But how can it be that an avaricious man is faithful, who denies by his works what he seems to confess with words? And how can he be a lover of the brotherhood whose hands are dry? And how patient, who always reaches out after what belongs to others? Or how far can he have quiet of mind, who day and night is inflamed with the greed of covetousness? But because this iniquity was foreign to Timothy, the man of God, he is bidden to enter upon the good contest of faith. For the victory of faith is when all vices and crimes are subjugated, so that one may come to the rewards of eternal life: the confession of which, made among the very rudiments of the faith, is contained in the ecclesiastical records, with a witness, as the question is put and answered.

(1 Tim. 6:13–16) I command you in the sight of God, who gives life to all things, and of Christ Jesus, who testified the good confession under Pontius Pilate: that you keep the commandment spotless, irreproachable unto the coming of our Lord Jesus Christ: whom in his own times the blessed and only mighty one will show, the King of kings and Lord of lords, who alone has immortality, and dwells in light inaccessible, whom no man has seen nor can see, to whom be honor and eternal power, amen. With great vigilance and foresight he gives precepts to the ruler of the Church; for in his person the salvation of the whole people consists. He is so circumspect not because he is anxious about Timothy's diligence, but on account of his successors, so that by Timothy's example they might keep the ordering of the Church, and they too, handing on a pattern to those who were to come, might begin from themselves. Thus he says: that you keep the commandment spotless until the coming of our Lord Jesus Christ. This he binds so tightly that he testifies that he commands it before God and Christ; and, to strike terror, he recalls the passion of Christ, of which passion he must needs become guilty who spurns that for which Christ suffered. Nor was he silent about the day of judgment, which will come at a predestined time at the will of God, whom he proclaims alone blessed and mighty, and King of kings and Lord: for he alone is the one who has blessedness and might, dominion and kingdom, and immortality too, as his own and received from no one. All these the Father has, and likewise the Son from the Father: but the inaccessible light is the Father's alone, because he has never appeared to anyone. Thus he added: Whom no man has seen nor can see. Not because the light of the Son is another, since it is written in the psalm: And in your light we shall see light. (Ps. 35:10), that is, that the light of the Father is in the Son: but because no one has seen the Father, and for this reason he dwells in light inaccessible. For it belongs to no one's order to see God the Father, except to Christ: he who is from God, he has seen God: but the Son, because he himself does all things, is said both to appear and to be seen. That, therefore, which was said above is peculiar to the Father: To whom be honor and eternal power, amen; that is, it is true.

(1 Tim. 6:17–19) Command the rich of this world not to be high-minded, nor to hope in the uncertainty of riches, but in the living God, who provides us with all things abundantly to enjoy, in the will for good works; to be rich in good deeds, ready to impart, and willing to share, to treasure up for themselves a good foundation for the future, that they may lay hold of the true life. The true life is that which we hope for in the future, which is ended neither by setting nor by times; for it is eternal, and therefore it is rightly called true: for this present life is an image of life, not the truth. And because an image is not altogether emptiness, that life is acquired in this one: and, so that one may hasten towards it, one learns from the image how far the truth excels it, the truth which is attained neither by pride nor by the hope of riches, but by humility and by hope in God, who promised it. For hope must be placed in him, because he himself provides what is necessary for our uses: not in the riches themselves, but in their author. For what hope is seen in a thing perishable and uncertain? since a good many depart from them: and those who seem to remain in them take no profit from them for the future, but loss, unless they share them with the needy: for they are not eternal, that they should be preferred to the future life. They make men proud just as though they promised immortality. We have the example of resurrection in Christ; and yet by many Christ is not loved as riches are, of which no example is seen that they ever freed anyone from death. Christ redeemed us, not riches; for he himself showed the likeness of the future life, so that, spurning all these things, we may follow him, in order that we may come to immortal and heavenly riches. Therefore we ought to strive for this, that we may be rich in good deeds, and by these earthly riches acquire spiritual riches; let us sow on earth, that we may reap in the heavens, where the eternal treasure is laid up.

(1 Tim. 6:20–21) Timothy, guard what has been entrusted, avoiding profane novelties of words, and the oppositions of knowledge of a deceitful name, which some, professing it, have fallen away concerning the faith. Grace be with you. He admonishes the same things, that what has been said above be kept, and that the things contrary to religion be avoided, which usurp only the name of knowledge, deceitful in their profession, so as to deceive the simple; for they feign the faith from which they have fallen away, so as to bring in faithlessness. And he subscribed with his own hand, saying: Grace be with you.
