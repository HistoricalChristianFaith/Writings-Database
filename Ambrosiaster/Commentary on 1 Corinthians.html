Commentary on 1 Corinthians

Latin source: the commentary on the First Epistle to the Corinthians by the anonymous Roman writer called Ambrosiaster (written at Rome about 366–384), from the Maurist text of 1690, printed under the name of Ambrose in J.-P. Migne, Patrologia Latina 17 (Paris, 1845), cols. 183–276, verified against the page images, collated in full with the Maurist edition (Paris, 1690) and checked against A. López Kindler's Spanish translation of the critical text (CSEL 81/2). At three places the manuscript reading is adopted where Migne's text makes no sense (5:11 "of the unbelievers", 10:19–20 "not only", 15:1–2 "fixed in the faith"), and Migne's misprinted Scripture references and verse labels are corrected. The lemmata are translated from Ambrosiaster's own Old Latin text of Paul, and all other Scripture as he quotes it, from his own Latin; italics mark the lemmata and the other Scripture he quotes. The edition's verse labels are given as (1 Cor. chapter:verse), and Psalms keep the Latin (LXX) numbering. The one Greek phrase is kept as printed, with an English gloss, as is the Aramaic "Maranatha", which he explains himself. This English translation is released into the public domain.

Prologue

Admonished by the Lord's command, the Apostle stayed among the Corinthians one year and six months, teaching the word of God among them. Hence it is that he deals with them with great confidence and with the affection of love, sometimes admonishing, sometimes reproving, sometimes coaxing them, as sons. For there are many causes on account of which he writes to them. The first of these is this: that, disagreeing with one another in the manner of heretics and devoted to men, they wished to be called Paulines, Petrines and Apollonians, not Christians; and this the Apostle strongly rebukes. There were, however, some among them who disagreed with these, dedicated to Christ alone. The second is this: that they had begun to take delight in eloquence and earthly philosophy, so that under the name of Christ they were being imbued with things that are contrary to the faith. The third, because they were puffed up that the Apostle was not coming to them. The fourth, on account of the fornicator whom they were suffering to be among them. The fifth, to remind them of an earlier letter which he had written before this one, which is called the first. The sixth, because they had begun to do wrongs and frauds to one another, and were seeking instead the judgments of unbelievers. The seventh cause is the one in which he shows that it is lawful for him to receive his expenses, but to disdain them, so as not to be a pattern for the rapacious false apostles. The eighth is to answer their letter; for they had begun to be troubled by heretics about marriages. The ninth is the one in which he declares that each one ought to remain as he was when he believed. The tenth, moreover, concerns virgins, because nothing has been commanded him. And there are the remaining causes, which will be seen in the body of the letter.

Chapter I

(1 Cor. 1:1) Paul, called an apostle of Christ Jesus through the will of God. To the Romans he began otherwise, because the case is different; but to these he writes that he is an apostle of Christ Jesus by the will of God, because all that he did, he did by the will of the Son of God, who said to him: Go, for I will send you far away to the Gentiles (Acts 22:21). Hence he is called an Apostle, that is, sent to the Gentiles by the will of God. By this he also touches those whom Christ had not sent, and whose claims were not true. For he names God and Christ frequently for this reason: to show Christ as God, yet not as the Father himself, but that Christ is the Son, and that there is also God the Father, not a union; since he is shown to be called the Son. For many sects had sprung up, which asserted the Gospel of Christ according to the judgment of their own mind, of which withered branches survive even to this day, and whose champions were overturning the churches. Hence the Apostle sets down everything that is contrary to the heresies, and affirms that he is a true preacher through the fact that he was sent by Christ by the will of God.

(1 Cor. 1:2) And Sosthenes, the brother. He commends this man too by associating him with himself. To the church of God which is at Corinth, to those sanctified in Christ Jesus. He writes to the church for this reason, that rulers had not yet been appointed over the individual churches. And though he reproves them in many things, he says: To those sanctified in Christ Jesus. He says it, however, because, reborn in Christ, they were sanctified, but afterwards they had begun to conduct themselves badly; so that he might show that the whole church had been sanctified in Christ, but that some of them had been turned away from the tradition of the truth by the perverse teaching of the false apostles.

To those called saints. That is, you have been called so that you may be saints: which means, that you should not depart from the rule of sanctification. With all who call upon the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, in every place, theirs and ours. That is, with the true Jews he joins the Gentiles too, because salvation is from the Jews; so that in every place where there are Gentiles who call upon the name of our Lord Jesus Christ, and where there are the Jews mentioned above, all are likewise one. For the false apostles, who preached the name of Christ through the prudence of the world, that is, with philosophy mixed in, rejected the old law and the prophets; for they denied that Christ was truly crucified, holding that he only appeared to be crucified, as Marcion and Manichaeus did. Hence the Apostle: But we, he says, preach Christ crucified (1 Cor. 1:23). Nor did they confess the resurrection of the flesh, which the prophet Isaiah proclaims, saying: Those who are in the tombs will rise (Isa. 26:19).

(1 Cor. 1:3) Grace to you and peace from God our Father, and the Lord Jesus Christ. Lest, because he had said: Who call upon the name of our Lord Jesus Christ, he should seem to have passed over in silence either the name or the gift of the Father, and perhaps give rise to suspicion of a union, or occasion for it, he teaches indeed that Christ is rightly to be invoked, but that all grace is the Father's; so that he might both show the two, who are one through the divinity, and give precedence to the authority of the Father.

(1 Cor. 1:4) I give thanks to my God always for you. Although he writes to all in the church, yet he signifies things in such a way, when he sometimes rebukes and sometimes praises, that each one, when the epistle is read, may understand what is said for him and what against him. For within one congregation he writes to two peoples, so that when he reproves, those who conduct themselves badly may know that these things are written to them; and likewise when he praises, he who remains in the rule may know that these things are said to him. And therefore he says:

I give thanks to my God always for you for the grace of God which has been given to you in Christ Jesus. He says that grace has been given by God in Christ Jesus, and this grace was given in Christ Jesus in this way: because it was ordained by God that whoever believes in Christ should be saved without works: by faith alone he receives the remission of sins freely.

(1 Cor. 1:5) That in all things you have been enriched in him, in all speech and in all knowledge. He means this: that in the grace they had received, and in the word of truth, having attained the spiritual knowledge of the teaching, they persevered; and therefore he gives thanks to God for these.

(1 Cor. 1:6) Even as the testimony of Christ has been confirmed in you. The testimony of Christ has been confirmed in them, because, strengthened in faith, they hoped for nothing from men; but all their hope was in Christ, and they were caught by no enticements of pleasure.

(1 Cor. 1:7) So that you lack no grace in anything, as you await the revelation of our Lord Jesus Christ. It is clear that he who is circumspect and careful awaits the day of judgment. He speaks of the future judgment of God, in which our Lord will be revealed to the faithful and the unfaithful alike; so that the unbelieving may recognize and know that what they refused to believe is true, and perish; but the faithful may rejoice, finding more good than they had supposed.

(1 Cor. 1:8) Who will also confirm you to the end without reproach at the coming of our Lord Jesus Christ. With an eager mind, confident of their hope, he is secure, because they will remain unharmed until the future judgment. For those who, amid so many conflicts of opinions and disturbances of differing views, could not be changed at all, show beyond doubt that they will remain in the same state. While he praises these, he invites those who had been corrupted in the error of the false apostles: for while he proclaims the faith of the former, he calls the latter to repentance.

(1 Cor. 1:9) God is faithful, through whom you were called into the fellowship of his Son Jesus Christ our Lord. Who would doubt the promises and the faithfulness of God, that it will not be otherwise than he said, namely that we who believe in Christ are adopted as sons to himself? For he grants us this, which we believe: that, because we believe that Christ is the Son of God, we may begin to be this ourselves, to remain in the same dignity, as Christ remains, whom we believe to be the Son of God. For fellowship is brotherhood; so that, just as he shows that the faithfulness of God will be unimpaired for us in this matter, so we may not be found distrustful and faithless, but persevering in the adoption.

(1 Cor. 1:10) Now I beseech you, brothers, by the name of our Lord Jesus Christ, that you all say the same thing. He now begs all to be of one mind, namely in that in which, reborn, they were called sons of God. Now he speaks to the whole church, so that those who had begun to dissent might return to the faith with which they began, which they saw praised in those who persevered in it.

And let there be no schisms among you, but be perfect in the same mind and in the same knowledge. He wants them to be perfect in the same mind which he had handed on to them, so that they might not disagree. For he spurs these on to the example of those whom he praises above, so that they may hold and defend this.

(1 Cor. 1:11) For it has been reported to me about you, brothers, by those who are of Chloe, that there are contentions among you. Although they had been subverted, he nevertheless calls them brothers; just as in the prophet Isaiah God says to those who were with him that they should say to those who believed the false prophets: You will be our brothers (Isa. 66:5); so that by this they might not separate themselves from them, as being brothers. As for his saying: By those who are of Chloe, to some they seem to be people remaining and bearing fruit in the faith of God; to some, it seems to be a place, as if one were to say: By those who are of Antioch; to others again it seems that she was a woman devoted to God, with whom there were many worshipping God, to whom credence would not be refused; so that what they reported about the Corinthians was true, that there were contentions among them. He said that there were contentions among them in order to show the diversity of their minds concerning the Lord's teaching. And what it was, he added, saying:

(1 Cor. 1:12) Now I say this, that each of you says: I am indeed of Paul, but I of Apollos, and I of Cephas, but I of Christ. He shows the error, but did not reveal the names of its authors; for they did not stay in one place, but went about to overthrow the simple. For those whom he names were without doubt good teachers; but under their guise he touches the false apostles. For if he denies that one should glory in these, how much more in evil teachers, whose perverse doctrine he signifies under the names put in their place! Among them, however, he marks out as persevering those who said that they were of Christ, not of a man, whom he praises above.

(1 Cor. 1:13) Christ is divided. He says that Christ is divided because men have shared out his glory among themselves. Just as the heretics, who do not shrink from being called Photinians, or Arians, or Cataphrygians, or Novatianists, or Donatists, or Manichaeans, so too the Corinthians had begun to subject themselves to the names of various heretics, so that they seemed to venerate men in the place of Christ: and these, while they receive different things about Christ, divide Christ. For one accepts Christ as man only, another confesses him as pure God without a man or a body, another says that Christ was foretold by the prophets, another denies that the prophets spoke of Christ. Since, then, Christ is one, God and man, these men, each claiming a different part of him for himself, divide Christ; and because they make many churches under their own name, he added:

Was Paul crucified for you? He began with himself for this reason, lest it should perhaps be thought that he was rejecting the persons of others in order to commend his own. For if Christ, he says, died for us, how is it that we ascribe his grace and benefit to men, to his injury?

Or were you baptized in the name of Paul? But if, he says, believing in Christ, we are baptized so that we may be justified in his name, why is it that we make men the authors of this faith for ourselves, forgetful of the belief with which we began?

(1 Cor. 1:14–16) I give thanks to God that I baptized none of you except Crispus and Gaius, lest anyone should say that I baptized in my own name. I baptized also the household of Stephanas; beyond that I do not know whether I baptized anyone else. He gives thanks to God that he did not baptize many of them; lest, since the error had begun on this ground, there should be an error among very many arising from his name; by this both the baptizers and the baptized are to be reproved, who, to the injury of the Savior, were ascribing the grace of his baptism to men, the baptizers conniving for the sake of glory. For they were then just as the Novatianists and Donatists are now, who claim baptism for themselves, rejecting those baptized by our people; and those baptized by them glory in their persons. In short, having renounced the name of Christ, they glory in being called Novatianists and Donatists. Crispus, then, and Gaius are Corinthians, whom he names as witnesses, because they were baptized by the Apostle in such a way that they asserted that no glory was to be given to him on that account. The household of Stephanas, moreover, are the firstfruits of Achaia, as he indicates in the last part of the epistle, and to them he bears witness that they had appointed themselves to the ministry for the saints.

(1 Cor. 1:17) For Christ did not send me to baptize, but to preach the gospel. Since it is a greater thing to preach the gospel than to baptize, he therefore says that he was not sent to baptize but to preach the gospel; because in the bishop is the dignity of all the orders; for he is the head of the other members. By this he also humbles those to whom they ascribed much, because they had been baptized by them (Acts 10:48); so that they might know from this that it is not a great thing to baptize, since not everyone who baptizes is fit also to preach the gospel. For the words which are spoken in baptism are set forms. Finally the apostle Peter ordered Cornelius, when he believed, to be baptized with his household, and did not deign to perform this task himself, since ministers were standing by; for if they had been lacking, he would have done it himself, compelled by necessity. So he shows how much better he is than those whom they revered; and yet he did not allow this to be credited to his name, knowing that it is dangerous to claim the glory of God for the name of a man; for it is a kind of idolatry.

Not in wisdom of the word, lest the cross of Christ be made void. Because Christian preaching has no need of pomp and refinement of speech, therefore fishermen, unskilled men, were chosen to preach the gospel; so that the truth of the teaching might commend itself, with power as its witness; lest it should seem acceptable through the craft of men and the cunning of human wisdom, not through truth, like the teachings invented by men, in which not reason, not power, but the arrangement of words is sought: and therefore he who wishes to adorn the faith of Christ with words seeks his own glory. For he obscures it with the splendor of words, so that not it, but he himself is praised; just as the false apostles too, lest they should seem foolish to the prudent of the world, preached Christ in the wisdom of men in two ways: by devoting themselves to eloquence, and by avoiding those things in us which the world judges foolish; so that they taught neither that the Son of God was incarnate and born of the Virgin, nor the future resurrection of the flesh; because the wisdom and reasoning of the world judges this foolish: and therefore the Apostle says that he does not preach Christ in the wisdom of men; lest, he says, the cross of Christ be made void; because he who announces Christ in the wisdom of man denies the truth of the preaching, as I mentioned above.

(1 Cor. 1:18) For the word of the cross is foolishness to those who are perishing. It is clear that those to whom the cross of Christ is foolishness are in perdition, for they have not been snatched from the death of hell.

(1 Cor. 1:19) But to those who are being saved it is the power of God. It is not obscure that to those who believe it is the power of God; for they believe that the cross of Christ is not weakness but power, understanding that death was conquered on the cross: those who have its sign are saved, because they cannot be held by it.

For it is written, in Isaiah: I will destroy the wisdom of the wise, and the understanding of the prudent I will reject (Isa. 29:14). He destroys the wisdom of the wise when he does what it denies can be done; and he rejects the understanding of the prudent when he proves that God, whom they call unconcerned, has undertaken the things that they think foolish, namely that the Son of God, incarnate of the Virgin, should be crucified for human salvation; a deed which he truly attests by power, not by words.

(1 Cor. 1:20) Where is the wise man? Where is the scribe? Where is the inquirer of this age? With these words he inveighs against the Jews as much as against the Gentiles; because both the scribes of the Jews and the teachers of the Law think it foolish to believe that God has a Son. In like manner the Gentiles too consign this to ridicule. But the Jews distrust it because this is not so openly signified in the Law; the Gentiles, however, because the reasoning of the world does not admit it; for it says that nothing can come to be without intercourse, and they judge it foolish. The inquirer of the age, however, is he who determines by calculations that the world is driven by the constellations, and that risings and settings come about through the twelve signs, thinking that nothing at all can happen without their motion.

Has not God made foolish the wisdom of this world? The wisdom of this world has been made foolish; for, thinking itself wise, it was found imprudent. For what it judged impossible has been declared possible, since it supposed God unconcerned. Is there anything more foolish than this assertion, that he should be said to have made the world and not to take care of it? To what end did he make it, if what he made does not concern him? But because they see some enjoying the world happily and others oppressed, those who act well despised and the malevolent exulting, for this reason they have believed that God is unconcerned. He who says that God is unconcerned does not deny that he is malevolent or unjust; for either he is going to judge the world, and he is unjust, disregarding the good and suffering evils to be done. If, then, they would pay attention, setting aside their hatred of the divine law, they can see that this case is settled by us who await the judgment of God, in which those oppressed by violence will be exalted and the violent will be humbled; because there is no respect of persons with God (Rom. 2:11).

(1 Cor. 1:21) For since, in the wisdom of God, the world did not know God through wisdom, it pleased God through the foolishness of preaching to save those who believe. The world, that is, men, did not know God in his wisdom; because they gave his unique majesty to figments or to the elements, disregarding him, thinking through the wisdom of the flesh that these things which are seen ought to be worshipped. Therefore it pleased God to ordain a preaching that would seem foolishness to them, so that those who believe what they distrust might be saved, while they are condemned. This, then, is the reversal by which, through the counsel of God, the false is condemned by the true.

(1 Cor. 1:22) For the Jews ask for signs, and the Greeks seek wisdom. The Jews ask for signs, because they do not doubt that it could have been done, but they ask whether it has been done; knowing that the dry rod of Aaron budded and bore fruit (Num. 17:8), and that Jonah, swallowed by the whale, was in its belly three days and three nights and was cast out alive (Jon. 2:1). Above all, however, they seek to see something such as that which Moses saw, God in fire; whence they say: We know that God spoke to Moses (John 9:29); although it is a greater thing that Lazarus, dead and stinking on the fourth day, came out of the tomb alive (John 11:44). But the Greeks seek wisdom, because they do not wish to hear anything except what is possible by the reasoning of the world.

(1 Cor. 1:23) But we preach Christ crucified, to the Jews indeed a stumbling block, but to the Gentiles foolishness. It is a stumbling block to the Jews when they hear that Christ professed himself the Son of God and made the sabbath void. But to the Gentiles foolishness; because they hear those things preached which, since they do not agree with worldly reason, seem senseless, such as the birth from the Virgin and the resurrection of the dead.

(1 Cor. 1:25) But to those who are called, both Jews and Greeks, Christ the power of God and the wisdom of God. With power persuading them, which is stronger than words, those who believe in Christ understand, the Jews first, who were seeking a sign, that Christ is the power of God; and the Greeks likewise see that Christ is the wisdom of God, and that the worldly wisdom which they formerly thought prudence is the greatest foolishness. He is the power of God, then, because through him God the Father made all things; and the wisdom, because through him God has been known. For God could not be known except through him who was from him; because No one has known or seen the Father except the Son, and he to whom the Son wills to reveal (Matt. 11:27).

Because what is foolish of God is wiser than men. The foolish of God, he says, not because it is truly foolish, but because it is thought foolish by men, since it does not agree with worldly reason, though it is spiritual reason: and therefore it is recognized to be wiser than men, because spiritual things are wiser than carnal things. For spiritual things do not exist through carnal things, but carnal things subsist through spiritual things; and therefore carnal things are subject to spiritual things.

And what is weak of God is stronger than men. Without doubt heavenly things overcome earthly ones. Therefore what is weak of God is not weak; because the weakness of Christ is a great victory. For he conquered when he seemed to be conquered, as he says in the fiftieth psalm: And that you may conquer when you are judged (Ps. 50:6). For he proves the victor who is unjustly slain, making the one by whom he is slain guilty.

(1 Cor. 1:26) For consider, brothers, your calling; that not many are wise according to the flesh, not many powerful, not many noble. It is clear that those are few who are puffed up rather than educated by the reasonings of the world, that is, who observe and distinguish the motions of the stars. He calls the same people strong according to the flesh whom he also calls prudent. And strong for this reason, that they assert that the passion of the cross of Christ is weak; for iniquity is strong, and seems to itself to conquer for a time. They are also the noble, through whom they assign the origin of their superstition to antiquity, calling us new. God, therefore, did not choose this assertion, which is found in a few.

(1 Cor. 1:27) But God chose the foolish things of the world, that he might confound the wise. The foolish things of the world are that the Virgin gave birth, and that the Son of God was crucified: for the world thinks it foolish to believe this; therefore God, in order to confound it, chose these who believe what it judges foolish. For the wise are confounded when they see many confess what a few deny; for there is no doubt that the opinions of many are preferred to the opinions of a few.

And God chose the weak things of the world, that he might confound the strong. The sense is the same; because what the few think weak, those who consider themselves prudent in the world, distrusting the hope, is asserted by many to be power; because more believe than do not believe, to their shame. For they see the so-called weak things of Christ command demons and work wonders: and to the world the injuries and the passion of the Savior are weak things, for it does not know that these serve for power; because he allowed himself to suffer them for this reason, that he might conquer death. For to suffer unjustly, and to be able to resist and not to will it, is the glory of the one who suffers and the condemnation of the one who kills.

(1 Cor. 1:28) And God chose the ignoble things of this world and the contemptible, and the things that are not, that he might destroy the things that are. He chose, then, the ignoble and contemptible things: not because they are truly ignoble and contemptible, but they are so judged by the world, because they spurn worldly reasonings by believing in Christ; that he might destroy the things that are truly ignoble and contemptible; because those who judge are rather the ones to be judged and condemned. For error invented their assertion, and it was not from the beginning: but our teaching is from the beginning. Theirs is destroyed, then, since it is asserted with bare words without testimony: but ours is proved not by words alone, but also with power as its witness.

(1 Cor. 1:29) That no flesh may glory in his sight. That is, that the wisdom of the flesh may blush at its error, with God as judge; for it is the mind of the flesh to distrust that a virgin gave birth by the power of God, etc., because the reasoning of the world, which is kin to the flesh, does not accept this.

(1 Cor. 1:30) For of him you are in Christ Jesus. Of him, that is, it is of God, who is almighty, that we are in the faith of Christ; for it is the purpose of God that we should learn his truth and mercy through Christ. The truth is the mystery of the Trinity; and the mercy, that when we were captives, he redeemed us.

(1 Cor. 1:31) Who was made wisdom for us from God, and righteousness, and sanctification, and redemption. For the confirmation of believers he says that Christ did by the will of God what he did; so that we may know that we have truly learned wisdom, and have been sanctified, and justified, and redeemed by God through Christ; for no one redeems except what had been his own. Whether, then, that we have been redeemed, or that we have been sanctified, that is, cleansed from the work of the flesh and the uncleanness of idols, or that we have been justified (for it is righteous to worship the Creator, the rest being spurned), or that we are wise through the fact that we have learned that the worldly are ignorant, all this is the benefit of God through Christ. This, however, is our redemption: for Christ offered himself to the devil, who desired him, so that he might take away the contradiction of sin in himself, and so rescue his captives.

So that, as it is written: Let him who glories, glory in the Lord. This is written in the prophet Jeremiah (Jer. 9:24). Worthy is what the prophet says, that we should glory in God; because he who glories in the Lord will not be able to be confounded; for his works and his magnificence appear in the things he has done. And therefore, he says, let them be confounded in their idols, which do not send rain and did not make heaven and earth: in like manner also those who glory in men, whom they surely know to be of no power; wherefore Scripture also says: Vain is hope in man (Jer. 17:5).

Chapter II

(1 Cor. 2:1–2) And I, when I came to you, brethren, came not with eminence of speech or of wisdom, preaching to you the mystery of God. For I did not judge that I knew anything among you, except Christ Jesus, and him crucified. Since above he has shown, step by step, the form of our teaching, he now proves that he handed on to them nothing other than what he has shown; for he spoke to them of the mystery of Christ both in lowliness of speech and in the foolishness of preaching, as it seems to the world. He calls it a mystery for this reason: because what became incarnate was hidden from the ages with God, God the Word. But those in whom they gloried were heretics, because they both commended a depraved doctrine through eloquence and, following the prudence of the world, emptied the cross of Christ, ashamed to be called foolish by the world; so that they neither preached that Christ was born of the Virgin nor taught that he was truly crucified; for it seems foolish to confess that the Son of God was born a man and crucified. These too the apostle John points out, saying: He who denies that Christ has come in the flesh is antichrist; and he who denies the Son does not have the Father either (1 John 2:23–24); for they said that the Father himself was called Son to himself; and from these Marcion drew his error. The things he censures seem closed; for he does not make them plain, but wishes them to be understood from the fact that the prudence of the world is found there, which is hostile to religion. And what the things are that he rebukes, he wishes to be learned from his judgment; so that, whatever of ours it derides as foolish, he may show that he reproves all these things in the Corinthians. For he mentions the body itself, so that from it the condition of its members may be discerned: to the Corinthians, however, they were plain; for they were not ignorant of the causes for which they were being reproved.

(1 Cor. 2:3) And I was with you in weakness, and fear, and much trembling. For by preaching Christ in the foolishness of human wisdom he provoked hatred and persecutions against himself, as one announcing a vain thing, and one hostile to Jews and Gentiles alike.

(1 Cor. 2:4) And my speech and my preaching were not in the persuasion of human wisdom. He shows that he did not seek to please human wisdom so as to gain the favor of men, nor give himself to the art of words, but showed faith to the Author, who willed that his teaching be acceptable not by the adornment of human tradition, by the din of words, but by the realities themselves; for realities come before words.

But in the showing of the spirit and of the power of God. For since, by words, which are weak in comparison with power, foolish things, once adorned, seem wise, God willed that his preaching be commended not by the testimony of words but by that of power; so that, when the word had been pronounced foolishness, it might show itself by deeds to be wisdom founded on spiritual reason.

(1 Cor. 2:5–6) That your faith may not be in the wisdom of men, but in the power of God; yet we speak wisdom among the perfect; that is, among those who, by the testimony of power, confess the cross of Christ to be wisdom. For those are wise and perfect who hold the faith no more in words than in deeds.

Yet a wisdom not of this age. He rightly says, a wisdom not of this age, but of the age to come, in which the truth of God will appear to those who deny it; for this age does not grasp this reason, through the weakness of earthly thought.

Nor of the princes of this age, who are being destroyed. It is manifest that this wisdom is not that of the princes of this world, because they are destroyed by it. For Christianity has cut away the errors which these planted, that is, idolatry, avarice and the other vices, as the apostle John says (1 John 3:8). For this the Son of God came, that he might undo the works of the devil.

(1 Cor. 2:7) But we speak the wisdom of God in a mystery, which is hidden. He testifies that he has been sent to make manifest a hidden meaning, which neither the princes nor the powers knew, nor had the world heard; and that for this reason it is thought foolish, because it is unknown: but that it is rational and saving, as power bears witness, to which every effort of human reasoning yields. The wisdom of God, then, is hidden, since it is not in words but in power; not attainable by human reason, but made credible by the efficacy of the spirit.

Which God predestined before the ages for our glory. So true a wisdom is it, and so plainly is it declared to have been always with God, that it is said to have been predestined before the ages for our glory, who believe. For God, foreknowing the errors that were to come in the world which he was going to make, decreed this for the confounding of those who were going to make foolishness their wisdom, but for the glory of us who were going to believe, and do believe.

(1 Cor. 2:8) Which none of the princes of this age knew; for if they had known, they would never have crucified the Lord of majesty. By the princes of this age are to be understood not only men, those of the Jews and of the Romans, but also those principalities and powers of which he spoke above, to whom this saying truly pertains, against whom we have a struggle, against the spiritual wickedness in the heavenly places; for Christ was crucified by their counsel and will (Eph. 6:12). Finally, after the temptations, the devil departed, it says, until a time (Luke 4:13). And the Lord himself: The prince, he says, of this world comes, and finds nothing in me (John 14:30). The princes of this age, then, crucified the Lord of majesty through ignorance; for how can the princes of the Jews be understood as princes of this age, when they were subject to the Roman rule? Nor did the princes of the Romans crucify Christ, seeing that Pilate said: I find no cause of death in him (John 19:4). Whence he also washed his hands, saying to the Jews: I am innocent of the blood of this righteous man; see to it yourselves (Matt. 27:24). It was these princes, then, who crucified Christ the Lord, those over whom he triumphed freely in himself (Col. 2:15); although Mark the evangelist says of the demons: For they knew that the Christ himself was Jesus (Mark 1:34). They knew indeed that it was he, but as the one who had been promised in the Law; yet his mystery, by which he is the Son of God, they did not know. This the apostle Peter also said to the Jewish people: I know, brethren, that you did this evil through ignorance, as did your princes too (Acts 3:17), yet not princes of the age. If, then, the servants killed the Lord through ignorance, sin ought not to be ascribed to them. But it is not permitted to be ignorant of this. And although they did not know that he was the Lord, still they were not unaware that they were doing an impious thing. Yet the Lord of majesty is said to have been crucified, though he does not know how to die: but because the Word was made flesh, that is, the Son of God, incarnate, was made man, death was ascribed to him; for the Jews certainly did not persecute the flesh, but him who worked through the flesh; and therefore, although the Lord of majesty knows nothing of death, yet so far as concerns the will of the Jews and of the princes of this age, they killed the Lord in the flesh.

(1 Cor. 2:9) But as it is written: What eye has not seen, nor ear heard, nor has it gone up into the heart of man, the things which God has prepared for those who love him. This is written in the Apocalypse of Elijah, among the apocrypha. By this example he says that an unlooked-for thing has been done, one which not only would not fall within human understanding but was hidden even from the heavenly powers, namely, the Son of God incarnate; so as to confirm what he had said above, that if the princes of this age did not understand that God had been made man, how much less did men! But if the words made an obstacle or a stumbling-block, faith still ought not to have been refused to the mighty works and signs. For power ought to have been set above human weakness and ignorance, and what seemed impossible to human reason ought to have been believed; and therefore he has prepared this gift for those who love God, that is, for believers.

(1 Cor. 2:10) But to us God has revealed it through his Spirit. And because the princes of the age did not know this, God revealed it through his Spirit to believers; for the things of God cannot be learned without the Spirit of God. For he says that he has been sent to declare this mystery not only to men, but also to the principalities and powers in the heavenly places, who crucified the Lord; so that, as he preaches it on earth, those may learn who are in the carnal heavens, who dwell beneath the elements of the firmament. This, then, is a singular apostle, who obtained this grace; for the mystery of the Trinity could be explained by no one as it was by him: whence he was also called a vessel of election by divine judgment (Acts 9:15).

For the Spirit searches all things, even the depths of God. For because this Spirit is from God, he knows all things. And he says the depths of God for this reason, because he knows all his power and foreknowledge: which is wholly impossible for a creature.

(1 Cor. 2:11) For who among men knows the things that are of a man, except the spirit of the man, which is in him? It is manifest that our thoughts are known by no one except by our own mind, which he called spirit. Finally, in the Gospel, among other things, it says thus: And her spirit returned to her (Luke 8:55).

So also the things that are of God no one knows, except the Spirit of God. This Spirit of God has taught us what he knows by nature, not by being taught; and he has taught us about the mystery of Christ; for he is not only the Spirit of God, but also of Christ.

(1 Cor. 2:12) But we have not received the spirit of this world; but the Spirit who is from God. From this we know that we have received the Spirit who is from God: because the spirit of the world cannot know the things that have been made known to us by him. The spirit of the world, however, is this one, by whom fanatics are seized, who are without God; for among the worldly spirits he is the stronger; whence he is accustomed to divine by conjectures the things that belong to the world, and they call him python. This is he who through likelihoods is deceived and deceives: this is he who spoke through the Sibyl, following our sense, wishing to have a place among the heavenly beings.

That we may know the things that have been given to us by God. For because the Spirit of God has been given to us, we know the things that have been given to us by God. For we could not know these things if we had the spirit of the world; for the spirit of the world cannot know them. For no one knows the mind of God except the one who is from God, the Spirit of God; for lower things cannot know the counsel of higher ones, nor can a creature discern the will of its maker.

(1 Cor. 2:13–14) Which things we also speak. So that it may be manifest from this that we know the things given to us by God: for we also speak them, so that others too may learn through spiritual teaching. For things were made known by God to the preachers, that is, to the apostles, for them to hand on to the peoples, and so that the princes of this age might learn what evil they had committed.

Not in the teaching of words of human wisdom, but in the teaching of the spirit; for the words of human wisdom do not see this meaning, nor is it grasped by literary studies: but it is conceived through faith by spiritual reason, as the prophet Isaiah says: Unless you believe, you will not understand either (Isa. 7:9). For it is learned by natural law rather than by the course of the stars and the reckonings of the signs that are marked out in the firmament. Finally, believers see that what they believe agrees, not with human words, but with their own nature; for the work recognizes its workman: and therefore the Gospel speaks spiritual things to our souls, rousing them to the knowledge of their Creator.

Comparing spiritual things with the spiritual. That is, to hand on to those who reject worldly prudence the spiritual efficacy which is contained in the mystery of faith, for enlightening the minds of well-disposed men; for those who believe what the wisdom of the world judges foolish are spiritual.

But the natural man does not perceive the things that are of the Spirit of God, he does not know them; for it is foolishness to him. For, like the cattle, he presses his mind down to the earth; and therefore he attains only what he sees, nor does he think that anything can be done otherwise than as he knows. Therefore whatever he hears that is otherwise than he knows, he judges foolish; for he reckons that nothing can come about without intercourse. Hence he laughs when he hears that God, whom he knows to be simple and incorporeal, has begotten a Son, and that a Virgin has given birth, and that bodies dissolved are called back to life again; although these things serve rather to the praise of God, so that he is believed to have done a work whose reason cannot be searched out; for it is God who has done it. For human weakness thinks foolish whatever its knowledge does not take in; whereas it ought rather to hold this to be foolish, and that to be wise which cannot be comprehended because it is said to be the doing of God.

And he cannot know; because it is judged spiritually. Those who follow human tradition, while they deny faith in the one God: what they, puffed up by the reasoning of the flesh, think cannot come about has been done spiritually; and so, marked down as reprobate, they have been spiritually condemned.

(1 Cor. 2:15) But the spiritual man judges all things, and he himself is judged by no one. Since true reason belongs to the spiritual, that is, to believers (for each is named by what he believes), therefore they themselves judge all things. For by their example unbelief will be judged, but they themselves will be judged by no one. For who would condemn one who speaks the truth? For since it is established that all the enemies of the faith hold false things for true, their accusation has already been brought to nothing, condemned by the judgment of truth; for he who has not believed, says the Lord, has already been judged (John 3:18).

(1 Cor. 2:16) For who has known the mind of the Lord? who has instructed him (Isa. 40:13)? The mind of God is this, that he commanded these things to be done spiritually, by which the wisdom of the world, denying that what has been done can be done, would be proved foolish. There is no one who has known this mind and is displeased with it, so as to correct the counsel of God; for he who has known it praises the power of God, whereas he who denies that God can do all things does not believe that he made the world either. For what great thing is it that he should be believed to have begotten from himself, who is not doubted to have made all things out of nothing? seeing that the Savior says: But with God all things are possible (Matt. 19:26).

But we have the mind of the Lord. He says this because, as believers, we are sharers in the same divine judgment.

Chapter III

(1 Cor. 3:1) And I, brethren, could not speak to you as to spiritual men, but as to carnal. He now speaks to those who were carnal because they were still serving the pleasures of the age. Although they had already been baptized and had received the Holy Spirit, yet because after baptism they had at once gone back to the old man, whom they had renounced, they are called carnal. For the Holy Spirit remains in the one into whom he has poured himself only if that one remains in the purpose of regeneration: if not, he departs; yet in such a way that, if the man reforms himself, he returns to him. For he is always ready for good, loving repentance.

As to little ones in Christ I gave you milk to drink, not food. Although they had been reborn in Christ, it was still not fitting that spiritual things be handed on to them; for having received the faith, which is like a spiritual seed, they had produced no fruit worthy of God, such that they would deserve to learn the words of perfection: but like little ones they were devoted to the notions of imperfection. But the Apostle, a man of God and a spiritual physician, handed on to each according to his strength, lest anyone, through imperfection and inexperience, should suffer a stumbling-block concerning spiritual things.

(1 Cor. 3:2) For you were not yet able. He shows openly that they ought not to have heard the things that are to be spoken to the established.

But neither are you able now; for you are still carnal. Here he strongly reproves the rashness of those who had for some time been complaining that they had not heard spiritual things, when they were still unworthy to hear them. For the false apostles handed things on to all indiscriminately in one and the same way, distinguishing no one's person, so as to become acceptable, commended by hypocrisy: whereas it is established that Christ, our Lord and Author, spoke in one way to the peoples and in another to the disciples, and that there was a difference even among the apostles themselves; for to three disciples he showed his glory on the mountain, telling them to keep silent about what they had seen until he should rise from the dead (Matt. 17:9).

(1 Cor. 3:3) For since there are rivalries and contentions among you, are you not carnal, and do you not walk according to man? He shows the cause of their unworthiness; for he who hopes for help from man is carnal. But he who scorns what is human and hopes in God is spiritual; for God is spirit.

(1 Cor. 3:4) For when one says: I am of Paul; and another: I of Apollos; are you not men? For just as those who glory in God and hope for every grace from him are called gods, adopted by God, so too those who glory in man are called both men and carnal, shut in by the sentence of God, who says: I said, You are gods; but you shall die like men (Ps. 81:6). He begins, then, with his own person, lest perhaps he be thought to be tearing down their persons rather out of envy, showing that it is a great error, and one that amounts to an insult to God, when, after God has been preached, glory is given to men. In what, then, does it fall short of paganism, so long as something is still hoped for from men?

(1 Cor. 3:5) What then is Apollos? And what is Paul? Ministers of him in whom you have believed. So that, because they are ministers, hope may not be in them, but in God, whose ministers they are. For thanksgiving must be rendered to him whose gift it is; but these are servants, who must dispense it even against their will. Was not Moses compelled to go to Pharaoh, and Jonah sent unwilling to preach to the Ninevites, and Ananias sent, though he objected, to lay his hand on Saul?

And to each as the Lord has given: that is, as he willed and knew, he distributed to each the offices of the ministry.

(1 Cor. 3:6) I planted, Apollos watered, but God gave the increase. To plant is to evangelize and to draw to the faith; to water is to baptize with the solemn words; but to forgive sins and to give the Spirit belongs to God alone. If, then, God gives the effect of salvation, there is no glory for man in this matter (Acts 2:4). For we know both that the Holy Spirit was given by God without the laying on of hands (Acts 10:44), and that one who was not baptized obtained the remission of sins. Was he not baptized invisibly here, when he obtained the gift of baptism?

(1 Cor. 3:7) So neither he who plants is anything, nor he who waters: but God, who gives the increase. For things planted often die, and things watered often fail to reach the effect of fruitfulness, unless God gives them vigorous life. As far, then, as the honor of God is concerned, man is nothing; but as far as the ministry is concerned, he is necessary; so that he is honored as a servant, not so that anything should be hoped for from him to the injury of God.

(1 Cor. 3:8) For he who plants and he who waters are one. They are one because both are hired workers, although their offices are different. But each will receive his own wage according to his own labor. For although they are one, yet because he who evangelizes is greater than he who baptizes, he has therefore distinguished their wages; and this pertains not only to that, but to the purity of doctrine: so that he who hands on doctrine inviolate is worthy of a greater wage.

(1 Cor. 3:9) For we are sharers in God's work: you are God's tillage, God's building. This pertains to the person of the apostles, who, it is established, are God's helpers; for they are vicars of Christ.

(1 Cor. 3:10) According to the grace of God which was given to me, as a wise architect I laid the foundation. He says that grace was given to him, that he might be worthy to preach the Gospel, so as to show that this too was of God, not of man. According to this grace, then, he says that he laid the foundation as a wise architect. A wise architect is one who lays the foundation according to the discipline of the author, that is, who evangelizes according to the tradition of the Savior.

But another builds upon it. The superstructures are the things handed on after the preaching of the apostles by teachers who come after, whether bad or good.

But let each one see how he builds upon it, that is, so that the superstructures may agree with the foundation; lest, if they are crooked and flimsy, they cause a collapse, while the foundation remains unharmed; for if he has taught badly, the name of Christ, which is the foundation, remains, but the bad teaching will perish, as the Lord says: Every planting which my heavenly Father has not planted will be rooted up (Matt. 15:13).

(1 Cor. 3:11) For no one can lay another foundation besides that which has been laid, which is Christ Jesus. No one can lay another foundation for this reason: because, although some are heretics, they still do not teach except under the name of Christ. For they cannot commend the fabrications of their error unless they preach with the name of the Savior set before them, so that the dignity of the name may make contrary and absurd things acceptable.

(1 Cor. 3:12) But if anyone builds upon this foundation gold, silver, precious stones. Precious stones are those which fire does not corrupt. He has set down three kinds of things that are splendid in the world, by which he signified good doctrine. Wood, hay, stubble: he has set down three other kinds, but flimsy ones. In these, corrupt and empty doctrine is recognized as designated.

(1 Cor. 3:13) He who does this work will be manifest; for the day will declare it, because it will be revealed in fire. That is, bad doctrine will appear to all in the fire; for now it deceives some. And the fire will prove what sort each one's work is. For since the examination will be made by fire, if it finds nothing in someone to burn up, it shows that he was a good teacher; for bad and adulterous doctrine was signified by wood, hay and stubble for this reason, to show that it is food for fire.

(1 Cor. 3:14) If anyone's work which he has built upon remains, he will receive a wage. For when nothing of the filth of depraved doctrine has been found in him, as in good gold, it will be with him as with the three brothers in the furnace of fire (Dan. 3:23): he will receive as his wage eternal life with glory; for just as gold and silver and stones are not consumed by fire, so too the good teacher will remain incorruptible.

(1 Cor. 3:15) If anyone's work burns, he will suffer detriment. The work which is said to burn is bad doctrine, which will perish. For all evil things will perish, like the way of the impious; for they are not substances, but perverse assertions. Now to suffer damage is to undergo punishments. For who, placed in punishment, does not suffer loss?

But he himself will be saved, yet so as through fire. He said that he will be saved, because the substance of which he consists will not perish, as the bad doctrine will perish; for that is an accidental thing. And he said, yet so as through fire, for this reason: that this salvation may not be without punishment; for he did not say, He will be saved through fire, so that, not burned by the fire because of his merit, he would be saved after being tested through fire; but when he says, Yet so as through fire, he shows that he will indeed be saved, but will suffer the punishments of fire; so that, purged through fire, he may be saved, and not be tormented for ever in eternal fire like the faithless; so that in some measure it may have been worth while to have believed in Christ. For he must always blush who sees that he has defended the false for the true: and in like manner he will always have confidence in God who, having cast away the false, has followed the true, and, having scorned impiety, has submitted to piety. For whatever is outside the catholic Church is contrary.

(1 Cor. 3:16) Do you not know that you are the temple of God, and the Spirit of God dwells in you? He says that we are the temple of God, so that we may know that God dwells in us; for he must dwell in his own temple. And because he says that the Spirit of God dwells in us, he signifies that the Holy Spirit is to be understood as God.

(1 Cor. 3:17) If anyone violates the temple of God, God will destroy him; for the temple of God is holy, which you are. He set these things down beforehand for this reason, to prick the consciences of those who, living shamefully, had corrupted their bodies by violating them; above all him who had his father's wife; so that he would be guilty before his case burst out into the open. Finally, in a similar, indeed in the same way, when he judges his case, he speaks, saying: Or do you not know that your bodies are the temple of the Holy Spirit, who is in you, whom you have from God (1 Cor. 6:19). Above he said the temple of God; but in this place, the temple of the Holy Spirit. Who here could doubt that he signified the Holy Spirit to be God?

(1 Cor. 3:18) Let no one deceive himself: that is, let no one think that he benefits himself by his own, or by human, counsel. He returns to the earlier sense: If anyone among you seems to be wise in this age, let him become foolish, that he may be wise. He says this because, if anyone understands the promised salvation and the mystery of the incarnation of our Lord Jesus Christ, let him become foolish, that is, let him flee the wisdom of the world, so that he is judged foolish by it, and then he will be prudent; for he who is foolish to the age is prudent to God, while he believes that God has done what the reasoning of the world does not grasp, as I recalled above. But the false apostles, wishing to seem prudent in the world, preached neither that God has a Son, nor that the incarnation was true, nor that the flesh can rise again.

(1 Cor. 3:19) For the wisdom of this world is foolishness with God. The wisdom of this world is foolishness with God in this way: when God does what the world denies can be done, he proves its wisdom to be foolishness; and since they speak of many gods, while faith proves there is one, with power as its witness, they will be confounded in their prudence.

(1 Cor. 3:20) For it is written: Rebuking the wise in their craftiness. And again: The Lord knows the thoughts of the wise, that they are vain. This is found in the ninety-third psalm. Finally, the sense is the same; for, knowing their thoughts to be vain, he rebuked their wisdom so as to prove them foolish, showing that what they called false is true, and what they thought true is false.

(1 Cor. 3:21) So let no one glory in man. For since human thoughts and counsels are improvident and weak, he teaches that one must not glory in man, but in God, whose counsel cannot be revoked: for whatever men are wise in without God is foolish.

(1 Cor. 3:22) For all things are yours, whether Paul, or Apollos, or Cephas, or the world, or life, or death, or things present, or things to come: all things are yours, but you are Christ's, and Christ is God's. In another place he says to the same effect: But ourselves your servants for Jesus' sake (2 Cor. 4:5). He put servants for ministers, so that they would not each claim individuals for themselves, since they had the use of all, as the Lord too says: I am in your midst, not to be ministered to, but to minister (Luke 22:27). For the world is indeed ours, but so that we may think about it according to the will of God, and set its appointed courses in the will of God. And the present life has been granted to us, but so that we may pass it modestly and with the glory of God. Or death, so that we may willingly accept dying for Christ in the hope of the promise to come. Or things present, so that we may use present things in such a way as not to give offense. Or things to come, so that, believing in the things to come, we may commend ourselves rather to them, because they are better.

(1 Cor. 3:23) But you are Christ's: so that, as these things have been granted and subjected to our judgment, so we too may be subject to Christ, seeing that it was through him that we began to be, both in our first origin and in regeneration. And Christ is God's. Christ is God's as the proper Son of his Father, doing his will, so that we too may do his. If, then, we are Christ's servants, why should we hope for anything from men, to his injury?

Chapter IV

(1 Cor. 4:1) Let a man so regard us, as ministers of Christ and dispensers of the mysteries of God. He indicated this because some of the Corinthians thought less of him; but so that a man might think of him what God also thought, who had chosen him, he therefore says ministers of Christ and dispensers of the mysteries of God. For since he strove to be understood not by a din of words nor by human wisdom, he had to dispense the mystery of Christ, in which not words but power shone forth, through which not man but God might be seen to be glorious; for as a colleague of fishermen he preached Christ no differently than they did. When, therefore, he proves himself a minister of Christ and of the mysteries of God, he brands the false apostles, and denies that what they hand down is Christ's, in that they disagreed with the apostolic tradition.

(1 Cor. 4:2) Here now it is required among dispensers that one be found faithful. What he says here pertains not only to the person of the false apostles, but also to that of the others who, having received a dispensation, out of bashfulness, or fear, or surely for the sake of their own commendation, were afraid to reprove steadfastly either evil opinions or evil morals. For he was so steadfast that he did not spare even his own life, provided that he performed all things faithfully.

(1 Cor. 4:3) But for me it is a very small thing that I should be judged by you, or by a human day. He says it is a wretched thing to be judged by those whom he rebukes for such great vices, since indeed he knew that he was not to be judged even by the righteous. Or by a human day, that is, because he was not to be judged even by the laws entrusted to the world. For having passed beyond human righteousness, he devoted himself to heavenly righteousness, as the Lord says: Unless your righteousness abounds more than that of the scribes and Pharisees, you shall not enter into the kingdom of God. (Matt. 5:20). When, therefore, he says a human day, he signifies also a divine one, on which Christ is to judge; for just as jurists or pontiffs, whom they call priests, have decreed fixed days on which judgment is given, so too the day of the Lord has been appointed, on which he is to judge the world.

(1 Cor. 4:4) But neither do I judge myself; I am indeed conscious of nothing against myself. It is plain that, having a pure conscience, he was not anxious about himself. But I am not justified in this. Now he humbles himself and speaks as a man, who could incur a fault without knowing it. But he who judges me is the Lord. He speaks rightly; for although he humbled himself, yet he granted very much to the Lord Christ, because, when he judges hidden things, he can make manifest what lies hidden from a man, not to make him guilty, but prudent. For though he is holy, he is yet a man, and it cannot be that nothing escapes him. For at times we think something useful which is useless; for we do not have all the members or sides of human conduct written down; but from the things that are grave we have been openly forbidden. For certain matters, arising from some causes, are apt to deceive us, so that we think useful what is not useful; and this indeed is a light thing, because the purpose is free.

(1 Cor. 4:5) Therefore do not judge before the time, until the Lord comes. He warns them not to judge, since they are themselves to be judged, and would have a double sin, as the Lord says: Do not judge, that you may not be judged (Matt. 7:1); but await the day of God's judgment, as if they were faithful and good. For it is an affront to the judge if a judgment or sentence proceeds from a servant before his hearing of the case.

Who will also illuminate the hidden things of darkness, and will make manifest the counsels of hearts: and then there will be praise for each one from God. He says this because on the day of judgment nothing will be hidden of the things that have been done or thought: both simplicity and hypocrisy will appear there; so that he who was held despised may perhaps appear worthy, and he who was thought to be something may be found reprobate. For all things will be naked on the day of judgment, and then there will be praise for him who acts or thinks well. For here, when someone is praised, it cannot be proven whether he is worthy, as he also says in another place: For it is not he who commends himself that is approved, but he whom God commends (2 Cor. 10:18), from whom surely nothing is hidden.

(1 Cor. 4:6) Now these things, brothers, I have transfigured in myself and Apollos for your sakes, that in us you may learn that no one be puffed up beyond what is written, one for one against another. He transfigured these things in himself and Apollos, in that under his own person and that of Apollos he makes void the glories and the perverse traditions of the false apostles, designating them summarily, not one by one, lest perhaps a greater discord should arise among the people. For no one who hears his own name censured, or that of the one he favors, is content to keep silent; but if the name is left unspoken, whoever hears, even if he understands that it is said of him, pretends otherwise. Under this proposition, then, he made void their persons when he says: I planted, Apollos watered, etc. (1 Cor. 3:6), so that in these they might learn that nothing is to be given to men.

(1 Cor. 4:7) For who distinguishes you? That is, there are some who, receiving more in baptism or in teaching, say that the rest have obtained less. He says this to those who thought that, being baptized by others, they were something more: by whose eloquence they were seduced, so that through a certain disguise they thought perverse things right. For there was such great discord among the people that there were many factions of different parties; and so the Apostle fitted all his words to all the parties without making names manifest, so that when the epistle was read, each party might take up what was said to it, hearing what it recognized was aimed at its own case.

For what do you have that you have not received? He says that the man has obtained no good from the others beyond what he had received from him, and that he therefore complains in vain; for what they had, they had received from the Apostle. He seems to speak to one person, because he is speaking to a part of the people.

But if you have received, why do you glory as if you had not received? Those who, as it were, insulted the Apostle did this through inexperience: hearing the same things which they had already learned from the Apostle, they made him void and gloried in the teaching of these men; for, commended by their eloquence, these turned the glory of the preaching to themselves. The Apostle, on the other hand, wished to appear contemptible, provided that he made the glory of God acceptable.

(1 Cor. 4:8) Already you are sated, already you have become rich, without us you reign. It is irony, as they say; for these are the words of one who is angry, not of one who affirms. For he says that those in whom he rebukes such great vices are reigning; for so they thought, glorying in the things that were handed down by the false apostles.

And would that you did reign, that we also might reign with you. Like a devoted father he wishes well even to ungrateful sons. For how could it happen that they should reign without the apostles? For whatever has not been handed down by the apostles is full of crimes. Yet the proper order would have been to say: That you may reign with us; but since he had said first: And would that you did reign, he could not say otherwise than: That we also might reign with you. Now to reign is to be secure about the hope and promises of Christ, and to rejoice in the adversities that befall us for the name of Christ; for they bring gain, not loss. Hence he says in another place: When I am weak, then I am strong (2 Cor. 12:10).

(1 Cor. 4:9) For I think that God has shown us apostles as the last, as though destined to death. He ascribes this to his own person for this reason, that he was always in distress, suffering persecutions and afflictions beyond the rest, as Enoch and Elijah are to suffer, who are to be apostles in the last time. For they are to be sent before Christ to prepare the people of God and to fortify all the churches to resist antichrist; and the reading of the Apocalypse testifies that they will both suffer persecutions and be killed. The Apostle, then, compared their time with his own time, saying: As though destined to death (Rev. 11:8 ff.); for they will come for this purpose, that they may be killed.

Because we have been made a spectacle to this world, both to angels and to men. Because Enoch and Elijah will be a spectacle to such a degree that their bodies will be cast out into the street in the sight of the whole unbelieving people, so too the apostles were made a spectacle, because they were publicly mocked, exposed to injury and to the death which they suffered. He spoke of the world, angels and men, because there are also evil angels, as David says in the seventy-seventh psalm: He troubled them by evil angels; and there are evil and unbelieving men; to these the injuries of the apostles are a delight. The world, moreover, stands for unbelief, because it follows visible things.

(1 Cor. 4:10) We are fools for Christ's sake. It is true that those who love Christ are fools to the world. But you are prudent in Christ. He who is judged prudent in Christ by the faithless does not rightly proclaim Christ. Thus Marcion, because he denies the Son of God, and that God could become incarnate, is prudent to the world; and Photinus, because he does not confess that Christ, by virtue of his having been born, is God, seems wise to worldly people.

We are weak. Weak for this reason, that, preaching Christ without flattery and with foolishness, as it seems to worldly people, they were subjected to injuries. But you are strong. Strong for this reason, that they professed Christ in such a way as not to offend men, so that they were safe.

You are noble. For he who does not confess that Christ was promised to Abraham and preached from the beginning is noble against Christ, because he assigns the antiquity of the promised Christ to others. But we are ignoble. He is ignoble to the world who professes Christ from the beginning, which the world denies. All these things said above, then, which he seems to deny, he affirms; and what he seems as it were to affirm, he denies. For these are the words of an angry man, who confesses by denying and denies by confessing.

(1 Cor. 4:11–12) Up to this hour we both hunger and thirst, and are naked, and are buffeted, and are unsettled, and labor, working with our own hands. For, preaching Christ freely and according to the true faith without any flattery, and reproving the deeds of a depraved life, they had no favor with men, and they were buffeted, that is, assailed with injuries, and they were unsettled, because they were driven away, lest, staying long in one place, they should teach more people. They also worked with their hands for this reason, that not only did they have no favor with men, but it was also unworthy to receive from those who were devoted to error, as he says in the hundred and fortieth psalm: But let not the oil of the sinner fatten my head (Ps. 140:5). For he who receives from one who gives in order not to be rebuked both loses the freedom to reprove and sins.

(1 Cor. 4:13) We are cursed and we bless: for they exhorted those who ill-treated them to what is good. We suffer persecution and we endure; because they did not resist those who persecuted them. We are blasphemed and we entreat: for they begged those who blasphemed them to allow them to give an account.

We have become as the refuse of this world, the offscouring of all until now. By resisting no one, and not repaying evil for evil, but always submitting himself, so that through patience he might call them back to what is good, he became contemptible and despised. Until now, that is, up to the present, even among the Corinthians, for whose sake he suffered these evils which he mentions above. And since he saw that his humility had achieved nothing among them, moved by grief he complains that such great submission and such injuries could bear no fruit, and, what is worse, had even turned out for the worse.

(1 Cor. 4:14) Not to shame you do I write these things: that is, I write these things not to make you blush, but that you may correct yourselves. But as my dearest sons I admonish you. He still makes himself a suppliant, lest, being rebuked and provoked to anger, they should spurn his admonitions. He acts like a healing physician who, cutting away the rotten parts, soothes the pain he has inflicted with hope and gentle words, so that the sick man allows himself to be cured.

(1 Cor. 4:15) For if you should have ten thousand tutors in Christ, yet not many fathers. For in Christ Jesus through the Gospel I have begotten you. By these words he shows that no one could love them with the affection with which he himself loved them. For who would put other men's sons before his own in love? And therefore they ought not to spurn the warnings of one who has also suffered such great evils on their behalf.

(1 Cor. 4:16) I beseech you therefore, be imitators of me. O the benevolence of the holy Apostle, who beseeches his sons to imitate their father, that is, of his own accord begs the sick to accept medicine! Yet in these things he wishes them to be imitators of himself: that just as he suffered many deadly perils from unbelievers for their salvation and did not give way, provided that he might preach to them day and night the gift of the grace of God, so they too, abiding in his faith and teaching, should not accept the depraved inventions of the false apostles, but should resist them, despising reproaches or detractions, so as to keep their affection for their spiritual father inviolate.

(1 Cor. 4:17) Therefore I have sent to you Timothy, who is my dearest son and faithful in the Lord. He exhorts them by this same Timothy, whom he solemnly declares his dearest and faithful son in the Lord for this reason, that in this they might learn how they were sinning; for in bearing witness to him he reproves them.

Who may remind you of my ways which are in Christ Jesus, as I teach everywhere in every church. Through this man, whom he confirms as a faithful son in the Lord, he wants them to return to the rule of the truth handed down by himself, so that, reminded by his words and by the examples of his works, they might come to their senses, recognizing that they had not been wrongly taught by the Apostle, when they learned that all the churches hold the same things.

(1 Cor. 4:18) As though I were not coming to you, so some are puffed up. Some of the Corinthians, not out of longing but out of pride, were indignant that he was not going to them, as though he held them unworthy; whereas the Apostle's zeal was rather this, to make worthy men of the unworthy. He had, then, the wish to go, but the things he was doing were greater.

(1 Cor. 4:19) But I will come to you soon, if the Lord permits. After the admonition he promises to go to them, but if the Lord should permit; so that, since God knows more than man, he would therefore permit it if he knew it to be worthwhile; and if he did not go, he would show that the Lord had not allowed it, surely by reason of their unworthiness.

And I will recognize not the speech of those who are puffed up, but their power. He puts them to shame, because, though they were living crookedly, they were indignant, as though they were worthy to be visited by the Apostle.

(1 Cor. 4:20) For the kingdom of God is not in speech, but in power. So that, just as the kingdom of God is commended not by the splendor of words but by the power of signs, so these too should show themselves worthy to be visited by the Apostle not by bare words but by the power of spiritual works.

(1 Cor. 4:21) What do you want? Shall I come to you with a rod, or in love and the spirit of gentleness? He adds words of terror, so that those who were puffed up, being put to shame, might be humbled; so that, if they truly desired the Apostle's presence, they would prepare themselves to receive him, that is, would wash themselves from every stain of crime, so that on coming to them he might have joy as with his dearest sons.

Chapter V

(1 Cor. 5:1) Fornication is actually heard of among you, and such fornication as is not even among the Gentiles. To show how grave this sin is, he said that it is not committed even by those who do not know God; so as to show accordingly with what penalty this man ought to be punished, who, though placed under eternal and divine righteousness, had committed the crime of so great a wickedness.

So that someone has his father's wife. He disclosed the kind of crime mentioned before, so as not only to make him openly guilty of death, but also to show that those who adhered to him were not free from the crime; for a sin that is rarely committed makes one the more guilty.

(1 Cor. 5:2) And you are puffed up, and have not rather mourned. He humbles their pride so far as to make them no longer querulous but rather suppliant. For they too were sharers, in that they allowed the one guilty of so enormous a deed to assemble with them unrebuked; whereas all of them, with one counsel, should cast him out if he refused to amend himself. But if anyone does not have the power to cast out one whom he knows to be guilty, or is unable to prove it, he is free from guilt; and it is not for a judge to condemn without an accuser, since the Lord too did not cast out Judas, though he was a thief, because he had not been accused.

That he who has done this deed might be taken away from your midst. Once this deed was known, he ought to have been driven out of the gathering of the brotherhood; for all knew his crime and did not reprove it; for he openly had his stepmother in place of a wife. In this matter there was no need of witnesses, nor could the crime be covered by any evasion.

(1 Cor. 5:3–5) I indeed, absent in body but present in spirit: that is, absent in face, but present by the authority of the spirit, which is nowhere absent. Have already judged, as though present, him who has done this, in the name of our Lord Jesus Christ, you and my spirit being gathered together, with the power of our Lord Jesus, to hand this man over to Satan for the destruction of the flesh. Since every pleasure of the flesh is from the devil, therefore when one gives himself up to this pleasure, he is handed over to Satan; for the flesh has this in its nature, that it perishes; the soul, then, when it joins itself to its desire, weakening its own spiritual vigor, suffers destruction along with it. Therefore if this man, rebuked for the debauchery he had committed, were to blush at being cast out, he would not perish, repentance following. Not only by the consent and presence of all, but by the power of the Lord Jesus, that is, by the sentence of him whose embassy the Apostle was discharging, he decreed that he must be cast out of the Church. When he is cast out, he is handed over to Satan for the destruction of the flesh; for both soul and body perish when things contrary to the law are done; but in this case the destruction of the flesh is ascribed to the man in another way. For although all sins make a man carnal, yet this is in a special sense the desire of the flesh, through which it hands him over, together with the body, to Gehenna; because the soul, when overcome, stained with filth, becomes flesh through the lust of the flesh; just as the body too, when rightly governed, is called spiritual. It is, however, the mind which either, overcome by enticements, makes the whole man fleshly, or, remaining in the vigor of its own nature, grants the flesh to be called spiritual.

That the spirit may be saved in the day of the Lord Jesus Christ. He hands the aforesaid defiled man over to Satan, so that the spirit may be saved in the men of the Church on the day of judgment; for unless he had been cast out, the spirit of the Church would not be saved on the day of judgment. For it was deserting them all because of the defilement, so that on the day of the Lord, being found bare of the Holy Spirit, they would hear from the Lord their judge: Depart from me, I do not know you (Matt. 7:23); as he says to the Romans: But if anyone does not have the spirit of Christ, he is not his. (Rom. 8:9); and in another epistle: Do not grieve the Holy Spirit of God. (Eph. 4:30). For if he is grieved, he deserts, and will not be saved: not, of course, for himself, who is impassible, but for us to whom he was given, so that through him we may be proved to be sons of God. For a thing that is lost is not safe, not, of course, for itself, since wherever it may be it must still exist, but for him by whom it is lost.

(1 Cor. 5:6) Is your glorying good? That is, it is not good; for they were glad, when they ought rather to have been grieved at a brother sinning so bitterly, as Samuel was grieved at Saul's sinning. This crime the bad teachers either overlooked or reproved too negligently (as Eli the priest (1 Sam. 2:23) did his sons when they sinned against God), lest they should be disagreeable to men.

Do you not know that a little leaven corrupts the whole lump? So too the sin of one man, which is known and not reproved, defiles many; indeed, all who know of it and do not shun it, or who, when they could reprove it, overlook it. For he does not think he is sinning when he is rebuked or shunned by no one.

(1 Cor. 5:7) Purge out the old leaven, that you may be a new dough. This is to be taken in two ways, because leaven is also human teaching, as the Savior says: Beware of the leaven of the Pharisees (Matt. 16:11), that is, of their teaching. It was said, then, both for guarding against bad teaching and for this case, because when the fornicator is cast out, the old leaven is cast out. And it is old because it is the sin of ancient error. The new compound, however, is the teaching of Christ.

As you are unleavened. Unleavened bread was so called from the unleavened flour, when the sons of Israel were driven out of Egypt; so that leaven was held to be oldness, and unleavened bread newness. For having cast away the old errors of the Egyptians, they were led into a new law; so too the Corinthians, after the errors of the pagans, were led into the Gospel of Christ. He exhorts them, then, to live also according to their profession, which they had received from the Apostle under the name of Christ, being now cultivators of a new life: and this is to be unleavened.

Christ our Pasch has been sacrificed. According to the Law he teaches that newness consists in the rationale of the Pasch, and that Christ was slain for this reason, that the new preaching that comes from him might produce a new way of life; so that, since we observe the rationale of the Pasch, we may not follow the old life. The Pasch, then, is a sacrifice (immolatio), not a passing-over (transitus), as it seems to some; for first comes the Pasch, and then the passing-over, because first there is the pattern of the Savior, and then the sign of salvation. For there is no sign before the cross; for when the lamb had been slain in the evening, the sons of Israel kept the Pasch in Egypt, and with its blood they marked the doorposts, so that the Angel, passing over by night, might not touch those places which had been smeared with the blood of the lamb (Exod. 12:28).

(1 Cor. 5:8) Therefore let us celebrate the feast, not with the old leaven. That is, having the joy of renewal, let us flee old deeds, casting away from us all uncleanness, which is corruption; because just as leaven corrupts the whole lump, so too a bad life corrupts the whole man. So, that we may guard ourselves not only from baseness of life but also from the pursuit of iniquity, he added:

Nor with the leaven of malice and wickedness, but with the unleavened bread of sincerity and truth. That is, that sincerity may make life clean, and truth exclude all fraud.

(1 Cor. 5:9–10) I wrote to you in the epistle not to mix with fornicators. He indicates that he had written earlier, where he warns them not to join themselves to men of evil life. And because they seemed perhaps not to have understood what he had written, he now interprets in this epistle what he wrote, saying: Not at all the fornicators of this world: but I wrote that one must withdraw from such brothers.

Or the covetous, or the rapacious, or those who serve idols; otherwise you would have had to go out of this world. He therefore reproves the understanding of those who perhaps thought that it was from unbelievers, fornicators, or the covetous, or the rapacious, or those who serve idols, that he had forbidden them, not from brothers; and therefore they were not troubled that the man who had his father's wife was among them. If, then, you mix with these, he says, it would be better for you to die; for it is a gain for evildoers if they die sooner rather than continue longer in sins.

(1 Cor. 5:11) But now I have written to you not to mix. If he who is called a brother among you is a fornicator, or covetous, or a server of idols, or a reviler, or a drunkard, or rapacious, with such a one not even to take food. He forbids taking food with a brother, but did not forbid it with an unbeliever, since indeed he says: If anyone of the unbelievers invites you to dinner and you wish to go, eat whatever is set before you (Luke 10:8). Therefore he says:

(1 Cor. 5:12) For what is it to me to judge those who are outside? It is true that a bishop cannot judge the faithless; but with a brother in whom these vices are found, he teaches that not only are the mysteries not to be eaten, but not even common food, so that he may blush when he is shunned, and correct himself.

(1 Cor. 5:13) Do you not judge those who are within? One judges when he discerns which brother he should cleave to, whom he should reprove, whom he should shun. For those who are outside, God will judge. Above: Do not, he says, judge before the time (1 Cor. 4:5); here he does not forbid it. But above he forbade judging unknown things on suspicion, especially concerning a ruler, because it belongs to God alone to judge hidden things; in this place, however, he even commands that brother examine brother (2 Cor. 13:5), as he says in another place: Test yourselves, whether you are in the faith: prove yourselves (2 Cor. 13:5). But those who are outside will be condemned on the day of judgment, for the Lord said: He who does not believe is already judged (John 3:18). For he in whom there is no hope is to be held as dead.

Remove the evil from among yourselves. He still warns them to separate themselves from evil, both works and men.

Chapter VI

(1 Cor. 6:1) Does any of you, having a matter against another, dare to be judged before the unjust, and not before the saints? He means "the unjust" in two senses: both those who do not believe, and those who often interpret with flattery the laws entrusted by God to the world, ascribing their authority to idols, and for this reason they are unjust. And because there is more law in the Church, where the Lord of the law is feared, he says it is better to plead a case before the ministers of God; for out of the fear of God they more readily bring forth a true sentence of the law.

(1 Cor. 6:2) Or do you not know that the saints will judge this world. The Apostle says nothing idly; for he said "this world" in order to show that there is another as well; for John the apostle too (1 John 2:15): Do not, he says, love this world. And the Lord in the Gospel: So, he says, God loved the world, etc. (John 3:16). This world, then, is in error, not that higher one, in whose likeness this one was made. Hence man was placed here, made in the image of God, so that, just as in the higher world all things are from one God, so in this one too all might have their origin from one man. Hence it is that Moses in Deuteronomy: When the Most High, he says, divided the nation, as he scattered the sons of Adam, he set the bounds of the nations according to the number of the angels of God. (Deut. 32:8). What is so plain as that this world is, for a definite reason, the image of the higher one? For there is both a lower Jerusalem and that higher one, which the same Apostle calls our mother (Gal. 4:26); there is a lower paradise, in which man was placed and received the command both to work there and to keep it (Gen. 2:8). There is also that heavenly one, into which the Apostle was caught up and heard secret words (2 Cor. 12:2). The saints, then, will judge this world, because by the example of their faith the faithlessness of the world will be condemned.

And if the world is to be judged in us. This world will be judged in us then, if the work of faithless men is not found in us. Are such men, then, unworthy to judge even concerning the smallest matters? That is, those who are going to judge the world itself are surely not unworthy to judge the things that belong to the world.

(1 Cor. 6:3) Do you not know that we shall judge angels? He added something more by saying: we shall judge angels, that is, spiritual powers, which he teaches in another place dwell in the heavenly places; meaning that the angels are to be judged by us in the same way as the world also will be judged.

(1 Cor. 6:4–5) If, then, you have secular judgments, appoint those who are contemptible in the Church to judge. Secular judgments are those that pertain to the body, or bodily ones. And because it ought not to be done so, he added: I say this to your shame. He says this lest, because above he named a "matter" but did not indicate of what kind, it be misunderstood; for there are also divine matters, as the same Apostle says: helper of my business (Rom. 16:2). And he says "secular judgments" perhaps because, since he had forbidden them to be conducted before the faithless, they thought that, though in the Church, as they had heard, they might nevertheless be heard or settled by anyone whatever; to make them blush he says: Appoint those who are contemptible in the Church to judge. For there are wooden vessels too in the Church; for he indicated that they were so intractable and thoughtless that they would perhaps choose unskilled men from among the brethren as judges. This the Apostle says from experience; for he knew how lukewarm and improvident they had been found to be in other matters.

So is there not among you anyone wise who can judge between his brother? Since, then, he says, there are wise brethren, let some of them be chosen to judge, whose judgment the world may admire. For it is a great shame if, among those who are said to have known God, no one is found who can examine a matter according to the law of the Gospel. But he says that a brother is to be chosen as judge because the ruler of their church had not yet been ordained.

(1 Cor. 6:6) But brother goes to law with brother, and that before unbelievers. It is clear that by unbelievers he means either arbiters called in, as is customarily done, or a public judge, to whom it is commonly said: Today is a day of sacred rites; justice cannot be pronounced.

(1 Cor. 6:7) Now indeed there is altogether a fault in you, that you have lawsuits among you. He indicates that it is a sin that brother goes to law with brother, when effort ought to be made that they be of one heart, like brothers by blood; especially since our faith is devoted to peace. For although a Christian ought not to go to law, nevertheless, if something serious has occurred, and such as cannot be disregarded, let him bring the case to the Church, lest he incur both loss for the present and perhaps offense as well.

(1 Cor. 6:8) Why do you not rather accept injury? Why not rather fraud? But you do injury and defraud, and that to brethren. Now he rebukes those by whose injustice the quarrel is produced; for they are guilty not only through the fraud they have committed, but they also have a share in the offense of those who, driven by their injury or fraud, ask unbelievers to judge; and whereas they ought not to pursue fraud or injuries inflicted on them, they not only avenge themselves, but they themselves also commit fraud and injuries, so that they give the brethren examples deserving punishment. And what may one believe he does to others, who does not spare his own?

(1 Cor. 6:9–10) Or do you not know that the unjust will not possess the kingdom of God? By saying this he shows that they do not sin in ignorance, and that for this reason it must be punished more severely. For if the unmerciful are guilty before God, how much more the unjust? Do not be deceived: for neither fornicators, nor those who serve idols, nor adulterers, nor the effeminate, nor those who lie with males, nor thieves, nor the covetous, nor drunkards, nor revilers, nor robbers will possess the kingdom of God. He did not mention these things as though they did not know them, they among whom he stayed a year and six months, teaching the Gospel of God; but he reminded them, in order to restore in them reverence for the Law, and merit for the kingdoms of heaven (Acts 18:11).

(1 Cor. 6:11) And this indeed you were. So as not to seem to ascribe this to all, or else to make all exempt from these crimes if he kept silent (for he seemed to have forbidden their crimes, not to have revealed them), he therefore speaks thus: And this indeed you were. And so as not to provoke them to anger, he added: But you have been washed; so that, acknowledging that they had become guilty of crimes, they might at once breathe again on hearing: But you have been washed. For some are wont to feel shame, and to amend, when they hear good things of themselves.

But you have been sanctified, but you have been justified in the name of our Lord Jesus Christ, and by the Spirit of our God. All these benefits of purity they are known to have obtained in baptism, which is the foundation of the truth of the Gospel. For there, all sins having been laid aside, the believer is washed and justified in the name of the Lord, and through the Spirit of our God is adopted as a son to God. By this he reminds them how great and of what kind is the grace which they obtained through the true tradition; but afterwards, thinking contrary to this rule of faith at the instigation of evil teachers, they stripped themselves of these benefits. Hence he works to call them back to the true and original belief, so that they might recover what they had obtained.

(1 Cor. 6:12) All things are lawful for me, but not all things are expedient. To some it seems that the Apostle said All things are lawful for me, etc., on account of the freedom of choice that has been granted, so that it is lawful to commit fornication, yet not expedient. But how is what is forbidden lawful? Or surely, if all things are lawful, nothing can be called unlawful. "But it is lawful, yet not expedient." These things they whisper rather than assert, being either forgetful or careless. For John the Baptist says to Herod: It is not lawful for you to have your brother's wife (Mark 6:18). But perhaps John was unacquainted with human speech, having been inspired in the womb; for if this is so, he ought to have said: It is lawful for you, but not expedient. But the Lord too likewise says to the Canaanite woman: It is not lawful to take the children's bread and throw it to the dogs (Matt. 15:26). And again the Lord says to the Jews: Have you not read what David did when he was hungry; how he entered the house of the Lord, and took the loaves of proposition, which it was not lawful for him to eat, etc. (Mark 2:25). Everything, then, that is forbidden is not lawful; and everything that is not lawful is forbidden; but sometimes, when some cause intervenes, a thing is lawful, but not expedient.

All things are lawful for me, but I will not be brought under anyone's power. He says that all things are lawful for him, namely those things which were lawful for his fellow apostles too, which the natural law also contains, not the law of Moses; for Moses forbade many things on account of the hardness of heart of an unbelieving and stiff-necked people (Matt. 19:8). Yet if you look to the occasion, something else was in the Apostle's mind when he said these things; for he set these words first, being about to deal with this matter later, just as he did also concerning the man who had committed incest; for he puts it forward, indicating what he would deal with later. Then he at once returns to the earlier sense, having for the moment briefly commended the matter; for he said this because it was lawful for him to receive support from them. But since he knew that the false apostles were seeking an occasion for receiving, he would not receive from them, lest for the belly's sake the vigor of the truth of the Gospel should grow torpid. For if he received from those whom he was reproving for such great vices, he would be bending the authority of teaching granted to him by the Lord; for one from whom something is received cannot be reproved with firmness: especially since such a man is ready to give for this very reason, that he may humble the one set over him to himself. Hence he also says: But I will not be brought under anyone's power.

(1 Cor. 6:13) Food for the belly, and the belly for foods; but God will destroy both it and them. The destruction of the belly is when the function of eating ceases; and the destruction of food will come then, when birth has come to a stop. But the false apostles, who went about not, indeed, for the sake of religion but for gain, did not wish to be bitter to those who sinned.

But the body is not for fornication, but for the Lord. Now he returns to the earlier sense: And the Lord for the body. What is the Lord for the body? It is understood: he will grant it immortality when it has been raised; for the body dedicated to God will be rewarded with a spiritual gift by the merit of its leader, that is, of the mind.

(1 Cor. 6:14) But God has both raised up the Lord, and will raise us up by his power. It is the same sense, which he wished to confirm by the example of the Lord's resurrection; for Christ is the power of God, by which he will raise us up, and the Lord himself. He said this because the Lord himself raised up his own body, just as the Lord himself says: Destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up. But he said this of the temple of his body (John 2:19).

(1 Cor. 6:15) Do you not know that your bodies are members of Christ? Our bodies are members of Christ, but in respect of the new man, who is created according to God; for he is the head of the Church. Shall I then take the members of Christ and make them members of a harlot? Far from it (Eph. 4:24). He says, Far from it, because members that cleave to a harlot cease to be members of Christ.

(1 Cor. 6:16) Or do you not know that he who cleaves to a harlot becomes one body? He says this because he who mingles himself with defilement becomes one with the one with whom he mingles himself. For fornication makes both one, so that, as they are one in nature, so they are one in stain as well. They shall be, he says, two in one flesh (Gen. 2:24). It is clear that, because the woman is from the man, both are in one flesh.

(1 Cor. 6:17) But he who cleaves to the Lord is one spirit. Because in those who do well the Spirit of God is common to God and to men.

(1 Cor. 6:18) Flee fornication. Rightly he warns that fornication is to be fled, through which the sons of God become sons of the devil. Every sin whatever that a man commits is outside the body. Because other sins, although they are produced through the body, nevertheless do not make the soul so bound and subject to carnal concupiscence as the practice of lust, acting in the work of carnal fornication, makes the soul to be mingled with the body itself; for the soul is glued to the body to such a degree that at that very moment a man can think of or attend to nothing else; for the very submersion and absorption in lust and carnal concupiscence subjugates the mind itself as a captive. Hence there follows:

But he who commits fornication sins against his own body. He shows that it is a most grave sin, because by it the whole body perishes, whereas in other sins a portion perishes, not the whole. For the whole body is man and woman, because the woman is a portion of the man. For whoever commits another sin sins outside himself; but the fornicator sins against his own flesh. Come now: if a man ends his life with a noose, or butchers himself with the sword, he does not seem to sin against his own body, but against the soul, to which he does violence. But to commit fornication is a bodily offense, which touches both the body and the soul: for the soul does not desire without the craving of the flesh (for the flesh too has its own motion), so as to sin against the flesh; although without the soul it can do nothing. By this, then, that he sins against the flesh which is from himself (for he referred it to its origin), he is said to sin against his own body; so that he may cease from fornication, as though sparing himself. To Novatian, however, he who commits fornication here seems to sin not against his own body but against Christ's; for he declares this otherwise, so as to say that even he who commits fornication sins against the Holy Spirit: on account of the cause for which he was cut off from the Church. This can by no reasoning be maintained; for the body of Christ is not one or two Christians, but all, while each one individually is a member. How then does he who commits fornication sin against the body of Christ, when he does not defile all? But if it were so, as it seems to Novatian, all other sins would be outside God, and fornication alone would be a sin against God. Even he who goes over to the idols does not sin against God, nor he who denies in persecution; because every sin whatever that a man commits is outside the body of Christ, as it seems to him. But if he chooses to declare this too otherwise, so as to deny that any sin is outside the body of Christ, he will be forced to say that everyone, whatever sin he has committed, sins against God and against the Holy Spirit; so that the thief, the perjurer and the liar would be said to sin against the Holy Spirit. And how does that stand which the Lord says: That all sins and blasphemy will be forgiven to men: but he who sins against the Holy Spirit, it will not be forgiven him, neither here nor in the future (Matt. 12:31)? It is clear, surely, that, apart from the other sins, there is another sin, which is called the sin against the Holy Spirit. Again, to some it seems that he who commits fornication is said to sin against his own body for this reason, that he is a member of the Church, and, defiling himself, sins against the Church, of which he is a member. Then it would have to be said of him who is a member of the Church, when he commits fornication, that he sins not against himself, but against the Church. But in fact the Apostle says that he who commits fornication, by the fact that he offends against his own portion, sins against his own body.

(1 Cor. 6:19) Or do you not know that your bodies are the temple of the Holy Spirit, who is in you, whom you have from God? Above (1 Cor. 3:16) he said the temple of God, here the temple of the Holy Spirit; because in substance the Spirit is what God is. He said this so that we may keep our bodies undefiled, so that the Holy Spirit may be able to dwell there.

(1 Cor. 6:20) And you are not your own; for you have been bought at a great price. It is clear that he who has been bought is not at his own disposal, but at that of him by whom he was bought, so that he does not do his own will, but that one's. And because we have been bought at a dear price, we ought to serve our Lord the more readily, lest, offended, he give us back to the death from which he redeemed us. For at how very dear a price did he buy us, that he should give his own blood for us?

Glorify and bear God in your body. This is to glorify and bear God in the body: that we walk according to his law; for through his law he himself is seen in us. Yet to bear God is this: to show the image of God in deeds well done.

Chapter VII

(1 Cor. 7:1) Now concerning those things which you wrote to me: it is good for a man not to touch a woman. Stirred up by the perverse notions of the false apostles, who out of hypocrisy taught that marriage was to be spurned, so that they might seem purer than the rest, they were asking the Apostle about these things by letter; for because they were not pleased with this opinion, they pass over everything else and ask about this alone. To them, however, he replied that it is indeed good not to touch a woman, although those men did not assert it in simplicity.

(1 Cor. 7:2) Nevertheless, because of fornication, that is, so that nothing be committed against the law, when at times what the law does not forbid is avoided; let each man have his own wife, and let each woman have her own husband. For those who seek a shortcut are accustomed to go astray. For how could it come about that these men should abstain from their wives, whom he found in such great vices? Therefore, then, he does not permit it, lest, abstaining from lawful things, they should presume upon things not granted, as the Manichaeans do.

(1 Cor. 7:3–4) Let the husband render the debt to the wife, and likewise also the wife to the husband. They are subject to each other in this matter, so that, since they are one body, theirs may also be one will in the law of nature. Let the woman not have power over her own body, but the husband; likewise also let the husband not have power over his own body, but the woman. He says this: that it is lawful neither for the husband nor for the wife to hand over his or her body to another; for they are debtors to each other in this matter, lest an occasion of sinning be given.

(1 Cor. 7:5) Do not defraud one another. He says this so that they may agree in the marital matter, lest disagreement beget fornication. Except by consent for a time; that is, that they abstain by agreement, for the sake of thanksgivings. That you may be free for prayer. For although one must pray without ceasing, since this meditation is to be practiced every day, yet so that they may apply themselves to prayer, he directs that they make themselves free for this, with times set between for winning the Lord's favor; for that he may have mercy, he must be entreated with greater purity. Although marriages are pure, yet one must abstain even from lawful things, so that prayer may more easily be brought to its effect. For in the law too, those who wished to be sanctified, among other things, also refrained from raisins at the Lord's command, that they might become holier (Num. 6:4). For when someone does not touch even things that are allowed, he shows that he wishes to receive what he prays for.

And return again to the same thing. Since he gives counsel to married people that after the days of prayer they ought to return to natural use: Lest Satan tempt you because of your intemperance; he advises that they abstain with days set between, lest an occasion be given to the devil, as the apostle Peter says: Behold, the devil, like a roaring lion, goes about seeking whom he may devour (1 Pet. 5:8). For if a wife is taken for the sake of begetting children, not much time seems to be granted for that use itself; because both feast days, and days of procession, and the very reckoning of conception and childbirth according to the law show that there ought to be a cessation at these times.

(1 Cor. 7:6–7) But I say this by way of indulgence, not by way of command. It is clear that he gives this counsel to exclude fornication, not to close the road to those striving toward a better life. But I wished all men to be as myself. As far, then, as befits a kindly and solicitous teacher, he wished all, if it could be, to be such as he himself was. And what sort he himself was, he shows presently in what follows: But each has his own gift from God, one in this way, another in that; that is, each has the gift of God according to his own desire, so that, if he wishes, he may obtain the ability by the assent of God himself. Therefore no one ought to be constrained, lest, forbidden what is lawful, he commit what is unlawful: but let him choose for himself what to follow.

(1 Cor. 7:8) But I say to the unmarried and to widows: it is good for them if they remain so, even as I. He would not say: it is good for the unmarried that they be even as I, unless he were intact in body. Nor would he say: I wished all men to be as myself; for if he had had a wife and said this, he did not want them to be virgins. But far be it; for from boyhood he served in such a way that he had no interest in this thing, seeing that as a youth he was forestalled by the grace of God. After he said that each has his own gift from God, he shows in which matter it is better to be more inclined; because a person is helped in that which he is seen to seek with eagerness of mind.

(1 Cor. 7:9) But if they do not contain themselves, let them marry. In this matter he wants them to strive, so as to be continent. But if they see that, because of the impulse of the flesh, they cannot persevere, because they are not very ready in this very thing so as to be helped by God (for God helps him whom he sees striving with all his strength), let them by all means marry, if they fear to burn. For perhaps they will be able to attain this afterwards; for once the hindrance has been worked off, the will restores itself with sturdier strength.

For it is better to marry than to burn. He did not say: For it is better to marry than to burn, as though it were good to burn, and therefore to marry were better: but he followed common usage. For we are accustomed to say: It is better to make a profit than a loss. To burn, however, is to be driven or overcome by desires; for when the will consents to the heat of the flesh, it burns: for to suffer desires and not be overcome is the mark of an illustrious and perfect man.

(1 Cor. 7:10–11) But to those who are joined in marriage I give charge, not I, but the Lord. After he has spoken to the unmarried and to widows, he addresses those who are joined in marriage, with the Lord's mouth: That a wife not depart from her husband; but if she has departed, that she remain unmarried. This is the Apostle's counsel: that if she has departed because of the bad conduct of her husband, she should from then on remain unmarried. Or be reconciled to her husband. But if, he says, she cannot contain herself, because she does not wish to fight against the flesh, let her be reconciled to her husband; for a woman is not permitted to marry if she has put away her husband on account of fornication, or of apostasy, or if, driven by unlawful lust, he seeks the use of his wife; because the inferior does not in every respect enjoy this law which the superior enjoys. If, however, the husband has apostatized, or seeks to pervert the use of his wife, the woman can neither marry another, nor return to him. And that a husband not put away his wife. It is understood, however: except for the cause of fornication. And therefore he did not add, as he did about the woman: but if he has departed, that he remain so; because it is lawful for the husband to marry a wife if he has put away a sinning wife: because the husband is not bound by the law in the same way as the woman; for the man is the head of the woman.

(1 Cor. 7:12) For to the rest I say, not the Lord. He said this to show what the Lord commanded with his own mouth, and what he granted to this man's authority; because the Lord speaks through him too. For he says: Or do you seek a proof of him who speaks in me, Christ (2 Cor. 13:3).

(1 Cor. 7:13) If any brother has an unbelieving wife, and she consents to live with him, let him not put her away. And if any woman has an unbelieving husband, and he consents to live with her, let her not leave her husband. He said this because in those beginnings, when both were of course Gentiles, it happened that one of them believed: and because the faithless shrank in horror from the worship of God, and likewise the believers from the defilement of their former error, therefore he directs that, if those were content to live with the ones who had been changed, the faithful too should be content to be with them.

(1 Cor. 7:14) For the unbelieving husband is sanctified through the faithful wife, and the unbelieving woman is sanctified by the faithful husband. He shows that they have the benefit of a good will, because they do not have a horror of the name of Christ: and it pertains to the protection of the household in which the sign of the cross is made, by which death was conquered; for it is a sanctification.

Otherwise your children would be unclean. Their children would be unclean if they put away those willing to live with them and joined themselves to others; for they would be adulterers, and through this their children too would be bastards; therefore unclean. But now they are holy. They are holy, because they are born of lawful marriages: and because they are born under the veneration of the Creator on the more devoted side. For just as whatever is done through dedication to idols is unclean, so whatever is done under the profession of God the Creator is holy.

(1 Cor. 7:15) But if the unbeliever departs, let him depart. He guards the purpose of religion by directing that Christians not abandon their marriages: but if the unbeliever departs out of hatred of God, the faithful one will not be guilty of a dissolved marriage; for the cause of God is greater than that of marriage.

(Gratian, Decretum C. 28, q. 2, c. Si infidelis) For a brother or sister is not subjected to bondage in such cases. That is, the reverence of marriage is not owed to one who shrinks in horror from the author of marriage; for a marriage that is without devotion to God is not ratified: and therefore it is not a sin for one who is put away for God's sake, if he joins himself to another. For the insult to the Creator dissolves the right of marriage with respect to the one who is left, so that he is not accused when joined to another. But the unbeliever who departs is recognized to sin both against God and against marriage, because he did not wish to have marriage under devotion to God. Therefore the faith of marriage is not to be kept with him, because he withdrew precisely so as not to hear that the God of the Christians is the author of marriage. For (1 Esd. 9 ff.) if Ezra caused unbelieving wives or husbands to be put away, that God might become propitious and not be angry, if they took others from their own race (for it was not so commanded them that, having sent these away, they should not marry others at all), how much more, if the unbeliever has departed, will she have free choice, if she wishes to marry a man of her own law? For that ought not to be accounted a marriage which was made outside the decree of God: but when one afterwards recognizes it and grieves at having transgressed, one amends oneself, so as to merit pardon. But if both have believed, they confirm the marriage through the knowledge of God.

But God has called us in peace. It is true, because one ought not to quarrel with one who departs; because he departs out of hatred of God, and for this reason is not to be held worthy either.

(1 Cor. 7:16) For how do you know, woman, whether you will save your husband? Or whence do you know, man, whether you will save your wife? He says this because those who do not shrink in horror from the name of Christ may perhaps believe.

(1 Cor. 7:17) Except as God has apportioned to each. The Lord has apportioned to each when he is to be saved; that is, he knows when he can believe, and he bears with him. And here he teaches that they are to be waited for, and that one ought not to take offense at them, because their hope must be guarded; but if they themselves depart, one must let them go unregarded.

As God has called each one, so let him walk. That is, that if one who believes is joined in marriage, he should not desert, neither husband nor wife: but let him remain so, whether Jew or Greek.

As I teach in all the churches. So as to persuade them that he does not hand down to them anything other than to the rest; so that, when they hear that the others of this faith are taught this, they may follow it more readily. For a person is easily inclined if he sees that his companion has consented.

(1 Cor. 7:18) Was anyone called circumcised? Let him not bring on a foreskin. This is what he said, that he should walk as he was called; so that a Jew made a Christian may not perhaps think himself unworthy because he does not have a foreskin, giving glory to the foreskin. Was anyone called with a foreskin? Let him not be circumcised. So that a Gentile who has been called may not judge that he ought to be circumcised, by the fact that he hears the sons of Abraham preferred. For they are not preferred because they are circumcised, but by the merit of Abraham, whose sons they are in both respects, if they believe: if not, they will be worse than the Gentiles; for it is worse to have been and not to be, than never to have been.

(1 Cor. 7:19) Circumcision is nothing, and foreskin is nothing. It is clear that it neither harms nor helps. But the observance of the commandments of God, that is, faith makes God propitious, if it is approved by good works.

(1 Cor. 7:20–21) Let each one remain in that calling in which he was called. This confirms what he said above. Were you called as a slave? Let it not be a care to you: but even if you can become free, make use of it rather. He exhorts that, by serving his carnal master well out of the fear of God, he should make himself worthy of freedom; lest, hearing perhaps: Were you called as a slave? Let it be no care to you, he should become more negligent regarding good conduct toward his carnal master, and the teaching of Christ be blasphemed, and he should not win the favor of God, he who, serving well in these earthly things, lays up merit for himself with God: because the Lord said: He who is faithful in the least is also in the great (Luke 16:10).

(1 Cor. 7:22) For he who was called in the Lord as a slave is the Lord's freedman. For, rescued from sins, which truly prove men slaves, he becomes a freedman; for he is altogether a slave who acts imprudently, as also seemed right to the ancients, who called all the wise free, but all the imprudent slaves. Hence also Solomon: To a wise slave, he says, the free will be slaves (Sir. 10:28). This man, then, who believes, even if he is a slave for a time, because he does a prudent thing in believing in Christ, becomes the Lord's freedman. If, then, sins make slaves, as Ham the son of Noah was made a slave on account of sin and imprudence, when someone receives the remission of sins, he is made a freedman.

Likewise he who was called free is a slave of Christ. It is an advance, from a free man to become a slave of Christ: for he was free from God, which is the greatest crime: and therefore, having lost a bitter and hostile freedom, he has obtained a condition that would profit him, as the Lord says: Take my yoke upon you, for it is sweet: and my burden, for it is light (Matt. 11:29). Thus he has cut off pride and made unity; so that neither the slave, from shame at his condition, should think himself despised, nor the free man, puffed up with elation of mind, should set himself above the slave.

(1 Cor. 7:23) You were bought with a price; do not become slaves of men. It is true, because we were bought so dearly that we could have been redeemed by no one except by Christ, who is rich in all things. He, then, who is bought with a price ought to serve the more, so as to make some return to his buyer. Bought, then, by God, that is, by Christ, they ought not to be slaves of men. Now the slaves of men are those who subject themselves to human superstitions. The Apostle, however, recalls that which he reproved at the head of the epistle, because they were saying: I am of Paul; and another: I of Apollos (1 Cor. 1:12). Yet he has given a ground for confessing Christ as God, when he shows that the slaves of Christ are not to be called slaves of a man.

(1 Cor. 7:24) Let each one, brother, remain with God in that in which he was called. This is what he said above; he repeats it so as to commend it fully.

(1 Cor. 7:25) Now concerning virgins I have no commandment of the Lord. This he answered to their writings, as he said above: But concerning the things of which you wrote to me, etc. (1 Cor. 7:1). But I give counsel, as one who has obtained mercy from the Lord to be faithful. He says that he has not received a commandment about virgins, because the author of marriage could not command a thing opposed to marriage, lest he accuse his own former deed. But counsel, he says, I give: not something that displeases, nor something colored by flattery; because he obtained grace to this end, that he might be fit in giving salutary counsels.

(1 Cor. 7:26) I think, therefore, that this is good because of the present necessity; because it is best for a man to be so. He says that it is good to remain a virgin. And to show this more absolutely, he says because of the present necessity; because it is best, so that it might be recognized that between good and best nothing is so excellent and useful as virginity; because he teaches that virginity is more commendable not only with God but also in respect of the present life, since it does not know the necessities of the time which married life suffers, and is ignorant of the sorrows of childbirth together with the bereavements of children. In order, then, to encourage them to aspire to it, he teaches that those women who have loved it are spared the ruinous effects of the present necessity; so that, when they learn that it holds a better place not only with God but also in the present, they might seek it with all diligence. For there is one thing in which it labors: to overcome the affection of the flesh, being free in all other things. For because the flesh is in its origin and nature, its use seems pleasant and its affection sweet; therefore to overcome these things is no slight glory.

(1 Cor. 7:27) Are you bound to a wife? Do not seek release. These are counsels of remedies; for he says that no one ought to be released from his wife except for the cause of fornication. For frequently those who put away their own wives under cover of continence deceive other women, their purpose growing cold. But if, placed with his wife, he wishes to live better, let him exhort her, that they may live more purely without scandal.

Are you released from a wife? Do not seek a wife. So that, knowing this to be more acceptable to God and free from necessity, he may not seek a wife; but in such a way that he restrains himself from other things. If not, what does it profit to suppress the desire of the flesh, that is, to spurn a lawful thing, and lie subject to unlawful things?

(1 Cor. 7:28) But if you take a wife, you have not sinned. He certainly does not sin, because he does what is granted. But if he has scorned it, he lays up for himself merit and a crown. For it is a mark of great patience not to touch this which is not forbidden. And if a virgin marries, she has not sinned. She does not sin, because before God she is free in this matter.

Yet such will have tribulations of the flesh. That is, even if they are free from sin, yet in this life they will have tribulations: the groaning of the womb, the nurture of children, food, clothing, dowries, sickness, the furnishing of the house, the wife's need, the husband's domination.

But I spare you. He spares when he urges them rather toward those things which exclude the tribulations of the flesh and the cares mentioned. He can be seen to spare in this way, when he permits, and does not oppose those who wish, what he shows to be burdensome.

(1 Cor. 7:29) This therefore I say, brethren: the time has been shortened, and it is what remains. In calling the time shortened and remaining, he signified that the end is imminent for the world, although he knew that times still remained. But he ought not to have written otherwise, on account of those who would be reading these things at the time when very short time will remain; lest, while the day of judgment was always thought to be far off, it should either not be feared, or be judged false. But to say that it is imminent, when it is still far off, profits much; for it strikes men with terror, so that they lead a better life. Indeed, here in the present, how anxious are those who have cases before judges, when the day of the proceedings is said to be imminent? Here this has the same sound as what he says in another place: deceivers, and yet true (2 Cor. 6:8).

That both those who have wives be so, as though not having. For since the end of the world is near, they ought not to be anxious about begetting, nor devoted to the use itself; so that, more inclined and watchful in divine works, and practiced in the care of the Law, they may fight back when the battle is imminent. For there will be distresses such as never have been, and many will fall into the snare of the devil: indeed none of us wishes these things to come about in his own time, fearing the distresses foretold by the Savior. (Matt. 24:15 ff.). Therefore, as we wish for ourselves, let us take counsel for others too; that we abstain from the begetting of many children, more given to prayer and to the service of God, foreseeing the day of judgment; lest both we be unprepared through the hindrances of the present necessity, and they incur what we fear. Even from lawful things, then, he wishes the faithful to restrain themselves, that they may appear not only innocent but also glorious: for to pass over what is allowed is a mark of the greatest virtue; and not to desire what is forbidden is not far off.

(1 Cor. 7:30) And those who weep, as though not weeping; for, knowing that, since the end of the world is near, consolation will soon come for those who are perhaps oppressed for the righteousness of God, let them console themselves among themselves with this hope. And those who rejoice, as though not rejoicing; for let those who rejoice in the present know that mourning will soon come, namely to those who rejoice in the world, doubting about the judgment of God. And those who buy, as though not possessing; for those who buy, believing that little time remains to the world, should so act as not to spend all their care on a thing that will soon perish: but rather let them take care of their soul, which they know to be eternal.

(1 Cor. 7:31) And those who use this world, as though they did not use it: that is, that they should not presume upon this use, because it will quickly perish when the world collapses. For the figure of this world passes away. Because he said that the world is coming to an end, he shows that its figure passes away; and that this is the end, that is, that not its substance passes away, but its form. Therefore, if the form of the world will perish, there is surely no doubt that all things that are in the world perish. It passes away because the world grows old every day.

(1 Cor. 7:32) I want you to be without anxiety. For when the anxiety of the world is lessened, one keeps watch more readily in the things of God. And how we can be without anxiety he shows, saying: He who is without a wife thinks of the things that are the Lord's. For when the anxiety of the marital matter is cut away, which alone in the world is heavier than the rest, the mind is trained for winning God's favor: if, however, the mind strips itself of these troubles in this hope, that it may serve God more.

(1 Cor. 7:33) But he who is with a wife is anxious about the things that are of this world, how he may please his wife. For care of a wife and children is a thought of the world: for among other things men sometimes commit punishable deeds, so as not to offend them, as Zerubbabel, one of the three chamberlains of Artaxerxes, relates (1 Esd. 4:13): for a sad wife is great bitterness in a house. He is called divided for this reason, because he cannot both apply himself to divine things and do his wife's will.

The woman is divided, and the virgin. Divided not, of course, in nature, but in conduct; because we read in Numbers that women were called virgins (Num. 31:9). The anxiety of a woman, then, is one thing, that of a virgin another; yet when "woman" is said, it is uncertain what it signifies, unless a specification follows: but when "virgin" is said, the expression is clear. Here the Apostle placed virgin after woman, so as to signify a woman who is not a virgin: for by this he wished to show that the virgin is free from the wearinesses and labors which a woman joined to a husband suffers.

(1 Cor. 7:34) For she who is not married is anxious about the things that are the Lord's, that she may be holy both in body and in spirit: that is, in mind; for while, in the hope of heavenly things, she does not take on the anxiety of husband and children, she thinks of the Lord, how she may guard the purpose devoted to God; if, however, in a pure body the mind, casting off earthly things, is zealous for heavenly things. For it is the mind that either sanctifies or pollutes the body. For what does it profit to have a pure body and a polluted soul, since by the merit of the soul the body is either honored or condemned? But she who is married thinks of the things that are of this world, how she may please her husband. For, bound by the marital law, she is anxious how to fulfill the right of the marital duty, subject to worldly necessities.

(1 Cor. 7:35) But I say this for your own advantage; not to cast a snare upon you, but for that which is honorable. Since to some what is more useful or better seems harsh and heavy because of the habit of temporal use, therefore, after giving his reason, he exhorts with the affection of love that what he says is simply more useful, more honorable. It is honorable because it is holy and pure; useful, because it is more worthy before God, and light in the world. What then shall we say? If virgins think of God, and those joined to husbands of the world, what hope is left before God for those who marry? For if it is so, their salvation is in doubt: for we see virgins thinking of the world, and those joined in marriage devoting themselves to the Lord's works. To these virgins holiness will not be credited by God, and these marriages will have a reward before God; because, though they were bound by earthly ties and the ties of the flesh, they gave their effort to merit some immortal reward for the future. To those, however, not only will virginity not be credited, but they will also be subjected to punishments, who, under cover of a better hope, occupying their life and conduct with worldly care and anxiety, have made themselves sluggish in doing the works of God; as Jeremiah the prophet says: Cursed is he who does the works of God negligently (Jer. 48:10). These are those of whom he says in another epistle: Having indeed the appearance of piety, but denying its power (2 Tim. 3:5). The Apostle, however, speaks to those who desire with wonderful devotion to obey the heavenly precepts, showing and teaching by what shortcut one goes more quickly to God; because he who wishes to remain a virgin for the sake of winning God's favor, knowing what reward he can receive who refrains from what is lawful so as to become better, at the same time spurns all the hindrances of the flesh, not unaware that these are the things which hold back the step of the runner as if with the weights of fetters.

(1 Cor. 7:36) But if anyone sees himself disgraced over his virgin, if she is past puberty, and so it ought to be done; let him do what he wishes, he does not sin, if she marries. Since above he so exhorted, with his reason given, that virginity and continence be held, that in comparison with them marriages almost seem useless and to be cast aside, now, lest he be thought to deny marriage, he shows that the virgin does not sin if she marries; but that she undertakes a matter of the greatest labor, and one that has no reward before God, just as it has no punishment: for he wants Christians to be better in all things. If, then, a girl is in desire of marriage, already ripe for its use, it is better that she marry publicly according to the law that has been granted, than that she do it shamefully in secret, and shame be brought on in her case.

(1 Cor. 7:37) For he who has resolved in his heart, steadfast, having no necessity, but has power concerning his own will, and has decided this in his heart, to keep his virgin, does well. He says this so that he who has a virgin whose mind is not set on marriage may keep her, and not thrust upon her the kindling of marriage, when he sees that she has no will to marry. If benefits are to be bestowed, how much more are they not to be taken away?

(1 Cor. 7:38) So also he who gives his virgin in marriage does well. He does well, because what he does is lawful. And he who does not give her in marriage does better. He does better, because he both lays up merit for her with God and frees her from the anxiety of the world.

(1 Cor. 7:39) A woman is bound by the law as long as her husband lives. He pursues these things for this reason, to teach that a woman, even if she has been cast out by her husband, does not have license to marry. But if her husband has died, she is set free. These things too pertain to the causes stated above; to show how blessed the virgin is, since she is subject to no one except God alone. For she seems to overcome natural subjection, when she raises up what was humbled by nature. Let her marry whom she wishes, only in the Lord: that is, let her marry him whom she thinks suited to her, because unwilling marriages usually have bad outcomes. Only in the Lord, that is, that she marry without suspicion of disgrace, and marry a man of her own religion: this is to marry in the Lord.

(1 Cor. 7:40) But she will be more blessed if she remains so, according to my counsel. When he says above, let her marry, he speaks by natural law; although first marriages are from God, second ones are permitted. Indeed, first marriages are celebrated sublimely under the blessing of God: second ones, however, lack glory even in the present; they are granted, though, because of incontinence: and because the younger of the widows are wont to fall; and for this reason he grants second marriages. But because it is better that she contain herself, so that she may be more worthy in the future, he gives a counsel, excellent by spiritual reasoning, that she contain herself. For I think that I too have the Spirit of God; to show his counsel to be settled and provident, he commends it by humility of word with a lofty sense.

Chapter VIII

(1 Cor. 8:1) Now concerning the things that are sacrificed to idols, we know that we all have knowledge. Now he is going to speak about knowledge: what this knowledge is he does not say at once, but he shows it in what follows, saying that an idol is nothing.

Knowledge puffs up. It is plain that one who has knowledge glories in that very thing, if not outwardly, since he is prudent, then at least within himself. For this is the nature of knowledge, that it glories in itself; therefore it puffs up. But love builds up. So knowledge is great, and useful to itself, at the time when it is humbled by love, so that it may grow the more. For it is tempered by love, so that it may not be too unmixed and intoxicate the one who knows, so that he exalts himself. For just as wine not tempered by an admixture of water estranges the mind, so too knowledge makes a man proud unless it has been tempered. For all things taken alone are unpleasant and harmful; for neither is bread eaten well by itself, nor are the other foods agreeable without admixture, but harmful; therefore love builds up. Love itself, although it is called by a single word, nevertheless consists of many things; for it cannot exist without patience, nor without humility, nor without simplicity of heart. These men, then, were puffed up by the knowledge that an idol is nothing, who, against the salvation of unskilled brethren, were eating the meat of sacrifices, knowing that it was lawful to eat meat, and that, since an idol is nothing, the one who eats is therefore not defiled. These men were a stumbling block to the brethren, not having love before their eyes; for it was a greater thing to despise meat than to put a stumbling block before the brethren. For their knowledge was destroying the minds of the unskilled brethren, who thought that there was some grace there at the idol, when they saw the more skilled brethren present there and not shrinking from it. Therefore he says: Love builds up; so that, being zealous for the salvation of the brethren, they should not use there the laws of knowledge, lest they put a stumbling block before them.

(1 Cor. 8:2) But if anyone thinks that he knows something, he has not yet known in what way he ought to know. He shows that those very men who glory in knowledge have not known in the way one ought to know. For as long as they do not pursue love, which is the mother of all good things, they do not know as they ought. So, in order that knowledge may be able to bear fruit, it must subject itself to love.

(1 Cor. 8:3) But if anyone loves God, this one is known by him. He loves God who moderates his knowledge for the sake of love, so as to benefit his brother, for whom Christ died.

(1 Cor. 8:4) Concerning, then, the foods of things sacrificed to images. Now he treats the matter itself, to show by reason that knowledge, when love is despised, not only does not profit but even does harm. We know that an image is nothing, and that there is no God but one. That is true; for among Christians there is one God.

(1 Cor. 8:5) Although there are those who are called gods and lords, whether in heaven or on earth. For by the pagans the sun and the moon and the other stars are called heavenly gods; on earth they call Apollo, Aesculapius, Hercules and Minerva gods and lords.

(1 Cor. 8:6) Yet for us there is one God the Father, from whom are all things, and we in him. For all things, whatever they are, wherever they are, are from him. But when he says: And we in him, he has distinguished us from the rest, who, although they are from him, are nevertheless not in him, as long as they do not yet believe. And one Lord Jesus, through whom are all things, and we through him. For all things are indeed from the Father, but they were created through the Son. But when he says: And we through him, he signifies that we have been re-formed in the knowledge of God through him through whom we had been created; for, having been made through him together with the rest, after the stupor of mind and of ignorance we have through him acknowledged the mystery of the one God. So he called the Father one God, and Jesus his Son one Lord, so that, since God cannot fail to be Lord, the Lord likewise might be understood to be God as well, and so he might show that God and Lord are one, preserving the manner of one principle.

(1 Cor. 8:7) But knowledge is not in all. That is, not all believers know the mystery of the one God; therefore some of those who believe think that there is something of a divine power in images.

For some, with conscience of the idol until now, eat as though it were something sacrificed to an image. For some of the people were still eating of the things sacrificed with veneration for the image, as though there were some divinity there. And the conscience of others, since it is weak, is defiled. The conscience is stained if it is weak in regard to God.

(1 Cor. 8:8) But food does not commend us to God. That is true, for we shall not please God on that account, if we eat everything, or offend him if we have spurned some things. For neither, if we eat, shall we abound; nor, if we do not eat, shall we be in want. For if the food of sacrifices is spurned, there will surely be no lack of something to eat; but if it is taken, it will not make one abound, so that nothing further is needed: and for this reason it is to be rejected, since it is not the case that one cannot live without it, and it is a stumbling block to the brethren.

(1 Cor. 8:9) See, however, lest perhaps your liberty become an offense to the weak. That is, lest, because you say that it is lawful for you to eat meat from the sacrifice, on the ground that you know that an idol is nothing, you cause offense to the brethren who do not yet know that an idol is nothing.

(1 Cor. 8:10) For if anyone sees him who has knowledge reclining in an idol-temple, will not his conscience, since it is weak, be built up to eat of the images? He said this because someone, seeing a skilled brother who has been invited into an idol-temple reclining and eating the meat of sacrifices, thinks that there is some grace there, and he too begins to eat the meat of sacrifices, not with that conscience with which the other, on account of his skill, knows that an idol is nothing, but with the one by which he thinks there is a divine power there, when he sees the skilled brother eating there and not shrinking from it.

(1 Cor. 8:11) And will the brother who is weak perish in your knowledge, for whom Christ died? The weak man will perish if he eats of the things sacrificed against faith in the one God: these indeed are the words, as it were, of one who is angry and who shows what evil knowledge can bring forth if it has not been tempered by love. In your knowledge: that is, your skill kills him, when he sees done by you what he understands otherwise. And you will be the occasion of death to your brother, to redeem whom Christ allowed himself to be crucified. And so he teaches that knowledge must sometimes be disregarded for the sake of love; for without doubt the salvation of a brother is worth more than food: for it is lawful indeed, but it is not expedient. This will be to have known as one ought to know.

(1 Cor. 8:12) But sinning thus against the brethren, and striking their weak conscience, you sin in Christ. For when they are not emulators of the love by which Christ set us free, they sin in Christ, not against Christ; for to sin against Christ is to deny Christ, but to sin in Christ is to sin against the things that are Christ's. Just as he also who is under the Law is said to sin in the Law, so too those who are under Christ are said to sin in Christ.

(1 Cor. 8:13) Wherefore, if food makes my brother stumble, I will not eat meat for ever, lest I make my brother stumble. He teaches that love is to be pursued to such a degree that lawful things are to be held as unlawful, lest they harm a brother. For how great an evil is it not to transgress through unlawful things, and yet to offend through those that are permitted, so that the Law is kept in unlawful things and is not observed in lawful ones, while they are eaten without consideration? For the Law permitted what it granted in such a way that there should nevertheless be a measure for men to keep. It is certainly lawful to have a wife, but if she has committed fornication, she is to be put away: so too it is lawful to eat meat, but if it has been offered to idols, it is to be rejected.

Chapter IX

(1 Cor. 9:1) Am I not an apostle? Am I not free? Have I not seen Jesus our Lord? While he seems to deny, he affirms; for he speaks in anger. For he proves himself an apostle, because he was sent to the Gentiles, the Lord saying to him: I will send you to the Gentiles (Acts 22:21). And he shows himself free, because he coveted nothing of anyone's, nor did he teach in flattery. And he saw Jesus our Lord while he was praying in the temple at Jerusalem (Acts 22:18). Are you not my work in the Lord? They are his work for this reason, because he himself planted them in the Lord, preaching to them the word of God for a year and six months.

(1 Cor. 9:2) Even if to others I am not an apostle, yet to you I am; because by the Jews who believed, and nonetheless kept the Law, it was denied that he was an apostle. For he was already teaching that men ought not to be circumcised, nor the sabbath kept; but since the other apostles, on account of the stumbling block, held back from this matter, he seemed to teach otherwise; therefore it was denied by these men that he was an apostle; for he was an apostle to those who had seen in him the signs of power. For you are the seal of my apostleship in the Lord; for through his preaching they were converted from idols to faith in the one God.

(1 Cor. 9:3) My defense to those who question me is this. Now he begins to plead the case which he set out above, saying: All things are lawful for me, but I will not be brought under the power of anyone. Food for the belly and the belly for foods (1 Cor. 6:12). So he begins this case in such a way as to say: My defense to those who question me is this. And what it is he adds, saying:

(1 Cor. 9:4–5) Do we not have the power of eating and drinking? This is what he had said: All things are lawful for me. Do we not have the power of leading a woman about, as also the other apostles and the brothers of the Lord, and Cephas? He added And Cephas for this reason: because, although he was the first among the apostles, he did not refuse the provisions offered. Cephas is Simon Peter himself. For women, out of desire for the Lord's teaching and eager for virtues, followed the apostles, supplying them both provisions and services, just as they also followed the Savior, ministering to him from their means.

(1 Cor. 9:6) Or do I alone and Barnabas not have the power of doing these things? That is, we have the power of doing so, but we do not wish to. Yet he says of doing in place of "of receiving", so as to speak more decently. And to confirm this, he adds examples, saying:

(1 Cor. 9:7–8) Who ever serves as a soldier at his own wages? Who plants a vineyard and does not eat of its fruit? Who feeds a flock of sheep and does not receive of their milk? Do I say these things according to man? That is, do I follow a human opinion? Does not the Law also say the same? He has taught that the testimonies mentioned above agree with the divine teachings, to show that it is surely not without reason that he is unwilling to accept provisions from them, since by so many examples he shows that one ought both to give and to receive.

(1 Cor. 9:9) For in the Law (Deut. 25:4) it is written: You shall not bind the mouth of the ox that threshes. Is it for oxen that God cares? Meanwhile let us settle this in a few words; for in the prophet Jonah it is written: Shall I not spare the city in which there dwell more than a hundred and twenty thousand men, and many cattle (Jon. 4:11). And the Psalmist: Men and beasts, he says, you will save (Ps. 35:7). How, then, does God not care about oxen and cattle? But he does not care about oxen and cattle for their own sake, but for ours, for whose sake they were made; therefore he cares about us, not about them.

(1 Cor. 9:10) Or rather, he says, he says it for our sake: for it was written for our sake. For through a figure all the things said pass over to us. Because he who ploughs and threshes in hope ought to receive the fruit of his hope. That is true, for one labors in this hope, that the fruit may be gathered.

(1 Cor. 9:11) If we have sown spiritual things for you, is it a great thing if we reap your carnal things? He says that it is neither a great thing nor an equal one, if those who preach the kingdom of God and fatten the Church with heavenly food were to take nourishment for the body, so that, while handing on eternal things, they should obtain temporal ones.

(1 Cor. 9:12) If others make use of your power, do not we the more? For if those who are not your fathers through the Gospel, and do not have the same affection toward you, have the right of receiving, how much more do we? And "your apostles" is understood; for Barnabas too was an apostle.

But we have not used this power; rather we endure all things, lest we put any hindrance in the way of the Gospel of Christ. He has disclosed a meaning long hidden; for he shows that he was unwilling to accept provisions, though it was lawful, for this reason: lest the vigor of the Gospel's authority grow sluggish. For the false apostles flattered those who sinned, since they wished to receive; so that, being subject to them, these did not seem to themselves to be sinning: but since this practice was contrary to the discipline of the Gospel, he is unwilling to receive, so that he may rebuke freely, lest he be found in their number. For in order that the right of the Christian rule might remain, he made lawful things unlawful; because those men gave offense through license.

(1 Cor. 9:13) Do you not know that those who work in the sanctuaries live from the sanctuary, and those who serve the altar partake of the altar? By "in the sanctuaries" he signifies the law of the Gentiles, but by "the altar" that of the Jews. For thus the Lord decreed through Moses (Deut. 18:3), that the priests should take a part of the things that were offered.

(1 Cor. 9:14) So also the Lord Jesus ordained that those who announce the Gospel should live by the Gospel. God, then, did not follow the pattern of the Gentiles through Moses; but natural reason itself holds this, that a man should live from the place where he labors. This principle, then, he decreed for nature itself and for human life. Yet by adding these things still to the heap, he gives weight to the case, so as not to make light or odious what he commends by so many testimonies: and yet he does not wish to profit by it himself, lest he humble the freedom of rebuking.

(1 Cor. 9:15) But I have used none of these things. This after he has shown in many ways that it is lawful to receive, yet that he himself has no need of it; for it is harmful to receive from those who sin, as I mentioned above. But I have not written these things so that it may be done so in my case; for it is better for me rather to die. No one shall make my glory void. He says that his glory lies in this, if the severity of the Gospel remains: and he chose rather to die than to violate the rights of the Gospel, knowing that this profits him more toward future salvation. For he refused that the service due to him should be rendered to him by these men, lest through this they gain confidence in sinning.

(1 Cor. 9:16) For if I preach the gospel, there is no glorying for me; for necessity lies upon me. As the Lord says: When you have done what I tell you, say: We are unprofitable servants: we have done what we ought to do (Luke 17:10). For woe will be to me, he says, if I do not preach the Gospel. For a servant sent by his master does, even against his will, what he has been commanded: if not, he must be held to it with blows. Thus Moses was sent against his will to Pharaoh (Exod. 4:14); and Jonah was compelled to preach to the Ninevites (Jon. 1:3).

(1 Cor. 9:17) For if I do this willingly, I have a reward. It is plain that no one receives a recompense for a thing which he does against his will. For if he is willing, he is worthy of a reward, because he is in agreement with his master; and what is done by will is done better. But if against my will, a dispensation has been entrusted to me. Nor is this obscure, for one who does something without devotion does it not by will but by necessity. For because he is not in his own power, he does against his will what he has been commanded.

(1 Cor. 9:18) What then will be my reward? That, announcing the Gospel, I may make the Gospel without expense, so as not to abuse my power in the Gospel. Holding back any boastfulness of words, he glories, and wishes it to be understood how great a reward he is worthy of, because he does not use his power in the Gospel, while he spurns the provisions offered, lest he humble the force of the preaching. This is: it is lawful, but not expedient, for the sake of the brethren's salvation, that they may be corrected. So he wishes to be a pattern to the others, so that where they see it is not expedient, they should not make use even of lawful things: if not, they will become guilty through a lawful thing, since they take it in such a way that it serves to do harm.

(1 Cor. 9:19) For though I am free from all, I have made myself the servant of all, that I might gain more. He is free from all, since he handed on the Gospel to no one in flattery, nor coveted anything of anyone's except his salvation, nor, in order to have luxuries, accepted from anyone more lavish provisions as the wage of hypocrisy. But he says that he made himself the servant of all in this way, while through humility he showed himself like all who are weak in mind, so that through patience he might strengthen them for the future salvation, cherishing the minds of those who were either sinning or following divine things too slowly, so that they would not take it harshly to be rebuked.

(1 Cor. 9:20) And to the Jews I became as a Jew, that I might gain the Jews. He seems, as it were, to have dissembled, in order to become all things to all, as flatterers are accustomed to do; but it is not so. For a man of God and a spiritual physician, knowing the causes and wounds of all, cherishes them with great diligence and suffers with them; for we have certain things in common with all men. So he became to the Jews as a Jew, because on account of the stumbling block to them he circumcised Timothy (Acts 16:3), and, having been purified, went up into the temple, lest they take occasion through him to blaspheme (Acts 21:26). So he did a thing which ought already to have ceased, but he did it according to the Law. For he both agreed with them that the Law and the prophets are from God, and from them showed that this is the Christ who was promised, so that his assent might have its effect.

(1 Cor. 9:21) To those who are under the Law, as if I were under the Law (though I myself am not under the Law), that I might gain those who are under the Law. Those who are under the Law are known to be the Samaritans; for they accept the Law alone, that is, the five books of Moses. They are of the stock of the Persians and Assyrians, whom the king of the Assyrians, when the children of Israel had been carried off into captivity, settled to inhabit the regions of Samaria (2 Kings 17:6); therefore they also worship fire after the manner of the Persians. To these, then, the Apostle, while he did not deny that the Law is from God, became as if under the Law: but from it, with a gentle word, he handed on to them how Christ was to be understood, just as that Samaritan woman too, who said to the Lord: I know that the Messiah comes (John 4:25), having been taught through the Law.

To those who are without the Law, as if I were without the Law (though I was not without the law of God, but was in the law of Christ), that I might gain those who are without the Law. He says this because, when he is in the law of Christ, he is in the law of God; for what is Christ's is God's, and what is God's is Christ's, as he says: All things that are mine are yours, and yours are mine. That I might gain those who are without the Law (John 17:10). For while he agrees with them, that is, with the Gentiles, in accordance with natural reasoning, that the world and the things that are in it were made by God, and that the soul is immortal, and that we have our origin from him (for so he says in the Acts of the Apostles (Acts 17:28): And as some of you have said: For we are also his offspring), in this way he became as if without law, and by this means he brought in to them that Christ is the very one whom God chose beforehand to judge the world, and that he is the very one through whom he made all things.

(1 Cor. 9:22) To the weak I became weak, that I might gain the weak. He became weak when for the sake of weak brethren he abstains from lawful things, lest he put a stumbling block before them. I became all things to all, that I might save all. This was the mark of a prudent and spiritual man, that, having become all things to all, he nevertheless did not go beyond the purpose of religion; for where he gave way, he gave way to this end, that he might make progress: nor did he do anything other than what the Law commanded.

(1 Cor. 9:23) But I do all things for the sake of the Gospel, that I may become a partaker of it. That is, I do all the things mentioned above so that I may be made a partaker of the Gospel's will, which it has concerning human salvation.

(1 Cor. 9:24) Do you not know that those who run in the stadium all run indeed, but one receives the palm? Now he wishes to set forth something else: and so by this example he shows how great is the advantage of our law, in which the palm is promised not to one, but to all. So run that you may lay hold of it. He wishes the race toward the promise to be run with care, because it is great.

(1 Cor. 9:25) And everyone who strives in the contest abstains from all things: from all those things which that same discipline teaches are to be avoided, although they know that only one is to be crowned; how much more must this be observed by us, since salvation is promised to all! And they indeed do it to receive a corruptible crown, but we an incorruptible one. In this too the gift is very unequal, and for this reason we ought to be the more diligent, for whom a spiritual crown is kept, set not with earthly and withering flowers but with eternal gems, in the manner of a royal diadem.

(1 Cor. 9:26) So I run, not as into uncertainty. He professes that he runs with great hope, holding as certain what has been promised. So I fight, not as one beating the air. That is, I contend not with words so much as about realities.

But I chastise my body and subject it to servitude. To chastise the body is to distress it with fasts, and to give it those things which serve for life, not for luxury. And it is subjected to servitude when it carries out not its own will but that of the spirit. Lest perhaps, while preaching to others, I myself be found reprobate. He shows that he restrains his body for this reason, that he himself also may be held worthy of the reward which he preaches to others, lest, having cured others, he himself should remain in his wound. So he affirms that the hope of his preaching is sure, when he shows that he pursues in his deeds what he teaches; for one who acts otherwise than he preaches makes his hearers suspicious, so that they doubt about the promises. And so by his own example he exhorts that it is to be practiced not by words so much as by works.

Chapter X

(1 Cor. 10:1–2) For I do not want you to be ignorant, brothers, that our fathers were all under the cloud, and all passed through the sea, and all were baptized in Moses in the cloud and in the sea (Exod. 13:21; 14:22). By the example of the Jews, who gave offense through their own negligence (Num. 9:22), he wants us to be careful; and therefore he says that they were under the cloud, because all the things that were done were done as a figure of us, so that they had the image of our truth. For, covered by the cloud and kept safe from their adversaries, they are said to have been baptized while they were freed from death. For when those, that is, the Egyptians, had died in the sea, while these, with Moses as their leader, crossed over happily, they were snatched from death: which is what baptism provides. For their past evils were not imputed to them, but, purified through the sea and through the cloud, they were prepared to receive the Law, and the form of our future mystery.

(1 Cor. 10:3–4) And all ate the same spiritual food, and all drank the same spiritual drink; for they drank from the spiritual rock that followed them: and the rock was Christ. The manna and the water (Exod. 16:15; 17:6) which flowed from the rock he calls spiritual, because they were not prepared by the law of the world, but were created for the occasion by the power of God without any mixture of the elements, having in themselves the figure of the future mystery which we now take in commemoration of Christ the Lord. Therefore it was also called the bread of angels, because it was created by the power by which the angels subsist: signifying this, that he who would feed them spiritually was to come from heaven. Hence also the manna first came from heaven on the Lord's day, to the satisfying of the people; the rock, however, is called "following", and it is understood to be Christ. For where human help failed them, Christ was present; therefore he followed, so that where it had failed, he might come to their aid. For it was not the rock that gave the water, but Christ.

(1 Cor. 10:5) But God did not think well of most of them. He said this because they did not understand the benefit they had received. For they were laid low in the desert (Num. 26:65). It is fitting that one who, having obtained great things, is distrustful in small ones, should not only not obtain these, but should also lose those. Since, then, he exhorted them above to better things, he says that he himself also labors at this, that he may come worthy to the promise; now, in order to bind them to this and persuade them by the terror of an example, he says that the Jews at that time in like manner received the gift and grace of God as their lot, just as we too have: and because they were careless and doubtful about God's promise, they were laid low in the desert; so that he might show that he afflicts himself for this reason, lest he be found in their number: and accordingly they must labor to become his imitators.

(1 Cor. 10:6–7) Now these things were done as a figure of us, that we may not be desirers of evil things, as they also desired. This is the sense and reasoning which I mentioned above. Nor serving images, as some of them did. Now he touches those who, reclining in the idol-temple, thought that it was not a sin. As it is written (Exod. 32:6): The people sat down to eat and drink, and rose up to play: before the idol, of course; so that those who feasted in the idol-temple should not believe themselves free from guilt. For while Moses was placed on the mountain with God, when the image of the calf had been made, after the offering, for the sake of the dedication: The people sat down to eat and drink, and rose up to play. This is self-indulgence, because, ever taking their pleasure, they were found to be unbelievers in the things of God. And therefore he instructs us not to fall into this distrust through consent to self-indulgence in the idol-temple.

(1 Cor. 10:8) Nor let us commit fornication, as some of them committed fornication, and there fell in one day twenty-three thousand men (Num. 25:1). This was paid back to them when they defiled themselves with the Midianite women; for the wrath of God arose against them, and it was quieted by the zealous ardor of Phinehas the priest, who had the zeal of God, so that no more should die.

(1 Cor. 10:9) Nor let us tempt Christ, as some of them tempted, and perished by serpents. Those who disparaged God are said to have tempted Christ, because it was Christ who spoke to Moses. Finally, lest we be found in the same case, he warns us not to be handed over to the devil, whose figure the serpents bear.

(1 Cor. 10:10) Nor murmur, as some of them murmured (Num. 14:1). To murmur is to complain falsely to one another about superiors and rulers, as far as pertains to the meaning of the passage; for murmuring also commonly arises from a just cause. And they perished by the destroyer. That is, they passed into the place of Judas, anticipating him; for, because he betrayed Christ, he was cast out of the number of the apostles by God the judge. Through these examples he calls us to a more corrected life; so that by his warnings we may merit either a reward, if we are obedient, or a heavier punishment if we are disobedient. For there is no doubt that those sinners are to be subjected to torments more, to whom the punishments of sinners were recounted precisely for this, that they should not sin.

(1 Cor. 10:11) Now all these things happened to them in figure; but they were written for our correction, upon whom the ends of the ages have come. We are the ends of the ages; for these things are known to have been done then concerning their requital for this reason, that they might be an example to us, who were to be in the faith of Christ in the last age. For they were done for our benefit, so that if, through the terror of these things, we remain in the fear of God, we may receive the glory promised to them: if not, their punishment will be doubled for us; because greater knowledge of the law makes one more guilty.

(1 Cor. 10:12) Therefore let him also who thinks he stands take heed lest he fall. He says this to those who, presuming on the knowledge by which it is lawful to eat all things, were a stumbling-block to weak brothers: and thinking that they were making some progress through the teaching of the false apostles, they had grown worse; and they judged the Apostle, when they themselves were guilty. He therefore cuts off pride, lest through it they be tempted, as the Jews were tempted, and perished.

(1 Cor. 10:13) Let no temptation seize you except a human one. And by this, in disparaging the Apostle, they are seen to tempt the Lord; because then too, when they disparaged Moses and doubted about him, they are said to have tempted God. For everything that is not held as proven is put to the test; therefore, so that this temptation may be torn out, he admonishes them with terror. But he exhorts that a human temptation should seize them: for human temptation is, for the sake of hope in God, to distrust man, because hope in man is vain; so that in need or distress one should not despair of God by seeking human help, as also the Jews did, who, doubting about God, implored the aid of idols. This is human help; because the gods of the pagans are earth, and human error invented their worship. To suffer for Christ, then, is a human temptation, through which one makes progress with God.

But God is faithful, who will not allow you to be tempted beyond what you are able: but will make with the temptation also an outcome, that you may be able to bear it. He calls God faithful, who will not allow you to be tempted beyond what you are able, for this reason: because God has promised that he will give the heavenly kingdoms to those who love him: and it is necessary that he give them, because he is faithful. For this reason, then, he will be present to those who are afflicted for his sake, and will not allow so much to be inflicted on them that they cannot bear it: but he will bring it about that either the temptation quickly ceases, or, if it is prolonged, he gives the strength to bear it: otherwise he will not give what he promised, because the one who suffers will be overcome. For man is subject to weakness, and there will be no one to receive it: but because God, who promised, is faithful, he comes to their aid, that he may fulfill what he promised. Finally, the Lord wills the days to be shortened, so that the elect may be saved and may be able to attain the kingdom of God (Matt. 24:22). How, however, is one helped by God, if he is allowed to be tempted only as much as he can bear? He is plainly helped, when no more is allowed to be laid on him than he is known to be able to bear; so that one who is known to be unable to endure beyond three days is not allowed to suffer on the fourth day.

(1 Cor. 10:14) Wherefore, beloved, flee from the worship of idols. He exhorts them even now to abstain from all fellowship with idolatry, so that not only hope but also the mind may be taken away from it, lest through this a temptation against God should arise. For no one who is assiduous in the idol-temple hopes for nothing from it; for to hope from that quarter is to doubt about God, just as Saul too, having abandoned God, who was angry with him because of his offense, betook himself to idolatry, hoping for something from it (1 Sam. 28:12 ff.). This both harmed him in the present, and provided for him an avenging Gehenna in the future.

(1 Cor. 10:15) As wise men, you, judge what I say. He adds examples so as to persuade more easily; for examples usually persuade the one whom words do not satisfy.

(1 Cor. 10:16–17) The cup of blessing which we bless, is it not the communion of the blood of the Lord? The bread which we break, is it not the communion of the body of the Lord? Because we, being many, are one bread, one body; for we all partake of the one bread and of the one cup. Since we are one, members one of another, he says that we ought to be of one mind; so that one faith may have one mind and one work.

(1 Cor. 10:18) See Israel according to the flesh: that is, learn, you men who see God, how carnal things stand, that is, idolatry. Are not those who eat the sacrifices partakers of the altar? For just as we, receiving of the one bread and of the one cup, are partakers and sharers of the body of the Lord, so also those who eat the sacrifices are partakers of the altar of error.

(1 Cor. 10:19–20) What then do I say, that what is sacrificed to an image is anything? Not that the image is anything; but what they sacrifice, they sacrifice to demons. An image is truly nothing, because it is seen to be the likeness of a dead thing: but under the covering of images the devil is worshipped.

I do not want you to become partakers of demons. He shows them that it is not only what is seen in the idol-temple: but that there is a hidden mystery of iniquity, which Satan devised in order to corrupt faith in the one God. Hence also John the apostle says in his epistle: For this the Son of God came, that he might undo the works of the devil (1 John 3:8).

(1 Cor. 10:21) You cannot drink the cup of the Lord and the cup of demons; you cannot share in the table of the Lord and the table of demons. As the Lord also says: You cannot serve God and mammon (Matt. 6:24): because he who drinks the cup of demons insults the cup of the Lord: and when he shares in the table of demons, he raises an outcry against the table of the Lord, that is, the altar, and crucifies the body of the Lord. For Christ was crucified for this reason, that he might destroy the works of the devil. He, then, who does the works of the devil fights against Christ.

(1 Cor. 10:22) Or do we rival the Lord Jesus? Are we stronger than he? It hardly happens that a lowly man is jealous of a powerful one; for he knows that the thing for which the other can be envied does not belong to himself: but one envies either an equal or a near-equal. Therefore the Apostle said that men cannot rival the Lord Jesus, because they know him to be the Lord: otherwise, he of whom they are jealous, lest he be Lord, will not seem to them worthy to be called Lord. For it was out of jealousy that Satan invented idolatry, so that men, by denying that God is Lord, might serve idols as gods and lords. All things are lawful for me, but not all things are expedient. He says that all things are lawful according to the law of nature; for all things are clean. For the question concerned foods, and meat sacrificed to idols. For how can things that are prohibited and forbidden be said to be lawful, when here he indicates that even the very things which are permitted are sometimes not expedient? as the apostle Peter says: If this is the case of a man with his wife, it is not expedient to marry (Matt. 19:10).

(1 Cor. 10:23) All things are lawful for me, but not all things edify. The sense is the same, because it is lawful to eat all things: but what is sacrificed to idols does not edify, because it causes a stumbling-block to a brother, as was said above: and therefore one must sometimes refrain from lawful things, so as to be of benefit. Hence it is that he was unwilling to accept support from them, although it was lawful for him, knowing that it was not expedient.

(1 Cor. 10:24) Let no one seek what is his own, but what is another's. It is true that, while each one reclines in the idol-temple, he satisfies his own will, and puts a stumbling-block before a brother weak in conscience. Wherefore we must make haste for the love of our Lord Jesus Christ; so that we may rather safeguard the things that are necessary to our neighbor's salvation than our own will, as the same Apostle, the vessel of election and the spiritual physician, says: Not seeking what is useful to me, but what is useful to many.

(1 Cor. 10:25–26) Eat everything that is sold in the market, asking nothing for conscience' sake; for the earth is the Lord's and its fullness. To show that all things are clean, he strengthened this by the example of the twenty-third psalm; because what is the Lord's cannot be unclean. And since above he said about foods: All things are lawful, he immediately followed it up: Eat everything that is sold in the market, asking nothing for conscience' sake. He added this so that the conscience may be free; for although something may be polluted by an accident, that is, by its offering to an idol, yet when the one who buys it does not know this, he suffers no scruple, and is guiltless before God.

(1 Cor. 10:27) If any of the unbelievers invites you to dinner, and you go; eat everything that is set before you, inquiring into nothing. That is, eat simply what is set before you, not asking where it comes from.

(1 Cor. 10:28–29) But if anyone says: This has been sacrificed to idols, do not eat, for the sake of him who pointed it out, and for conscience' sake. Now I say conscience, not your own, but the other's. He says this to the perfect, who, despising idolatry, are able to eat of the sacrifice, because it is nothing, being certain that what is eaten under the name of God the Creator cannot pollute: but because another, who serves idols, will boast when you eat of the things sacrificed, as though you venerated the idols; therefore it ought not to be eaten. For that man will rejoice in his own conscience, because he sees you eagerly seeking what has been sacrificed to idols.

For why is my liberty judged by another conscience? He says this because, since his conscience is free from devotion to the idol, what need is there that he be thought to eat things sacrificed to idols for the sake of veneration? For he is judged not to differ from one who worships idols, when he does not shrink from what has been offered to an image.

(1 Cor. 10:30) If then I am a partaker of grace, why am I blasphemed for that for which I give thanks? That is, if I share in the grace of God, because I eat in his name: what need is there for anyone to think this of me, that I am devoted to an idol, while I do not shrink from the things that have been offered to an idol, that is, am I blasphemed? So he both blasphemes me, and that man rejoices in his idol-temple; while he thinks me a partaker, he has occasion to remain in error, and a bad example is given to the brothers. For if it is commanded in the Law that the groves and the altars and the pillars and the idols themselves be broken, see whether it is not a sin not only not to do these things, but even to take part in feasts of this kind; therefore it is not expedient to eat of things sacrificed.

(1 Cor. 10:31) Whether therefore you eat, or drink, or do anything, all things to the glory of God. By these words added he has declared that to eat in the idol-temple, with whatever conscience it may be, is to be an enemy of God; for these things are dedicated to the devil to the reproach of the one God. For to eat and drink and do anything to the glory of God is this: that a banquet be celebrated with moderation under the invocation of the Creator; for something is done to the glory of God when God is praised in the actions and conduct of a Christian, or when the procreation of children is hoped for from God.

(1 Cor. 10:32) Be without offense to Jews and Greeks, and to the Church of God. He teaches that our conduct must be so tempered that no one suffers a stumbling-block in it. Offense, then, is given to the Jews when they see that a Christian, who says that he accepts the Law and the prophets, does not shrink from idols, which they abominate. But to the Greeks, that is, the Gentiles, this is the offense, if in the state in which they are, they are not only not reproved, but even become more ready, while the votive offerings of those idols of theirs are not avoided. And a stumbling-block is made for the Church of God when she sees some of her own number cleaving to the things that are hostile to God.

(1 Cor. 10:33) Just as I please all in all things, not seeking what is useful to me, but what is useful to many, that they may be saved. To please all in all things is to act without being a stumbling-block to anyone, for the advancement of their benefit, so that it may be useful both to them and to him.

Chapter XI

(1 Cor. 11:1) Be imitators of me, as I also am of Christ. What he says is a human thing: that we should be imitators of him who has been granted to us by God as a teacher. For if he is an imitator of God, why should we not be imitators of a man? Because just as God the Father sent Christ as the teacher and author of life, so also Christ sent the apostles to us as teachers, so that we might be their imitators, since we cannot be his. For so the Lord says to the Father: As you sent me into this world, so I also have sent them into this world (John 17:18). And because they were worthy imitators of him, he added: And for them I will sanctify myself (ibid., 19).

(1 Cor. 11:2) But I praise you, brothers, that you keep all my things in memory, and that, as I hand them on everywhere, you hold my traditions. After rebuking their morals and way of life, he now wishes to correct their traditions. So he is not affirming this, but is angry with them; because, though he was their apostle, they were unmindful of his traditions; nor did they follow, from the tradition of other churches, what they had not yet learned; and for this reason he hands it on to them as if anew, saying:

(1 Cor. 11:3) I want you to know that the head of every man is Christ. He has referred it to authorship; because man is indeed from God, but through Christ. But the head of the woman is the man. Because although the woman too is through Christ, she was nevertheless made from the man, and for this reason she is subject to the man. But the head of Christ is God. It is fitting that the Father be called the head of the Son, as being his begetter. Yet Christ is the head of the man in one way, and the man of the woman in another, and God is the head of Christ in another. Now God is the head of Christ for this reason, that he was begotten of him, or by him. But the man is the head of the woman for this reason, that she was formed from his rib by the power of God (Gen. 2:21). And Christ is the head of the man for this reason, that when the man did not exist, he was created through him. So one word has a different meaning, because both the persons and the substances differ.

(1 Cor. 11:4) Every man praying or prophesying with veiled head disgraces his head. By "praying" he means making supplication; and by "prophesying", declaring after the prayer, in the words of the creed, that the coming of the Lord is to be.

(1 Cor. 11:5–7) But every woman praying or prophesying with unveiled head puts her head to shame; for it is one and the same thing as if she were shaved bald. But if a woman is not veiled, let her be shorn; but if it is shameful for a woman to be shorn or shaved bald, let her veil her head. For a man ought not to veil his head, since he is the image and glory of God. Although man and woman are of one substance, yet because the man is the head of the woman, he is declared to be set before her; so that he is greater by cause and reason, not by substance. The woman, then, is inferior to the man, for she is a portion of him; because the man is the origin of the woman: for she is from him, and for this reason the woman is seen to be bound to the man, so that she is subject to his authority. He says that the honor and dignity of the man stand in the way of his veiling his head; for it is unfitting that the image of God be concealed: for it ought not to be hidden, for the glory of God is seen in the man.

But the woman is the glory of the man. There is a great difference between the glory of God and the glory of the man; for the man was made in the image of God, not the woman. Now this image of God is in the man because the one God made one man; so that, just as all things are from the one God, so all men too might be from one man; so that one visible man might have the image of the one invisible God on earth, so that the one God might be seen, in one man, to preserve the authority of one principle, to the confusion of the devil, who, disregarding the one God, wished to usurp lordship and divinity for himself. This the prophet Ezekiel signifies (Ezek. 28:2), and the Apostle likewise, for he says: So that he sits in the temple of God, showing himself as if he were God (2 Thess. 2:4).

(1 Cor. 11:8–10) For the man is not from the woman, but the woman from the man: for indeed the man was not created for the sake of the woman, but the woman for the sake of the man. Therefore the woman ought to have power over her head, also because of the angels. By "power" he meant the veil; by "angels" he means bishops, as is taught in the Apocalypse of John (Rev. 2:1 ff.). And because they are, of course, men, they are rebuked for not correcting the people, and what is right in them is praised. The woman, therefore (Gratian, Decretum C. 33, q. 5, c. Mulier), ought for this reason to veil her head: because she is not the image of God, but so that she may be shown to be subject. And because transgression began through her, she ought to have this sign, so that in church, out of reverence for the bishop, she may not have her head free, but covered with a veil; nor may she have the power of speaking, because the bishop bears the person of Christ. As before a judge, then, so before the bishop, since he is the vicar of the Lord, she ought to be seen to be subject, on account of the origin of guilt.

(1 Cor. 11:11) Nevertheless neither is the woman without the man, nor the man without the woman, in the Lord. In the Lord for this reason: because God made both from one, and made them one; for they are one flesh and one body in the Lord, that is, according to the Lord, who created them. Finally, those who do not believe that the woman was taken from the man believe in an origin of human beings without the Lord, or not according to the Lord.

(1 Cor. 11:12) For just as the woman is from the man, so also the man is through the woman. He has confirmed what he said, because both are one in nature, and the man is the origin of the woman, as we read in Genesis that it was done by the Lord the Creator. But all things are from God (Gen. 2:22). After setting out each point step by step, in order to subject all things to God, keeping one principle, he says: But all things are from God; so that neither should the woman be saddened because of her subjection, nor the man be proud as if exalted.

(1 Cor. 11:13) Judge for yourselves: is it fitting for a woman to pray to God unveiled? Hence it is that above he is angry with them by irony, saying: I praise that you keep all my things in memory: and that, as I hand them on everywhere, you hold my traditions (above, 1 Cor. 11:2). For since the rationale of the tradition was this, that women should be veiled in church, they on the contrary allowed them to be unveiled in church. And therefore he now strives to persuade them of the truth no longer by the authority of tradition, which they had neglected, but through nature itself, saying:

(1 Cor. 11:14–15) Does not nature itself teach you that if a man has long hair, it is a disgrace to him? This he said according to the Law; for it forbids a man to wear long hair. But if a woman has long hair, it is a glory to her; since her hair has been given to her for a veil. He means that it is naturally honorable, and almost decreed, that a woman should make satisfaction by being veiled, and give thanks to God. Her hair, then, is a sign of the veil, so that will may be added to nature. Finally, no woman, even an unveiled one, approaches authority.

(1 Cor. 11:16) But if anyone seems to be contentious, we have no such custom, nor has the Church of God. Having given the reason, he interposes authority in order to overcome the contentious; because not even Judaism had this, whence he says: Neither we, nor the Church of God, meaning that neither Moses nor the Savior handed it down so.

(1 Cor. 11:17) But this I command, not praising that you come together not for the better but for the worse. Just as he amends their way of life point by point by rebuke, so too he amends their traditions, not praising them, of course, but censuring them.

(1 Cor. 11:18–19) For first. By saying first, he shows the kind of sin from which arose the errors that he rebukes; for where there is dissension, nothing is right. When you come together into the church, I hear that there are dissensions, and I partly believe it. For there must also be heresies, so that those also who are approved may become manifest among you. Knowing that some were corrupted in mind by the cunning of the devil, he says: There must be heresies. He did not, of course, will it or wish it; but because he knew it would come, he said so, just as the Lord also: It must be, he says, that scandals come (Matt. 18:7). And: The Son of man must suffer (Luke 9:22), foreknowing that Judas would be the betrayer. That the approved, moreover, may become manifest among you: when he says this, he signifies those who, persevering in the tradition they had received, were an example of the proof of the gospel discipline, to the condemnation of the rest. These are those whom he signifies at the head of the letter, in that they said: But we are Christ's; while others said: I am Paul's, I am Apollos' (1 Cor. 1:12).

(1 Cor. 11:20–22) When you come together, therefore, into one, it is no longer to eat the Lord's supper. For each one of you takes his own supper beforehand in eating: and one is hungry, and another is drunk. Do you not have houses for eating and drinking? Or do you despise the Church of God, and put to shame those who have not? He marks out those who came together into the church in such a way that, offering their gifts, when the presbyters arrived (because rulers had not yet been established for the churches in all places), those who had offered claimed the whole for themselves, for the sake of schism. For the false apostles had sown dissensions among them, so that they were jealous over their own offerings, although the offerings of all were blessed with one and the same prayer; so that those who, as usually happens, had not offered, or did not have the means to offer, were overcome with shame and confounded, taking no share; and they did this so quickly that those who came later did not find anything to eat. And therefore, he says, if you come together in such a way that each one takes his own, these things should be done at home, not in church, where people come together for the sake of unity and of the mystery, not of dissension and the belly. For the gift offered should belong to the whole people, because in one bread all are signified. For by the fact that we are one, we must all take from one bread.

What shall I say to you? Shall I praise you? In this I do not praise. It is plain: having detected and rebuked the error, so that they may correct it for the future, and know that this is true which they themselves had learned long since, at the beginning, he repeats the form of that which was given by the Savior in a matter of this kind, saying:

(1 Cor. 11:23–25) For I received from the Lord what I also handed on to you, that the Lord Jesus, on the night in which he was being betrayed, took bread, and giving thanks, broke it, and said: Take, and eat. This is my body, which is broken for you: do this in remembrance of me. In like manner also the cup, after supper, saying: This cup is the new testament in my blood; do this, as often as you shall drink it, in remembrance of me. He shows them that the mystery of the eucharist, celebrated during supper, is not a supper; for it is a spiritual medicine, which, tasted with reverence, purifies the one devoted to it. For it is the memorial of our redemption, so that, mindful of the Redeemer, we may deserve to obtain greater things from him.

(1 Cor. 11:26) For as often as you eat this bread and drink of the cup, you will proclaim the death of the Lord, until he comes. For because we have been set free by the death of the Lord, being mindful of this, in eating and drinking the flesh and blood which were offered for us, we signify that we have obtained in these the new testament, which is the new law, which delivers the one who obeys it to the heavenly kingdoms. For Moses too, having taken the blood of a calf in a bowl, sprinkled the children of Israel, saying: This is the testament which God has appointed for you (Exod. 24:8). This was a figure of the testament which the Lord called new through the prophets, so that that one which Moses handed down might be the old. The testament, then, was established with blood, because blood is the witness of the divine benefit. As a type of this we receive the mystical cup of blood for the protection of our body and soul; because the blood of the Lord has redeemed our blood, that is, has saved the whole man. For the flesh of the Savior is for the salvation of the body, but the blood was poured out for our soul, just as had been prefigured earlier by Moses; for so he says: The flesh is offered for your body, but the blood for the soul (Lev. 17:11); and therefore blood is not to be eaten. If, then, among the ancients there was an image of the truth which has now appeared and been made manifest in the Savior's coming, how does the old seem contrary to the new to the heretics, when they bear witness to each other?

(1 Cor. 11:27) Therefore whoever eats this bread or drinks the cup of the Lord unworthily of the Lord will be guilty of the body and blood of the Lord. He says that he is unworthy of the Lord who celebrates the mystery otherwise than it was handed down by him. For he cannot be devout who takes it otherwise than it was given by its author. And therefore he warns beforehand that the mind of one approaching the eucharist of the Lord should be devout according to the order handed down; since there is to be a judgment, so that each one, according to the manner in which he approaches, may render account in the day of the Lord Jesus Christ; because those who approach without the discipline of tradition and of way of life are guilty of the body and blood of the Lord. And what is it to be guilty, if not to pay the penalty for the Lord's death? For he was slain for those who make his benefit void.

(1 Cor. 11:28–29) But let a man prove himself, and so let him eat of that bread and drink of the cup. For he who eats and drinks unworthily eats and drinks judgment to himself, not discerning the body of the Lord. He teaches that one must approach communion with a devout spirit and with fear, so that the mind may know that it owes reverence to him whose body it approaches to receive. For it ought to judge this within itself: that it is the Lord whose blood it drinks in the mystery, the blood which is the witness of God's benefit. If we receive it with discipline, we shall not be unworthy of the body and blood of the Lord; for we shall be seen to render thanks to the Redeemer.

(1 Cor. 11:30) Therefore many among you are weak and sick. And many sleep. In order to prove it true that there is to be an examination of those who receive the body of the Lord, he shows already here an image of the judgment upon those who had received the body of the Lord inconsiderately, in that they were seized with fevers and infirmities, and many were dying: so that the rest might learn from them, and, terrified by the example of a few, be amended, knowing that it does not go unpunished to receive the body of the Lord negligently; and that he whose punishment has been deferred here will be dealt with more severely, because he has despised the example.

(1 Cor. 11:31–32) But if we discerned ourselves, we would surely not be judged. But while we are judged, we are corrected by the Lord, so that we may not be condemned with this world. He says this because if we ourselves corrected our errors, we would not be judged by the Lord. And our being corrected is nevertheless for our sake: so that we may be amended by fear itself. For in a few lies the amendment of all. Lest with this world, that is, with the unbelievers, we be condemned. For he who approaches the table of the Lord inconsiderately differs in nothing from an unbeliever.

(1 Cor. 11:33–34) Therefore, brothers, when you come together to eat, wait for one another. If anyone is hungry, let him eat at home. He says to wait for one another so that the offering of many may be celebrated together, and that all may be ministered to: and if anyone is impatient, let him feed on earthly bread at home. So that you may not come together unto judgment: that is, lest you perform the mystery reprehensibly and with offense.

The rest I will set in order when I come. He shows that the chief matter of salvation was to be dealt with first in order, and he has set forth beforehand how both sexes are to come together in church: in which matters, if there is error, it is no light sin. But the rest, which pertains to the edification of the Church, he promised to set in order by his presence.

Chapter XII

(1 Cor. 12:1–2) Now concerning spiritual things I do not want you to be ignorant, brethren. You know that you were Gentiles, going in the form of images, as you were led. Being about to deliver spiritual things to them, he recalls the example of their former manner of life; so that, just as they were the form of the images, worshipping idols, and were led with the will of the demons as their leader, so also, now worshipping God, they may be the form of the Lord's law, walking as pleases the Lord. For the form of each law ought to be seen in the profession and manner of life of its worshipper; for he is the form and image of the law of God in whose faith and manner of life the truth of the Gospel shines.

(1 Cor. 12:3) Therefore I make known to you that no one speaking in the Spirit of God says anathema to Jesus; and no one can say Lord Jesus except in the Holy Spirit. Since, being ignorant of the reason of spiritual things, they gave the glory for the individual gifts to men rather than to God, not having grasped that this gift is ministered through the Holy Spirit; and because he who calls Jesus Lord does not say this without the Holy Spirit (for in that very thing he has the grace of his faith on account of the glory of the name of Christ; for it is not without a gift of God that one says Jesus is Lord): by this he shows them that in all things the praise and the grace are God's. For just as the image of idols is in its ministers, having its order through the individual ranks, and yet the whole is man's; so also in the Lord's law there are ranks of gifts for the offices of the Church, granted certainly not to human merits, but as members pertaining to the edification of the Church, which have glory through themselves and in themselves, as is also the case in human offices. For there are colleges which give dignity to those placed in them, so that it is the honor of the place that makes a man glorious, not his own praise. He says, then: No one speaking in the Spirit of God says anathema to Jesus. For the utterance which says anathema to Jesus is brought forth by human error; for whatever is false is from man. And no one can say Lord Jesus except in the Holy Spirit. For the very saying by which Jesus is signified as Lord has been poured forth not from the flattery of men, as idols too are called gods, but from the truth of the Holy Spirit; for whatever true thing is said by anyone is said by the Holy Spirit. So that they should not, then, suppose that there is human favor in the Christian rule, and on that account should by no means suffer themselves to be reproved, as is the case with images too (for man contrived that he should be called God who is not; and for this reason the priests are subject to them), he shows them that there is no human benefit in it when Jesus is called Lord, but rather a gift of God, who deigned to declare his mystery to men. For indeed the profession itself acquires the remission of sins, just as the dedication of idols claims to do. He teaches them, then, that in saying Lord Jesus they do not confer a benefit on religion, but receive one; lest, after the manner of idols, they should think that the favor in the law of the Lord is men's, as when one is called God who is not. Finally, not understanding that the gift of God lies in faith, they had each chosen for themselves individual men to follow, saying: I am of Paul, but I of Apollos (1 Cor. 1:12). He therefore humbles their pride, so that they may suffer themselves, as I said, to be reproved.

(1 Cor. 12:4) Now there are divisions of graces. He does not want this to be ascribed to human merits, but to the grace of God, for the honor of his name; for just as he who says Lord Jesus says it in the Holy Spirit, whatever sort of man he may be, so also he who is placed in the position of the order of ecclesiastical office has grace, whatever sort of man he may be: not indeed his own, but that of the order, through the efficacy of the Holy Spirit. Hence at the beginning too he says: Neither is he who plants anything, nor he who waters, but God who gives the increase (1 Cor. 3:7).

(1 Cor. 12:5–6) But the same Spirit. And there are divisions of ministries. He says that different gifts are bestowed through the same Spirit. But the same Lord. And there are divisions of operations. Now he joins Christ to the Holy Spirit. But the same God, who works all things in all. So far is he from granting this to men, as though it were their own, that he claims it for God alone; so that he calls even the gift of the Holy Spirit and the grace of the Lord Jesus the operation of the one God; lest the grace and the gift be divided on account of the persons of the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit, but that the one work of the three, of undivided unity and nature, may be understood, so that he may bring back all glory and divinity to one. Now there are divisions of graces assigned to the offices of the Church, not to human merits. For if the Holy Spirit is the same Lord, and the Lord is the same God, the three too are one God; for since the glory and the power and the nature of the Holy Spirit is God's, and the Lord Jesus is the same in nature as God is, then surely the Holy Spirit, and the Lord Jesus, and the Father are one God. For each singly is one God, and the three are one God. Finally, when one works, the three are said to work, so that the mystery of the Trinity may be enclosed in the nature and power of one God, though it is immeasurable.

(1 Cor. 12:7) But to each one is given the manifestation of the Spirit for profit. That is, he receives the gift so that, governing his life by divine reins, he may be useful both to himself and to others, while he shows an example of a good manner of life.

(1 Cor. 12:8) For to one is given through the Spirit the word of wisdom. That is, prudence is given him, not from letters, but by the flashing fire of the Holy Spirit, so that he may have an enlightened heart and be prudent, so as to discern what is to be avoided and what is to be followed. But to another the word of knowledge according to the same spirit: that is, that he may have knowledge of divine things. To another the grace of healings in the one Spirit; that is, that he may bestow a remedy on the infirm or the sick.

(1 Cor. 12:9–10) To another faith in the same Spirit. He says this: that, shame being suppressed, he receives the capacity for professing and defending the faith. To another the working of powers. He means that power is given for casting out demons or for doing signs. To another prophecy: that is, that, filled with the Holy Spirit, he may tell things to come. To another the discerning of spirits. He says this: that he may understand and judge whether what is said is of the Holy Spirit or of a worldly spirit. To another kinds of tongues, to another the interpretation of words. To interpret is this: that through the gift of God he faithfully interprets the sayings of those who speak in tongues or in letters.

(1 Cor. 12:11) But all these things one and the same Spirit works, dividing to each one as he wills. What he said above of the three persons, he now professes to be done through the one Holy Spirit; so that, because they are of one nature and power, what one works, the three work: yet there is one God, whose grace is divided to individuals as he wills, not according to the merits of men, but rather for the edification of his Church; so that all the things which the world wants to imitate but does not fulfill, because it is carnal, may be seen here in the Church, which is the house of God, granted to the offices of individuals both by the gift and by the teaching of the Holy Spirit, for the proof of the truth, in those who are contemptible to the world.

(1 Cor. 12:12–13) For just as the body is one and has many members, and all the members of the one body, though they are many, are one body, so also is Christ. For indeed in one Spirit we have all been baptized into one body, whether Jews or Greeks, whether slaves or free; and we have all drunk one Spirit. By these words he teaches that no one's person is to be scorned as though despised, nor is anyone's to be preferred as though he were perfect; nor is the glory which is owed to God alone to be attributed to men, since in all one and the same God is glorious: inasmuch as we all have both one baptism and one and the same Holy Spirit. This is on account of the cause mentioned above, because they gloried in some, but spurned others as contemptible.

(1 Cor. 12:14) For the body too is not one member, but many. By saying this he shows that unity has varieties of offices, and that this diversity does not disagree in the unity of power; since the unity of the body consists not in singleness but in many members, so that they may render to one another what they owe.

(1 Cor. 12:15) If the foot should say: Because I am not the hand, I am not of the body; is it for that reason not of the body? That is, he who seems weak among the brethren cannot be denied to be of the body because he is not powerful.

(1 Cor. 12:16) If the ear should say: Because I am not the eye, I am not of the body; is it for that reason not of the body? He says that one who is a little lower ought not to be thought unnecessary to the body because he is not among the first.

(1 Cor. 12:17) If the whole body were the eye, where is the hearing? If the whole were hearing, where is the smelling? That is, if all were of one office and work, how would the rest of the body's need be fulfilled, since it is evident that many offices are needed for governing the body?

(1 Cor. 12:18) But now God has placed the members, each one in the body, as he willed. He says that the will of God, because it is provident and rational, has fitted the members to the body, so that nothing may be lacking to the body, but it may be perfect with many members.

(1 Cor. 12:19) But if all were one member, where would the body be? It is clear that if all had been of one dignity, they would not be called members, nor a body; and therefore they are governed together by the various offices of the members; for all could not be one member. And they are many for this reason, that they differ from one another in dignity.

(1 Cor. 12:20) But now there are indeed many members, but one body. He says this because the many members, since they need one another, do not disagree in the unity of nature, although they are diverse; because this diversity runs together into one, so that the usefulness of the body may be fulfilled, just as the things too of which the world itself consists, though they are diverse not only in their offices but also in their natures, nevertheless contribute to the perfection of the one world; and from all these arises a certain temperate blending in the fruits, which contribute to human usefulness.

(1 Cor. 12:21) But the eye cannot say to the hand: I do not need your work. That is, the greater cannot say to the lower: I have no need of you; because the eye indeed sees, but it is the hands that work. Or again the head to the feet: You are not necessary to me: that is, he who is greater in rank and dignity cannot be without him who is lowly; because there is something the lowly can do which the exalted cannot, just as iron can do what gold cannot; and for this reason the feet do honor to the head.

(1 Cor. 12:22) But much more those members of the body which seem to be weaker are more necessary. It is clear that, although someone may be exalted in dignity, yet if a subordinate is lacking who by his services makes him glorious, the dignity itself will be contemptible: for it is the office through which the dignity stands. It is as if an emperor lacked an army. However great the emperor may be, then, he nevertheless has need of an army; for it is a member of his body, having before it tribunes, counts and masters. Lower than all these are the soldiers, and they are more necessary, like the members of the body which, while they seem lower, are more useful; for without the eyes the hand works, and the foot, by walking, seeks food.

(1 Cor. 12:23) And those members of the body which we think to be less noble, on these we bestow more abundant honor. The sense is similar: that in those who are thought to be without dignity we find something to praise, as also in the meaner members we find something that pleases us more than what we find in the others. For of what honor are the hands worthy, when we hold what we want! or the feet, with which we go where we want! For this reason we too add honor to them, for example to the feet, which, because they are lowly and without dignity, we adorn with shoes. And our unseemly parts have more abundant honor. It is clear that our private parts, which seem shameful, while they avoid public view, cover themselves with seemliness, lest they should be repellent through irreverence. In like manner some of the brethren too, though they are unseemly in poverty and dress, are yet not without grace, through which they are members of our body: for they are accustomed to go about girded in a drab little garment, with bare feet. Since, then, they seem contemptible, they are the more in honor, because they are accustomed to lead a purer life. For what seems despised to men is usually judged beautiful by God.

(1 Cor. 12:24) But our seemly parts need nothing. It is plain that the head does not need, nor the face, nor the hands, that something be added to them by which they may be adorned: so also for the brethren in whom zeal for learning and seemliness of manner of life flourish, there is nothing that is added by us; for the honor due to them is rendered to them. But for the despised or lowly, exhortation is necessary, through which some honor may be added to them, so that they may become useful: otherwise, through that very contempt they will become more negligent about themselves, though it is in them that greater progress must be made.

(1 Cor. 12:25) But God has tempered the body, giving more abundant honor to that which lacked it, so that there may be no schism in the body, but the members may be solicitous for one another. He says that the human body has been so regulated by God that all its members are necessary, and for this reason are solicitous for one another, because one cannot exist without the other: and what is thought lower is more necessary; as has also been expounded, or argued, concerning the brethren, that no one ought to be despised as useless.

(1 Cor. 12:26) And if one member suffers anything, all the members suffer with it. This is not doubtful concerning the members of the body of flesh: because if the eye, or the foot, or the hand is seized by some sickness of infirmity, the whole body grieves with it: so he teaches us also to grieve with the brethren, if anything of this kind, or of need, has arisen. Or if one member is glorified, let all the members rejoice with it. It is clear that the head or the other members are glad if the feet have been well tended or are healthy. So we too ought to become cheerful if we see some brother to be a worshipper of God and increased in seemliness of morals, that is, sound in counsel.

(1 Cor. 12:27) But you are the body of Christ, and members of a member. He shows openly that he has treated our case through the reasoning of the carnal members; because we cannot all do the same things, but each of us has also had grace granted according to the quality of his faith.

(1 Cor. 12:28) And God has placed some in the Church, first indeed apostles. So he has placed the apostles as the head in the Church, who are the envoys of Christ, as the same Apostle says: For whom we perform an embassy (2 Cor. 5:20). These are the bishops, as the apostle Peter confirms, saying among other things concerning Judas: And let another take his bishopric (Acts 1:20).

Secondly prophets. Let us understand prophets in a twofold kind: both those who tell things to come and those who reveal the Scriptures; although the apostles too are prophets, because the first rank has all things subject to it. Finally, the most wicked Caiaphas prophesied because he was chief of the priests (John 11:51), certainly on account of his order, not of his own merit. Yet there were, in a special sense, prophets who were both interpreters of the Scriptures and tellers of things to come (Acts 21:11), as was Agabus, who prophesied to this Apostle the ruin and the chains that awaited him at Jerusalem, and foretold the famine which came about under Claudius (Acts 11:28). Therefore, although the apostle is the better, yet he sometimes needs the prophets. And because all things are from the one God the Father, he decreed that single bishops should preside over single churches.

Thirdly teachers. He calls those teachers who in the Church instructed the boys in letters and in retaining the readings, after the custom of the Synagogue; because their tradition has passed over to us. In the fourth place he says:

Then powers, then the grace of healings; for someone can not be a bishop and yet have in himself the gift of the power of healings. Helps, governings; that he may be watchful in understanding in divine things: yet in such a way that in some matters which he is not granted to fulfill, he takes from another what he does not have; because the whole cannot be granted to one. There are also governors, who by spiritual reins are an instruction to men. Kinds of tongues; that it be a gift of God to know many languages. Interpretation of words; that the grace of God may impart this to someone, that he have skill in interpreting tongues.

(1 Cor. 12:29) Are all apostles? It is true, because in the Church there is one bishop. Are all prophets? It is not doubtful that prophecy is not granted to all. Are all teachers? He is a teacher to whom it is granted to instruct others.

(1 Cor. 12:30) Are all powers? He can have power to whom God grants to cast out demons. Do all have the grace of healings? How can it come about that all have gifts of healings? Do all speak in tongues? Certainly not, except him who receives the gift in this matter. Do all interpret? He can interpret words to whom God grants it. These things are to be inserted into the sense stated above. For he has explained, giving the reason, that all have different graces, and that the whole is not granted to anyone, by the example of the members. For by the example of the body of flesh he suggests the spiritual body: and for this reason God is to be blessed in all things, and glory is to be had in his name, whose grace it is. This reasoning we find also in physical things: for though gold is better than silver, yet silver is more in use; and though bronze is necessary, yet there is more need of iron; for almost nothing is done without iron, though it is lower. And after this:

(1 Cor. 12:31) Be zealous for the better gifts. This he soon resolves in what follows. And still I show you a more excellent way. Step by step he advances them to more useful things, showing them that the grace of every gift mentioned above which is seen in men, whether of speaking, or of healing, or of prophesying, does not pertain to the merit of man, but to the honor of God. And therefore he now says that he is showing them a plainer way, by which one goes to heaven, a way which places merit with God. For because the things mentioned above do not always pertain to merit, the Savior says: Many will say to me on that day, that is, the day of judgment: Lord, Lord, did we not prophesy in your name, and cast out demons in your name, and do many works of power (Matt. 7:22)? And because this does not pertain to merit, but these are offices of the Church, for the confounding of the Gentiles and for bearing witness to the honor of God, the Lord says to them: Depart from me: I do not know you, workers of iniquity (ibid., 23). For, secure because the working of God was seen in them, they were negligent about themselves; for to the seventy-two disciples too, when they rejoiced because the demons had been subject to them, the Savior says: Do not rejoice in this, that the demons are subject to you; but rejoice in this, that your names are written in heaven (Luke 10:20). Why, unless because they were subject to the name of God, not to the merit of man? And why does it not happen so now, that men have the grace of God? At the beginning it had to happen, so that the foundations of the faith might receive firmness: but now there is no need, because people bring people to the faith, when their good works and simple preaching are seen.

Chapter XIII

(1 Cor. 13:1) If I speak with the tongues of men and of angels, but do not have love, I am one like sounding bronze, or a tinkling cymbal. Great indeed seems the grace of speaking in diverse tongues; but it is something more if one can know even the tongue of angels, that is, if one can have the motion of angels known spiritually. But that this is not to be ascribed to merit, but to the glory of God, he shows in what follows, saying that it is like sounding bronze, or a tinkling cymbal. Because, just as bronze resounds at the impulse of another, and a cymbal tinkles, so also this man who speaks in tongues has the effect and motion of the Holy Spirit, so that he can speak, as the Savior also says in another place: For it is not you who speak, but the Spirit of your Father who speaks in you (Matt. 10:20). For even the ass spoke with a human tongue to Balaam son of Beor, that he might learn the majesty of God (Num. 22:28); and infant children burst forth into the praise of God, to the confusion of the Jews (Matt. 21:16). And the Savior showed that not only these but even the stones could cry out, for the condemnation of the faithless and the glory of God (Luke 19:40). And at the very beginnings, for the commendation of the faith, those who were baptized spoke in tongues (Acts 10:46).

(1 Cor. 13:2) And if I have prophecy, and know all mysteries, and all knowledge, but do not have love, it profits me nothing. Truly it profits nothing; for one prophesies for the glory of God, as David the prophet says: Not to us, O Lord, not to us, but to your name give glory (Ps. 113:1). For Balaam too prophesied (Num. 23:8 ff.), though he was not a prophet but a soothsayer; and Caiaphas prophesied (John 11:51), not by merit, but by the dignity of the priestly order; and Saul prophesied (1 Sam. 19:23), when he had already been filled with an evil spirit because of his disobedience; but this was for God's cause, so that he might not be able to seize David, whom he also wanted to kill.

And if I know all mysteries; for it profited Judas nothing to have been with the apostles and to have learned the mysteries, since, an enemy of love, he betrayed the Savior (Ezek. 28:13 ff.). And the prophet Ezekiel shows that the devil knows heavenly mysteries, when with a voice of rebuke he testifies that he was in the paradise of God and had precious stones, which stones the same Apostle signified as the mysteries of divine doctrine (1 Cor. 3:12): and it profited him nothing, because, unmindful of love, he leapt forth into pride.

And if I have all knowledge. Knowledge profits me nothing if there is no love. Finally, it profited the scribes and Pharisees nothing, as the Savior says: You have the key of knowledge, and neither do you enter, nor do you allow others to enter (Luke 11:52); for, corrupting love through envy, they brought its knowledge to nothing. For Tertullian and Novatian too were men of no small knowledge; but because through rivalry they lost the bonds of love, they turned to schism and created heresies to their own perdition.

And if I have all faith, so that I move mountains. To do works of power, or to cast out demons through faith, is the power and glory of God: nor does this avail for merit, unless one has been a zealous follower of a good manner of life, as I mentioned above.

(1 Cor. 13:3) And if I pay out all my substance. It is plain that if all one's substance is spent, it profits nothing if love is neglected; because love is the head of religion, and he who does not have the head does not have life. And if I hand over my body that I may burn, it profits me nothing. Without love it profits nothing; because love is the foundation of religion. Whatever, then, is done without love is perishable.

(1 Cor. 13:4–8) Love is magnanimous, it is pleasant. Love is not envious, is not puffed up, does not act wrongly, is not ambitious, does not seek the things that are its own, is not provoked, does not think evil, does not rejoice in iniquity, but rejoices with the truth: it tolerates all things, believes all things, hopes all things, endures all things. Love never falls. He has taught such great praises of love that he would seem, not undeservedly, to set it before the rest, and to show that those labor in vain who give their attention to the other commandments while not obeying this one. Hence the apostle John says: Love is God (1 John 4:8); so that he who does not have love may know that he does not have God; whence also in another epistle the same apostle Paul says: But God, who is rich in mercy, on account of his great love has had mercy on us (Eph. 2:4). He, then, who does not have love is ungrateful for the mercy of God, because he does not love that through which he has been saved. So that they might accordingly learn that those sinned gravely who put food before brotherly love. For this is what both profits in the present and abides with God for ever.

(1 Cor. 13:9–10) Whether prophecies will be made void, or tongues will cease, or knowledge will be made void. For we know in part, and we prophesy in part: but when that which is perfect has come, those things which are in part will be made void. He said that all the gifts of grace are made void, because they cannot comprehend as much as the truth itself contains. Nor can we either grasp or tell out the fullness of the truth. For how can it be that a human tongue should embrace all that is God's? Therefore it is our imperfection that will be destroyed; that which is true will not be made void: but while what is lacking is added to the imperfect, it will be destroyed. For the destruction of imperfection is when that which is imperfect is filled out into the true.

(1 Cor. 13:11) When I was a little child, I spoke as a little child, I understood as a little child, I thought as a little child: but when I became a man, I destroyed the things which were of a little child. He says this because the saints, on going out of this world, must necessarily find more than they now suppose, as the apostle John said of the Savior: Then we shall see him as he is (1 John 3:2). In this life, then, we are little children in comparison with the future life; because just as this life is imperfect, so also is our knowledge.

(1 Cor. 13:12) We see now through a mirror in an enigma, but then face to face. It is plain that now images are seen through faith, then the realities themselves. Now I know in part; but then I shall know, even as I am known: that is, I shall see the things which have been promised, as I am seen; that is, I shall be present with the Lord, where Christ is.

(1 Cor. 13:13) But now there remain faith, hope, love, these three: but the greater of these is love. Deservedly love is greater, because it was love that brought it about that faith should be preached and that there should be hope of the future life, as I mentioned above. Hence also the apostle John: By this we know, he says, his love; that he laid down his life for us (1 John 3:16). Rightly, then, love is greater, through which the human race has been refashioned.

Chapter XIV

(1 Cor. 14:1) Pursue love, be zealous for spiritual things, but rather that you may prophesy. After love he urges that zeal for prophesying should be held above the rest: because, although the spiritual ranks which he enumerates are great, this one is nevertheless better, which advances the benefit of the Church, so that all may learn the reasoning of the divine law. For in whatever a man has set his mind on, in that very thing he receives a gift, as Solomon says: To know the law is the mark of a most excellent mind (Prov. 9:33); for knowledge supported by love is not puffed up, but is gentle, profiting all for their benefit.

(1 Cor. 14:2) For he who speaks in a tongue speaks not to men but to God; for no one hears, but in the spirit he speaks mysteries. This is what he says: that he who speaks in an unknown tongue speaks to God, because he knows all things; but men do not know, and therefore no one profits from this. But in the spirit he speaks mysteries, not with understanding; because he does not know what he says.

(1 Cor. 14:3) For he who prophesies speaks to men for edification and exhortation and consolation; for a man is edified when he learns the solution of questions. And exhortation is given him, so that he may feel the desire to prophesy; and he is consoled, because he sees in hope the contempt borne for the discipline. For knowledge of the law strengthens minds and spurs them on to advance toward a better hope.

(1 Cor. 14:4) He who speaks in a tongue edifies himself; but he who prophesies edifies the Church. For through this, that perhaps he alone knows what he is saying, he edifies himself alone; but he who prophesies edifies the whole people, since what he says is understood by all. He calls prophets the interpreters of the Scriptures; for just as a prophet foretells things to come which are not known, so this man too, when he makes plain the meaning of the Scriptures which is hidden from many, is said to prophesy.

(1 Cor. 14:5) Now I wish you all to speak in tongues, but rather that you may prophesy. He could not forbid speaking in tongues, since he says above that this is a gift of the Holy Spirit; but zeal for prophesying is to be held higher for this reason, that it is more useful. For he who prophesies is greater than he who speaks in a tongue, unless he interprets. For if he is able to interpret, he will not be less, because he edifies the Church; for that is greater which benefits all. For that man speaks in tongues by the gift of God who also interprets, as did those twelve in the Acts of the Apostles (Acts 2:4).

(1 Cor. 14:6) But now, brethren, if I come to you speaking in tongues, what shall I profit you, unless I speak to you either in revelation, or in knowledge, or in prophecy, or in doctrine? All these signify one thing; for no one will be able to teach unless he is understood.

(1 Cor. 14:7–8) Yet things without a soul that give a sound, whether flute or harp, if they do not give a distinction of sound, how will what is piped on the flute or played on the harp be known? For if the trumpet gives an uncertain sound, who will prepare himself for battle? Since examples persuade more easily than words, he commends by examples, through which they may easily grasp that those who cannot interpret ought not to speak in tongues in the Church. For why should he speak whom no one understands?

(1 Cor. 14:9) So you also, unless you give through the tongue speech that signifies, how will it be known what you are saying? For you will be speaking into the air; that is, profiting nothing. For there are, for instance, many kinds of tongues in this world, and nothing is without voice. Many indeed are the kinds of tongues, he says, but they have their own meanings of words, so that they may be understood.

(1 Cor. 14:10–11) If then I do not know the force of the voice, I shall be a barbarian to him to whom I speak, and he who speaks will be a barbarian to me. He certainly does not advise zeal for this, that through an unknown tongue they should seem barbarians to one another; but since this is a matter of concord, one must strive for these things, so that through unanimity of understanding they may glory in a common joy.

(1 Cor. 14:12) So you also, since you are zealous for spirits, seek, for the edification of the Church, that you may prophesy. Because it is profitable to explain the Scriptures (for the mind is stirred and rejoices when it gathers something from the Scriptures; and the more inclined it becomes in this direction, the more it forsakes vices), therefore he advises that zeal should be applied to this part.

(1 Cor. 14:13) Therefore let him who speaks in a tongue pray that he may interpret. He advises that he who desires to speak in tongues ought to pray that he may receive the gift of interpreting, so that his zeal may profit the rest.

(1 Cor. 14:14) For if I pray in a tongue, my spirit prays, but my mind is without fruit. It is plain that our soul is ignorant if it speaks in a tongue which it does not know, as Latin men are accustomed to sing in Greek, delighted by the sound of the words, yet not knowing what they are saying. But the Spirit who is given in baptism knows what the soul is praying while it speaks or pleads in a tongue unknown to itself; but the mind, which is the soul, is without fruit. For what fruit can he have who does not know what he is saying?

(1 Cor. 14:15) What is it then? I will pray with the spirit, I will pray with the mind also; I will sing a psalm with the spirit, I will sing a psalm with the mind also. He says this: that when someone speaks in a tongue which he knows, he prays as much with the spirit as with the mind; because not only does his spirit, which I said is given in baptism, know what is being prayed, but the soul also, in like manner, is not ignorant of the psalm.

(1 Cor. 14:16) Otherwise, if you bless with the spirit; that is, if you speak the praise of God in a tongue unknown to the hearers: who fills the place of the uninstructed? How does he say Amen over your blessing, since he does not know what you are saying? For the unskilled man, hearing what he does not understand, does not know the end of the prayer and does not respond "Amen", that is, "true", so that the blessing may be confirmed. For the confirmation of the prayer is fulfilled through those who respond "Amen", so that all that is said may be confirmed in the minds of the hearers by the testimony of truth.

(1 Cor. 14:17) For you indeed give thanks well. He says this of the one who speaks things known to himself, because he knows what he is saying. But the other is not edified. If indeed you come together to edify the Church, those things ought to be said which the hearers understand. For what does it profit that someone should speak in a tongue which he alone knows, so that he who hears profits nothing? Therefore he ought to be silent in the Church, so that those may speak who profit the hearers.

(1 Cor. 14:18) I give thanks to my God that I speak in the tongue of you all. Since he said above that speaking in tongues is a gift of the Holy Spirit, he therefore refers it to God that he spoke in the tongue of all. And lest perhaps he should seem to say this as a rival, out of envy, he shows that he does indeed speak the tongues of all these, and that it is not of much profit.

(1 Cor. 14:19) But in the Church, he says, I wish to speak five words through the Law, that I may edify others also, rather than ten thousand words in a tongue. He says it is more useful to speak in a few words, in openness of speech, what all may understand, than to hold a lengthy discourse in obscurity. These were of the Hebrews, who sometimes used the Syriac tongue, but mostly Hebrew, in their discourses or oblations, to commend themselves. For they gloried in being called Hebrews because of the merit of Abraham, which the same Apostle counted as nothing, saying: But far be it from me to glory, save in the cross of our Lord Jesus Christ (Gal. 6:14). Some, imitating these, preferred to speak in the church to the people in a tongue unknown to themselves rather than in their own.

(1 Cor. 14:20) Brethren, do not become children in your senses; but be little ones in malice, that you may be perfect in your senses. He wishes them to be perfect, so that they may know what is necessary for the instruction of the Church; so that, withdrawing from malice and errors, they might be zealous for those things which would profit the benefit of the brethren. For he is perfect in sense who acts so as to profit someone, above all a brother.

(1 Cor. 14:21) For in the Law it is written: That in other tongues and in other lips I will speak to this people, and not even so will they hear me, says the Lord (Isa. 28:11). The Lord said this of those whom he foreknew would not believe even the Savior. For to speak in other tongues and in other lips is to preach the new testament, as the prophet Jeremiah says: Behold, the days will come, says the Lord, and I will accomplish for the house of Israel and for the house of Judah a new testament, not according to that which I appointed for their fathers (Jer. 31:31): that is, with the order changed, to speak otherwise than the words of the old law run; when they hear that the sabbath is loosed, the new moons made void, circumcision ceasing, the sacrifices changed, foods long forbidden permitted to be eaten, Christ preached as God from God: this is to speak in other tongues and in other lips; and not even so were the faithless willing to obey God. It can also be understood thus: that, since many of the Jews were ill-disposed, and therefore it was not fitting to speak the Gospel to them in revelation, he spoke to them in parables; and that, understanding that it was not revealed to them because they were wicked, not even so would they correct themselves, so as to make themselves deserving to hear the words of God through explanation. Hence the disciples say to the Lord: Lord, why do you speak to them in parables (Matt. 13:10)? And the Lord: Because to you it is given, he says, to know the mystery of the kingdom of God, but to them not; that seeing they may not see, and hearing they may not understand (ibid., 11); lest the unworthy should receive salvation; and perceiving that this was done in accordance with their deserts, not even so were they willing to be converted and make satisfaction to God.

(1 Cor. 14:22) Therefore tongues are for a sign. That is, the words of God were obscured by the veil of an unknown tongue, so that they might not be seen by the faithless; so that, when unknown tongues are heard, it may be a sign that this was done because of faithlessness, so that the hearer may not understand. Not, to be sure, to those who believe, but to those who do not believe: this is what he said, that tongues serve to hide the meaning from unbelievers. But prophecy is not for unbelievers, but for those who believe. That is, it is not fitting for the faithful to hear tongues which they do not understand, but for unbelievers, who are not worthy to understand, as the prophet Isaiah says: Go and say to this people: With the ear you shall hear and shall not understand, etc.

(1 Cor. 14:23) If the whole church comes together in one place, and all speak in tongues, and unbelievers and the uninstructed come in, will they not say that you are mad? It is plain that if all speak in different tongues, there arises a kind of disordered uproar of the people, as of one suffering a frenzy.

(1 Cor. 14:24–25) But if all prophesy, and some unbeliever or uninstructed man comes in, he is reproved by all, he is convicted by all, the secrets of his heart are made manifest; and then, falling on his face, he will worship God, declaring that God is truly among you. For when he understands and is understood, hearing God praised and Christ worshiped, he perceives that the religion is true and to be revered, in which he sees nothing counterfeit, nothing done in darkness, as among the pagans, whose eyes are veiled, lest, seeing clearly the things they call sacred, they should perceive that they are being mocked by various vanities. For every imposture seeks darkness and displays false things for true; therefore among us nothing is done craftily, nothing under a veil, but simply one God is praised, from whom are all things, and one Lord Jesus, through whom are all things (1 Cor. 8:6). For if there were no one who understood, or by whom he himself might be examined, he could say that it is some deception and vanity, which is chanted in tongues for this reason, that it would be a matter of shame if it were revealed.

(1 Cor. 14:26) What is it then, brethren? When you come together, each one of you has a song: that is, he utters the praise of God in a song. He has a doctrine: that is, he has an exposition of meanings through spiritual prudence. He has a revelation: that is, there is in him a prophecy of hidden things, which may reach the mind of all, with the favor of the Holy Spirit. He has a tongue; so as not to grieve those who were able to speak in a tongue, he permitted them to speak in tongues, yet on this condition, that interpretation should follow. Therefore he says: He has an interpretation; so that, if an interpreter were present, room might be given for speaking in tongues.

Let all things be done for edification. This is the conclusion: that nothing should be done to no purpose in the Church, and that one should labor rather for this, that even the unskilled may profit, lest any part of the body be in darkness through want of skill. For this reason he wishes all to come together prepared with various spiritual gifts, so that, wakeful through the very eagerness of their minds, exhorting one another, they might be zealous for the better gifts, for the enlightenment of the brethren.

(1 Cor. 14:27) If anyone speaks in a tongue, let it be by two, or at the most three, and one at a time, and let one interpret. That is, let two or three, no more, speak in tongues; but singly, not all at once, lest they should seem to be mad. Therefore, then, at the most three, lest those speaking in tongues and their interpreters should take up the day, and the prophets, who are the enlighteners of the whole Church, should have no time for discoursing on the Scriptures.

(1 Cor. 14:28) But if there is no interpreter, let him be silent in the church; but let him speak to himself and to God; that is, let him pray silently within himself, or speak to God, who hears all things that are mute; for in the church that man ought to speak who profits all.

(1 Cor. 14:29) But let the prophets speak, two or three, and let the others examine or question. He kept the same measure in saying: Let two or three speak; but singly, as above. And he permitted the rest to ask questions about things which perhaps come into doubt, or which someone cannot grasp; because minds are diverse, so that they may be made clear by plainer discussion.

(1 Cor. 14:30) But if something is revealed to another who is sitting, let the first be silent: that is, let the superior give way to the inferior, so that he may speak if he can; and let him not take it ill, because to that one too, though he seems inferior, a gift may be given to say what has not been granted to the superior. For just as everything cannot be granted to one man, even to the superior, so neither can nothing be imparted to anyone, however inferior; so that no one is empty of the grace of God.

(1 Cor. 14:31) For you can all prophesy one by one, that all may learn and all may be exhorted. This is the tradition of the Synagogue, which he wishes us to follow; because he writes indeed to Christians, but to those made so from the Gentiles, not from the Jews: that they should sit and discuss, the elders in rank on chairs, those next to them on benches, the last on the floor upon mats. If something is revealed to any of these, he commands that room to speak be given them, and that they not be despised, because they are members of the body.

(1 Cor. 14:32) And the spirit of the prophets is subject to the prophets. For since it is one and the same Spirit who pours himself both into the prophets who tell things to come and into those who disclose the Scriptures, according to the reason and nature of the cases, he therefore said: Is subject to the prophets; so as to kindle their minds with this hope, that the Spirit helps good endeavors. For he comes to the aid of the best desire to set forth the things of God, so as to fulfill the will of a good purpose. For the same was said also of the Savior: And they drank of the spiritual rock that followed, and the rock was Christ (1 Cor. 10:4): that is, to be subject is the same as to follow; for he followed so that, when human support failed, he might be at hand to give help. So also the Spirit is said to be subject, in that he helps good endeavors when he prompts; for he seems subject who completes what another has begun.

(1 Cor. 14:33) For he is not the author of dissension, but of peace. Since, then, he is the author of peace, as the Savior says: My peace I give to you, peace I leave to you (John 14:27), let no one fail to allow another to speak, nor ought he, out of zeal for contradiction, to resist one who is speaking, lest discord arise in the body. For those who are called in peace ought to be zealous for patience, lest the rights of peace be dissolved. As I teach in all the churches of the saints. By this saying he exhorts them to do what he has commanded, since he testifies that he preaches in like manner to the churches of the saints.

(1 Cor. 14:34) Let your women be silent in the church. Now he hands down what he had passed over; for above he commanded women to be veiled in the church; now he shows that they should be quiet and modest, so that it may be worthwhile that they are veiled (1 Cor. 11:5). For if man is the image of God, not woman, and she is subject to the man by the law of nature, how much more ought they to be subject in the church, out of reverence for him who is the envoy of the one who is also the head of the man! For it is not permitted to them to speak, but to be in silence, as the Law also says. What does the Law say? Your turning shall be to your husband, and he shall have dominion over you. (Gen. 3:16). This is a special law; hence Sarah called Abraham her husband lord; and for this reason they are ordered to be in silence, lest the sentence of the law mentioned above be weakened, mindful of which Sarah was subject to her husband, as has been said (Gen. 2:24). Although she is one flesh, yet she is ordered to be subject for two reasons: because she is both from the man, and through her sin entered in.

(1 Cor. 14:35) But if they wish to learn anything, let them ask their husbands at home; for it is shameful for women to speak in the church. It is shameful, because it is against discipline that in the house of God, who commanded them to be subject to their husbands, they should presume to speak about the Law, since they know that there men hold the primacy, and that it is more fitting for them to give themselves to prayers in the house of God, holding their tongue, and to open their ears, so as to hear how the mercy of God conquered through Christ the death which reigned through them. For if they dare to speak in the church, it is a disgrace; because they are veiled for this reason, that they may appear humbled; but they show themselves immodest, which is also a reproach to their husbands; for in the insolence of wives the husbands too are branded.

(1 Cor. 14:36) Or did the word of God go forth from you, or did it come to you alone? These are the words of one rebuking; for they were so puffed up, as though this salvation had been promised to them, and the other nations were being called to the faith by their example, or as though there were no others who could receive the grace of God at the preaching of the apostles. For they boasted as if, in coming to the faith, they were conferring a favor rather than receiving one; hence he says: Or did the word of God come to you alone? For everyone who wishes to buy something which he knows is not sought by others approaches the purchase with a certain disdain, as though about to do the seller a favor. Therefore the Apostle rebukes this in the Corinthians, who showed themselves such through the pride of vanity, as if, were they themselves not to obey the words of faith, there would be no one who would believe; as he says to the Jews: It was necessary to speak the words of this life to you first; but since you rejected them, making yourselves unworthy of eternal life, behold, we turn to the Gentiles (Acts 13:46).

(1 Cor. 14:37) If anyone is reckoned to be a prophet or spiritual, let him acknowledge that the things I write to you are the commandments of the Lord. In saying this he touches the false apostles mentioned above, by whom they had been corrupted, who, following the desires of men, taught not divine but earthly things. Therefore he says here that he hands down nothing of his own, but the Lord's, so that those whom he persuades may be seen to be won for God, not for men; with which confidence he also preaches steadfastly, having a free conscience, because he wishes to please not men but God. Hence he does not flatter sinners so that they may grow, but admonishes them so that they may cease.

(1 Cor. 14:38) But if anyone does not know, he will not be known. Rightly; for he who does not know that the things the Apostle speaks are the Lord's will himself also not be known by the Lord on the day of judgment, as the Lord says: Amen I say to you, I do not know you. (Matt. 25:12).

(1 Cor. 14:39) Therefore, brethren, be zealous to prophesy. Although he rebukes them, and in many things reproves and corrects them, because they had withdrawn from his tradition, he nevertheless calls them brethren; because Isaiah says to the people of the Lord: Say to those who do not walk rightly in my ways: You are our brethren (Isa. 66:5). So then, to console them after his corrections, he calls them brethren; and he urges them to zeal for prophecy, so that by constant discussion and explanation of the divine law they might become better instructed, so that they might be able to learn that the preachings of the false apostles were perverse.

And do not forbid speaking in tongues. This too is for the sake of love, so that those who can speak in tongues, if an interpreter is present, may not be forbidden, lest dissension arise.

(1 Cor. 14:40) But let all things be done decently and according to order; that is, according to the order stated above. And that is done decently which is done with peace and discipline.

Chapter XV

(1 Cor. 15:1–2) Now I make known to you, brethren, that the Gospel which I preached to you, which you also received, in which you also stand, through which you also will be saved, which I also announced to you in word, you ought to hold fast; unless perhaps you believed without cause. He shows them that if in this matter, which is set out below, they have been seduced away from his tradition, they have lost what they believed; for the whole hope of believers lies in this sense: that the dead will rise. And when he says: In which you also stand, he says it to those who were firm in the faith of this tradition; for he is speaking to a mixed company, so that those who are fixed in the faith may rejoice, and those who waver may grieve at being rebuked, and be corrected.

(1 Cor. 15:3) For I delivered to you first of all that Christ died for our sins according to the Scriptures. He recalls the Scriptures of the old testament, which foretold in song the future passion of the Lord. For Isaiah the prophet says: As a sheep he was led to the slaughter (Isa. 53:7). And among other things: For his life will be taken away from the earth; because of the iniquities of my people he was led to death (ibid., 8): these things are known to have been found fulfilled in Christ. But that the wording seems to signify past time is no objection; for with God, who foreknows all things, nothing is future; therefore he speaks of what is future for us as if it had been done. For in the Apocalypse of John the apostle it is said: The Lamb who was slain from the foundation of the world (Rev. 13:8). For since it will not happen otherwise than as God knows, it is said to have been done; for it happens for us, not for God, since with God there is nothing that may be called future. And in the Psalm he says: The Lord has reigned from the wood (Ps. 95:10). And Moses signified his cross thus, saying: Then you will see your life hanging before your eyes, and you will not believe (Deut. 28:66). For this reason, in the same matter, an expression of future time is also sometimes set down, lest perverse men should maintain that it was not said of Christ, if it all signified as though past.

(1 Cor. 15:4) And that he was buried. No one doubts that the dead man was buried, as Isaiah the prophet says: And I will give the wicked for his burial (Isa. 53:9).

And that he rose again on the third day according to the Scriptures. All things are enclosed in faith; therefore the resurrection of the Lord that was to take place on the third day was described in the person of the people. For Hosea the prophet says: After two days he will heal us, and on the third day we shall rise, and we shall live in his sight (Hos. 6:3). For none of the faithful denies that in Christ all have risen, just as in Adam all have died. In a similar way, in a psalm, under the person of man, he signifies Christ as well, saying: You have subjected all things under his feet: you have crowned him with glory and honor (Ps. 8:8).

(1 Cor. 15:5) And that he was seen by Cephas: that is, by Peter. This too the disciples told Cleopas and his companion, as in the Gospel κατὰ Λουκᾶν (according to Luke): that he had appeared to Peter alone. Afterward by the eleven (Luke 24:34). And this is contained in the Gospel.

(1 Cor. 15:6) Then he appeared to more than five hundred brethren at once. This he states on his own credit. Of whom many remain until now, but some have fallen asleep. Of those to whom the Lord appeared after the resurrection, he says that many were then still in the body.

(1 Cor. 15:7) Then he appeared to James. He appeared to James individually, as also to Peter: which I think was done for this reason, that appearing in many ways he might make faith in the resurrection firm. Afterward to all the apostles. Again he was seen by all the apostles on the mountain where Jesus had appointed them, as Matthew the evangelist relates (Matt. 28:16). Before that, in Jerusalem, eight days after his resurrection, that is, on the Lord's day, he came in to the disciples when the doors were shut, so that the mind of Thomas might be made firm, as John the apostle says (John 20:26). For it is on the mountain that he is understood to have given them his commandments.

(1 Cor. 15:8) And last of all, as to one untimely born, he was seen by me also. He appeared to him first in heaven, afterward while he was praying in the temple (Acts 9:4; 22:17). He called himself one untimely born because, born in Christ outside the due time, he received the apostleship when Christ had already been taken up into heaven with the flesh.

(1 Cor. 15:9–10) For I am the least of all the apostles. He humbles himself, and ascribes the reason to time; for he is lesser in time, not in dignity. But because he was also a persecutor, he bows himself down, saying: Who am not worthy to be called an apostle, because I persecuted the Church of God. But by the grace of God I am what I am. He gives the glory to God, who chose him; and he does not claim his dignity for himself, but calls himself unworthy. Hence he truly merits more, knowing that the Lord said: He who humbles himself will be exalted (Luke 14:11).

And his grace was not poor in me. He says this because he did not receive a lesser grace in the apostleship on account of having been a persecutor. But I labored more than all of them. He adds to the case that not only was he not lessened, but he was even afflicted more, because of the constancy with which he flourished in Christ; and bearing this without resentment, he was made more worthy. Yet not I, but the grace of God with me. Lest he be thought to have exalted himself boastfully, he at once humbles himself, since he ascribes this very thing not to himself but rather to the grace of God; so that he always refers all glory back to God, for which he is rightly to be exalted.

(1 Cor. 15:11) Whether then it was I or they, so we preach, and so you believed. This is what he indicated above, that he received in preaching no grace inferior to that of the other apostles, but one and the same. Hence he reproves the faithlessness of the Corinthians all the more, because, although this belief had been made manifest among all the churches, they had fallen away from this faith and hope, which he set down next, saying:

(1 Cor. 15:12–14) But if Christ is preached, that he rose from the dead, how do some among you say that there is no resurrection of the dead? If then there is no resurrection of the dead, neither has Christ risen. But if Christ has not risen, our preaching is vain, and your faith also is empty. These things had been handed down by the false apostles, who asserted that Christ was neither born, nor suffered in the flesh, nor rose again: these John the apostle also marks out, because they denied that Christ had come in the flesh. Hence he says: He who denies that Christ has come in the flesh, this is the Antichrist: and he who denies the Son does not have the Father either (1 John 4:3). These men went about and subverted the faith of certain people, lest they be judged unwise by the wise of the world; because the prudent of this age judge it foolish when they hear of the resurrection of the dead. But if, he says, it is true that Christ has not risen, we are false preachers, and your faith is vain. For this is what they believed when the Apostle preached, that the dead will rise, and by this hope they were drawn to the faith. He asserts that this would surely turn to their loss, if they believed what is not going to be: and it is a matter of shame for anyone to profess that he believed what is false. So he both strikes shame into them and calls their labors fruitless, if what they heard from the false apostles is true, that the dead will not rise: which surely no one bears to hear said of himself; so that, seeing that this was against them, they might return to their first faith.

(1 Cor. 15:15) And we are also found false witnesses of God, because we have borne witness against God, that he raised up Christ, whom he did not raise up. Whoever asserts that God raised Christ from the dead is a false witness, if it was not done; yet he proclaims the power of God, surely not as an enemy, since he ascribes so wonderful a deed to his power. But if it is true that he raised Christ from the dead, what must be said to this man, who is both a false witness against God and asserts that his work is folly?

(1 Cor. 15:16–17) For if the dead do not rise, neither has Christ risen. And if Christ has not risen, your faith is vain; for you are still in your sins. Then those also who have fallen asleep in Christ have perished. He frightens them, so that, since no one wishes ill to himself, they may grieve that they have begun to believe this, which is against themselves. For who would not wish to hear that his sins are forgiven?

(1 Cor. 15:18) Then those also who have fallen asleep in Christ have perished. He adds this to their terror, because they are unwilling to reckon the departure of their dear ones as perdition. For those who left the world under this hope, or who did not fear to be killed because they believed that they would rise after the example of Christ, have perished, if it is not true. He tells them this, which out of love for their departed they are unwilling to hear, so as to cut away from them what earlier, through error, they were willing to hear.

(1 Cor. 15:19) If in this life only we are hoping in Christ, we are more miserable than all men. It is manifest that both in this present life and in the future one we hope in Christ; for neither does Christ desert his servants here, but he gives them grace, and in the future they will be in eternal glory. But if there were no hope of the future life, we would be, as he said, more miserable than all men. For to what purpose are fasts, vigils, departures, a pure life, righteousness, mercy, death, if there will be no reward for these in the future? Unbelievers at least enjoy this life.

(1 Cor. 15:20–21) But now Christ has risen from the dead, the beginning of those who sleep; since indeed through a man came death, and through a man the resurrection of the dead. In saying these things he touches the false prophets, who denied that Christ was born, and therefore that the flesh had risen; because one who has not been born does not die either. Thus he proves that Christ rose from the dead because he was a man, so that there may be no doubt that a man was raised from the dead by God; so that, since death was found through the sin of a man, the righteousness of Christ has merited the resurrection of the dead.

(1 Cor. 15:22) For as in Adam all die, so also in Christ all will be made alive. He says this because, just as Adam by sinning found death, and held all who are of his origin, so that they are dissolved, so also Christ, not sinning, and through this conquering death (for he who does not sin conquers death, since death comes from sin), acquired life for all who are of his body, that is, resurrection. Although, then, he has granted a general resurrection, so that just as in Adam all, whether righteous or unrighteous, die, so also in Christ all, believers as well as those who distrust, rise, though the unbelievers rise to punishment; yet they seem to be made alive, because they will receive their bodies, no longer to die, but to suffer punishment in them without end: for they refused to believe.

(1 Cor. 15:23) But each one in his own order. Now he wishes to set out the order of the resurrection, lest they should think it a fable because it has not yet happened in the rest: he sets out for them the orders and the times, when it happened and when it is going to happen that the dead rise. Christ the beginning. As he testifies in the Acts of the Apostles that it is written in Moses (Acts 26:23): If Christ is capable of suffering, if he is the first to rise from the dead, etc. He rose first, then, that he might become a pattern for those who believe in him. Then those who are Christ's, who believed in his coming. This is to be understood in a twofold way: because both at his second coming the saints will rise, according to the faith of the Apocalypse of John (Rev. 20:12), and also at the first coming, when he rose, many bodies of the saints rose (Matt. 27:52), as a public witness that death had been conquered and despoiled. Under one signification, then, he includes a twofold coming of the Lord.

(1 Cor. 15:24–26) Then the end, when he has delivered the kingdom to God and the Father, when he has destroyed every principality and every power and every virtue and every dominion. For he must reign until he puts all enemies under his feet; for he has subjected all things under his feet. And last of all, death the enemy will be destroyed. He says this because, when the kingdom has been delivered, there will be the end of the world, the resurrection having been accomplished. Certain people, frightened by the harshness of this expression, turn aside from the proper sense while they wish to think, as it were, piously. For they shudder at the sound of the word when they hear: When he has delivered the kingdom to God and the Father; thinking that by this delivery of the kingdom, if it is understood according to the sound of the word, he remains empty; so that, if he delivers it, he himself no longer has it: as if the Father, in delivering all things to the Son, had emptied himself. For the Lord himself says: All things have been delivered to me by my Father (Matt. 11:27); and afterward: And this is eternal life, that they may know you, the only true God, and Jesus Christ whom you have sent (John 17:3). See: while the Son reigns, the Father reigns too. And how could the Son be believed to empty himself by delivering the kingdom, when the angel says to Mary: And the Lord God will give him the throne of David his father, and he will reign in the house of Jacob for ever, and of his kingdom there will be no end (Luke 1:32)? And Daniel says of the same kingdom, among other things: The God of heaven will raise up another kingdom, which will never be corrupted; and this kingdom will be eternal (Dan. 2:44). Let no one, then, doubt that the Son will always reign with the Father. But this delivery of the kingdom is this: that when all things have been subjected to the Son, and have worshiped him as God, death having been destroyed, then the Son will show them that he is not the one from whom are all things, but the one through whom are all things: and this will be to deliver the kingdom to God and the Father, to show that he it is from whom all fatherhood in heaven and on earth is named (Eph. 3:15): and then will be the end. The destruction of death, however, is the resurrection of the dead.

(1 Cor. 15:27–28) But when he says: All things are subjected to him, clearly except him who subjected all things to him. And when all things have been subjected to him, then the Son himself also will be subjected to him who subjected all things, that God may be all in all. It is the same sense, which he now opens up, to make plain what it is for the Son to deliver the kingdom to God and the Father: but he adds this to the sense, that the Father, in delivering the kingdom to the Son, did not subject himself to him, as the Son subjected himself to the Father; for it is incongruous that the Father should be subjected to the Son. So the Father subjected all things to the Son, that he might be honored in like manner, just as the Father too is honored. When, then, all things, laid beneath his feet, have confessed Christ as God, then Christ the Lord himself also will be subjected to God the Father: That God may be all in all. He says this because, when the pride of all principalities and powers and dominions has been crushed, and they have worshiped Christ as God, then Christ too, on account of the unique authority of the Father, will indeed show himself God, but from God; so that the sublime and ineffable authority of the one principle may remain. This is the Son's subjecting himself to the Father, this is God's being all in all; because when every creature learns that its head is Christ, and that the head of Christ is God the Father, God the Father is all in all (Phil. 2:10–11), that is, so that every creature may think one thing, so that with one voice every tongue of those in heaven and on earth and under the earth may confess that there is one God, from whom are all things. But if he had said: That God may be in all, it would be understood that he is indeed in all, but by affection, or by a common sense; not, however, that those very things are from him. But when he said: All in all, he signified this: that they are both from him, and he is in them: from him, because they were created by him; and in them, because in the confession of all there is one God. Yet the Son is not subjected to the Father in the way that the creation is subjected to the Son.

(1 Cor. 15:29) Otherwise what will they do who are baptized for the dead, if the dead do not rise at all? Why are they also baptized for them? So firmly does he wish to show the resurrection of the dead to be ratified and stable that he gives the example of those who were so secure about the future resurrection that they were even baptized for the dead, if death had perhaps overtaken someone: fearing lest one who had not been baptized should rise either badly or not at all, a living person was dipped in the name of the dead. Hence he added: Why are they also baptized for them? By this example he does not approve their deed, but shows their fixed faith in the resurrection.

(1 Cor. 15:30–31) Why are we also in danger every hour? Daily I die for your glory, which I have in the Lord. By saying: Why are we also in danger? he distinguished the persons, showing that those who were baptized for the dead were not catholics. Indeed he said above: Why are they also baptized for them? Are we also baptized for them? For Jephthah too, though in a matter that could not be made acceptable, was found faithful, offering his daughter according to his vow, which he had foolishly vowed (Judg. 11:39); so it is not the deed that is approved, but perseverance in faith is held up as an example. He recalls the dangers and labors of the preachers, saying: Why are we also in danger? and there is understood: unless because we hope for the future resurrection, as he recalls above. For, secure that after this death true life will follow, they do not fear the dangers brought on them by unbelievers, because of their zeal for those whom the unbelievers grieve to have lost, because they became believers. So he shows the solicitude which he had for their salvation, on account of the glory promised by the Lord, as it was also said in the Law: Lift up your voice like a trumpet; if they hear you, you will gain their souls (Ezek. 33:5). To this the Apostle, ever solicitous, was hastening, that the glory of the salvation he had won for many might increase for him with God.

(1 Cor. 15:32) If according to man I fought with beasts at Ephesus, what does it profit me, if the dead do not rise? That is, if according to human sense (which, seeing that it does not fall within the nature of things that flesh once dissolved should be re-formed to life, by no means believes in the future resurrection of the dead) I did not fear to be thrown to beasts and to die, I have gained nothing. But this is not so, since, certain of the promised life, he not only did not fear, but even willingly allowed himself to be thrown to beasts, as among other things he says in the Acts of the Apostles: For I am ready not only to be bound, but also to die in Jerusalem for the name of the Lord Jesus Christ (Acts 21:13).

Let us eat and drink, for tomorrow we shall die. This was said by Isaiah the prophet on account of those who, as if there were to be nothing after death, cared only for the belly, like cattle (Isa. 22:13), as did those also who were corrupting the Corinthians.

(1 Cor. 15:33) Do not be seduced: evil conversations corrupt good morals. He signifies that a good purpose can be overthrown by the talk of evil men; for constant depraved conversation vitiates the mind, and therefore one must beware of them. For whoever wishes to guard the faith he has received must avoid those who clamor against it, above all at the very beginnings, so that he may fulfill what he has judged useful.

(1 Cor. 15:34) Be watchful, be righteous, and do not sin. He warns that they must be watchful, lest, caught by the deceit of a depraved sense and led away from the faith, they be corrupted by not believing in the resurrection of the dead. He commanded them to be righteous, so that they might cultivate not only earthly righteousness but also heavenly; for earthly righteousness does not produce merit, just as it does not make one guilty either. For what great thing is it not to take what belongs to another, since this comes of fear? But it is a great thing to give of one's own to one who has not; this is true righteousness. And he added that they should not sin; from which he wished it to be understood that he had not commanded temporal righteousness to be kept, but divine; for he who keeps heavenly righteousness is without doubt perfect in this present one.

For some have ignorance of God; I speak to your shame. Ignorant of God's purpose, those who were being converted among them said that it was foolish to believe that the dead would rise. Therefore he strikes shame into them as being imprudent; for they believed those who, having ignorance of God, taught contrary to God's decrees.

(1 Cor. 15:35) But someone will say: How will the dead rise? Or with what body will they come? He said that those have ignorance of God whose words of depraved opinion he brought forward, saying: How will the dead rise? or with what body will they come? To whom he fittingly replied, saying:

(1 Cor. 15:36) Fool, what you sow is not made alive, unless it first dies? With the natural man he certainly does not deal by the authority of the Law, so as to persuade him by examples which, if he accepted them, he would not err; but by physical reasoning, with which he flatters himself so as not to believe that dissolved and dead bodies rise. For he shows that dead things are restored again to life, and multiplied, so that human error may be confounded.

(1 Cor. 15:37–38) And what do you sow? You do not sow the body that is to be, but a bare grain, of wheat, say, or of some one of the others: but God gives it a body as he wills, and to each of the seeds its own body. If, then, a bare grain is sown, and by God's will, in a certain way through the ministry of the elements, rises clothed, having with it many increases for human use, why should it not be credible that by God's power a dead man can rise, with his substance only improved, not multiplied in number? Why, then, does the unbeliever ask with what body the dead rise, when examples go before to lead to this faith in a resurrection that is improved, not one of lost substance? For he will receive his own body, in which Adam was created, according to the examples mentioned above.

(1 Cor. 15:39) Not all flesh is the same flesh: but one is of man, another of cattle; one flesh of birds, another flesh of fish. Since all flesh is of the same mass, whence and how is there such diversity in these, that one material should produce diverse kinds? Let the sophists of the world and the searchers of the stars now say whether they are able to comprehend what is outside themselves, when they do not know what is within themselves. In fact, all the worldly philosophers, who were unwilling to humble their mind to the law of God so as to receive faith in it, always asserting different and contrary things, have confounded one another with the emptiest disputations; because in none of them was a sign of attested truth recognized which, set before their words, might commend their doctrine, as God also decreed for our teaching, which is proved not by words but by power, which words cannot resist. As, then, from one material the flesh of living creatures is diverse, so also men of one flesh will be diverse in dignity in the resurrection, so that each will appear such as his merit has been: seeing that even here we see a diversity of bodies.

(1 Cor. 15:40) There are both heavenly bodies and earthly bodies, but the glory of the heavenly is one, and that of the earthly another. The heavenly bodies are those of the risen, but earthly ones are those before they die or rise; so that, since Christ is heavenly, bodies are called heavenly from him, but from Adam, because he is earthly, bodies are called earthly.

(1 Cor. 15:41) One is the brightness of the sun, another the brightness of the moon, and another the brightness of the stars; for star differs from star in brightness. He uses a comparison of one nature to give an account of an undifferentiated substance; because just as the sun and moon and stars, though they are indeed of one nature, are yet of diverse brightness, so men too, though they are of one kind, will nevertheless be unlike in glory according to merit; so that the dignity of those who have the hundredfold number may be made equal to the brightness of the sun, those who, to be perfect, strove for the first degree, of whom it was said: Then the righteous will shine like the sun in the kingdom of their Father (Matt. 13:43). To the brightness of the moon are to be compared those who have purchased the sixtyfold number by good works, so as to have the merit of the second degree. And to the brighter stars are to be compared the merits of those who by righteous labors have sought the thirtyfold number, the dignity of the third degree. But to the following stars, which are midway between the bright and the darker ones, sinful men are to be fitted, who were unwilling to attain the honor of any of the three degrees. And to the gloomy stars, the last of all, the resurrection of the faithless is like; because, having followed error, they are to be compared to the stars that lead into error, as Jude the apostle says in his Epistle (Jude 13). For unbelief cannot have a bright resurrection; because as a coal covered with its own ash is darkened, so these too, surrounded by the darkness of error through their faithlessness, will lack light.

(1 Cor. 15:42) So also will be the resurrection of the dead. He gives examples so that there may be no doubt that the resurrection of the dead will take place; so that, the reason having been grasped, no one may hesitate. It is sown in corruption, it will rise in incorruption. To sow is to bury, so that it decays; but to rise in incorruption is that, once raised up, it can no longer decay, but has the brightness of immortality.

(1 Cor. 15:43) It is sown in dishonor, it will rise in glory. It is sown in dishonor, because while it is laid in a gloomy place it becomes fetid and swarms with worms: but it will rise in glory, because when raised it will both be light and suffer no injury of squalor. It is sown in weakness, it will rise in power. It is sown in weakness, because it is immobile and ungovernable: but it will rise in power, because it will then be wholly one and vigorous.

(1 Cor. 15:44) It is sown a natural body, it will rise a spiritual body. If there is a natural body, there is also a spiritual one. It is a natural body while it is sustained by food in order to live; but a spiritual one when it will need none of these things, being changed into life. All that was said above is concluded in this sense; for nothing else is contained in the preceding except that the natural body dies and will rise spiritual, so that it no longer eats, nor drinks, nor falls sick, nor is fetid, nor foul by nature.

(1 Cor. 15:45) The first man Adam was made into a living soul. This was said in Genesis (Gen. 2:7): that, having received the spirit of the breath of God, man was made into a living soul; for the office of the spirit is to animate the body, yet in such a way that it does not have continuous life unless it makes use of the help of food and drink. But the last Adam into a life-giving spirit: that is, so that he who before had been made into a soul was made into a spirit through the resurrection, so that he cannot die, because he will become wholly living.

(1 Cor. 15:46) But not first what is spiritual; because the natural is from Adam, but the spiritual through Christ and the resurrection of the dead.

(1 Cor. 15:47–48) The first man from the earth, earthy; the second man from heaven, heavenly: as is the earthy, such are the earthy also; and as is the heavenly, such are the heavenly also. The first man, Adam, is from the earth, temporal, and death succeeds to him: the second man, Christ, is from heaven, who, having risen, knows not how to die: As is that earthly one, such are the earthly ones also: that is, because the earthy one is mortal, the earthy are mortal too: for Adam by sinning found death, so that all who are from him are mortal. And as is that heavenly one, such let the heavenly ones be also: that is, because Christ did not sin, conquering death he is heavenly; so let those be who believe in him: although unbelievers too will be spiritual, but like the spiritual powers of wickedness, so that they are dark. For he is now speaking of the saints, who will rise to glory; for just as those who do not believe are already judged, so those who believe are already called heavenly (John 3:18).

(1 Cor. 15:49) Therefore, as we have borne the image of that earthy one, let us bear also the image of this heavenly one: that is, as we had the form of mortality through the sin of Adam, having been made slaves of sin, so let us also have the form of life through the righteousness of the Savior, having been made slaves of righteousness, not of the world but of God, while we do the things that are worthy of immortality.

(1 Cor. 15:50) But this I say, brethren, that flesh and blood cannot possess the kingdom of God. By flesh he wants faithlessness to be understood, by blood a base and luxurious life, because this desire is generated by the heat of the blood; to show that not only does the unbeliever not have a worthy resurrection, but also the one who obeys the desires and vices of the flesh. So he admonishes and instructs us by what means we may merit to attain the kingdom of heaven.

Nor will corruption possess incorruption. He says this because corruption and wantonness of life must necessarily suffer punishment. For he set down "corruption" for suffering; for it is certainly established that all men rise immortal.

(1 Cor. 15:51) Behold, I tell you a mystery: we shall all indeed rise; that is, believers as well as unbelievers. But we shall not all be changed. Now he signifies each one's merit in the resurrection, of what kind it will be in each individual.

(1 Cor. 15:52) In a moment, in the twinkling of an eye, that is, quickly, without any delay. At the last trumpet; last for this reason, because the final war is waged against the demons, and princes, and powers, and the devil himself. For this will take place after the thousand years in which the Savior will reign here, the Antichrist having been destroyed, when Satan will be let loose from his prison to seduce the nations Gog and Magog, who are demons, so that they may fight against the camp of the saints; for temporal men will not be able to contend against eternal ones. For John the apostle too says: I saw Michael and his angels in heaven fighting against the dragon and his angels, who is the devil and Satan (Rev. 12:7). And therefore he says here last trumpet, because the battle against him has been fought more than once; because both in the Antichrist (the false prophets, who worked signs and wonders before him, and he himself the victor) and in this place he will be fought against for the last time, so that he may now be sent into Gehenna. And the Savior, among other things, says: Now the prince of this world will be cast down (John 12:31). So both then and often he has been conquered, as Isaiah the prophet says: How Lucifer has fallen from heaven, who rose in the morning (Isa. 14:12).

For the trumpet will sound, and the dead will rise incorrupt, and we shall be changed. For when sinners and the ungodly rise, the saints will be changed in brightness, so that the flesh becomes spiritual with glory, as if lead were to become gold. For the saints will rise at the coming of the Lord, as he said above (1 Cor. 15:23): Christ the beginning: then those who are Christ's at his coming, as is contained in the Apocalypse; for sinners are not Christ's, who hear from the Lord: I do not know you (Matt. 7:23). For in the first psalm too it is written: Therefore the ungodly will not rise in judgment, nor sinners in the council of the righteous (Ps. 1:5). For of the ungodly there is nothing to be inquired: but they will rise for this, that they may perish, seeing that what they refused to believe is true: and to perish is to be estranged from God, as he says to the Romans: For whoever have sinned without the law will perish without the law; and those who have sinned in the law will be judged by the law (Rom. 2:12): and so sinners will judge; for they are unworthy to rise with the saints, as he says: Nor sinners in the council of the righteous.

(1 Cor. 15:53) For this corruptible must put on incorruption, and this mortal put on immortality. Above he said, and the dead will rise incorrupt, that is, they will be immortal, yet not impassible; for those of whom he speaks will suffer punishments according to the qualities of their offenses, so that they go out from there when the debt has been paid: but the ungodly are tormented with eternal punishment; for he says: He who does not believe is already judged (John 3:18). Hence Isaiah the prophet also says: Their fire will not be quenched, and their worm will not die (Isa. 66:24). At the coming of the Lord, however, both the saints will rise, and those who are found alive will be caught up to meet the Lord in the air, undergoing death as if it were sleep; for in the very catching up they will have both death and resurrection, as the same Apostle writes to the Thessalonians (1 Thess. 4:16 ff.). For in the time of the Antichrist men will be either apostates, or guilty, or in hiding places, or placed in punishment; the rest are Gentiles, whom the Lord Jesus, together with their leader the Antichrist, will slay at his coming with the spirit of his mouth (2 Thess. 2:8), that is, at his command they will be burned up with fire by the angels of his power.

(1 Cor. 15:54–55) But when this mortal has put on immortality, then will come to pass the word which is written: Death is swallowed up in victory. Where, O death, is your victory? where, O death, is your goad? For after the resurrection of the ungodly and of sinners there will be swallowed up death in victory. For they are the words of one who exults over him, saying: Where, O death, is your victory? where, O death, is your sting? For the victory of conquered death is the resurrection of the dead: and death is the devil, over whom the triumph is raised; and he is called contentious, because through pride, though he was unequal in strength, he kept resisting. When, then, overcome, he is destroyed and the spoils are stripped from him, wrath is vented on him who fought against him.

(1 Cor. 15:56) But the sting of death is sin. By sting he signifies authority, because death received authority through sin; as though, if there were no sin, the devil would have grown numb, and there would be no death. But the power of sin is the law. The law is the power of sin for this reason, that sin would not be imputed if there were no law.

(1 Cor. 15:57) But thanks be to God, who has given us the victory through our Lord Jesus Christ. He says that the triumph of the Savior has profited us; for what the Savior did, he surely did not do for himself, but for us. Wherefore the victory is ours; for this reason he was made man while remaining God, that, conquering the devil by not committing sin, he might acquire the victory for us, who were held in death because of sin; so that the devil, conquered when Christ, who had not committed sin, was slain, might lose all whom he was holding because of sin. But in insulting man he became guilty, and so, the handwriting having been blotted out, that is, the sentence having been blotted out by which the human race was held for death, it was rescued, and souls were led into heaven; so that they might be taken from him not by power, but by righteousness, by God through our Lord Jesus Christ.

(1 Cor. 15:58) Therefore, my beloved brethren, be steadfast and immovable, abounding in the work always, knowing that your labor is not vain in the Lord. Having set out all the things that are necessary for ordering the Church's teaching, and having shown either what is to be avoided or what is to be followed, he now exhorts them to be fixed in the good, and always ready and abundant in the works that bear fruit with the Lord; because through these things which have been set out, they will in future receive the reward of good work from the Lord, so that they could not be overthrown by depraved conversations.

Chapter XVI

(1 Cor. 16:1–2) Now concerning the collections which are made for the saints, as I ordered the church of Galatia, so do you also. On the first day of the week let each one of you lay by with himself, storing up whatever he will, so that the collections are not made only when I come. He commands that the collection be made on the Lord's day, just as he had also arranged for the other churches; so that on the day on which the Lord rose, his people may be gathered to the praise and glory of God, death having been vanquished: then the funds are to be gathered for the ministry of the saints, who were suffering affliction for the name of the Lord through the zeal of the Gentiles or of the Jews, who did not allow them to preach the grace of God under the name of Christ; so that they might have relief from those on whose account they are afflicted. So he bids the people be gathered on every Lord's day, and those funds be collected, as they will, for the gifts of God, and each one lay this by with himself as in a treasury; so that when the Apostle comes, it may be found prepared for necessary uses; because what is gathered little by little is not burdensome, and is found to be much.

(1 Cor. 16:3) But when I come, those whom you have approved by letters, these I will send to carry your grace to Jerusalem. This consolation brought benefit in a twofold kind, as it does now also; so that it benefited both the saints mentioned above and the poor established in the Church, as it now too benefits both the needs of the aged and the needy. He says that he sends by letter those whom they have chosen as suitable for the dispensation: these letters give testimony to those who are sent, so that they may be received with grace.

(1 Cor. 16:4) But if it is worthy that I also go, they will go with me. He says this: if the collection is plentiful, he too can go; for it is worthy, and so it is needful, that if he himself goes, he should carry much with him for the relief of the saints. This is what he signifies when he says to the Romans: For if the Gentiles have shared in their spiritual things, these ought to minister to them in carnal things (Rom. 15:27); for they were of the Jews, of whom the Lord says that salvation is from the Jews, and at Jerusalem they were in great ill will among the Jews who did not believe.

(1 Cor. 16:5–6) But I will come to you when I have passed through Macedonia: and with you perhaps I will remain, or even winter; so that you may bring me on my way wherever I go. After all his instruction, to make them glad, he promised them his coming, by which he also strengthens all the more all his admonitions with which he corrects them. One who hears that the one from whom he received the law is coming will be more careful, lest he be put to shame when he arrives.

(1 Cor. 16:7) For I do not wish to see you now in passing. Knowing that he had many things to deal with among them, he does not wish to see them in passing, and lest they be saddened, he promises that he will stay a long time with them; because what he does, he does with providence. For I hope to remain with you some little time, if the Lord permits. If the Lord: for the Lord then permits him to stay with them (he says permits), if they show themselves worthy, amending the vices which he rebukes in them.

(1 Cor. 16:8–9) But I will stay at Ephesus until Pentecost; for a great door has been opened to me, and one full of work, and many adversaries. He shows the cause for which he had remained at Ephesus; for he had found there hearts thirsting for the grace of God, into which he might pour the mystery of Christ. But because the devil is always restless, and envious of those who desire God, he added: And many adversaries; for the more people were found suitable for the faith, the more there was no lack of those who were jealous, contradicting and resisting the teaching of the Lord.

(1 Cor. 16:10–12) But if Timothy comes, see that he is without fear among you; for he does the work of the Lord, as I also do. Therefore let no one despise him. For although Timothy preached the things which he had received from the Apostle, and was a suitable evangelist, yet because he was not of the same authority, he commends him; lest perhaps he should not be received as he deserved by those among the people who were at variance, and his coming should profit nothing for their salvation. For the Gentiles too could be stirred up to sedition and to the discord of the people, once they had an occasion to rush upon Timothy; therefore let no one despise him, because he does the work of the Lord, he says, as I also do; that is, that he should be received on my authority, by which he was an apostle; for he himself was a bishop. Bring him on his way in peace, that he may come to me; for I am waiting for him with the brothers. He records so great a merit in Timothy that he not only commands that he be honored among them, but shows that he is to be brought on his way with deference as an apostle of the Lord; so that he himself was also waiting for him with the brothers for the sake of the Gospel of Christ.

But concerning brother Apollos I make known to you that I asked him much to come to you with the brothers; and indeed it was not his will to come now. But he will come when it is opportune. In saying this he shows both that he has satisfied their wish or request, since he displayed his own desire to them, and that he excused Apollos: since he did not declare why he was not going to them; yet in such a way that he did not excuse himself altogether, but put it off. It can also be understood thus: that through Apollos he touches them, signifying that Apollos was unwilling to go to them for this reason, that they were at variance; so that on hearing this they might be zealous for peace; and that for this reason he said of Timothy: See that he is without fear among you; because some who were sent were not well received: and therefore that Apollos will come when they are of one mind.

(1 Cor. 16:13–14) Watch, stand in the faith, act manfully, be made strong: let all your things be done in love. Watch, he says, lest they be circumvented in the faith. Act manfully: this is to warn them to be strong concerning the things which had been handed down to them: not like children, who do not know what they should follow. Be strengthened: so that they may be robust as much in word as in deed; because profession and good acts make men perfect. And he warns that these very things be done in love; because what is done with love has the fear and zeal of God; for whatever is done with offense to a brother has no fruit. For some are to be provoked to good, not saddened: for example, if you do not show mercy, by dissuading another, or are inhuman, or brand others, or eat something, insulting him who abstains from it, so as to cause him a scruple. All things are to be done in such a way that there is exhortation to better things, not insult, and then there is peace. Hence the Lord says, by this it is known if you are my disciples, if you love one another (John 13:35): but where there is contention and dissension, there is no love.

(1 Cor. 16:15–16) But I beseech you, brothers: you know the house of Stephanas and of Fortunatus, that they are the beginning of Achaia, and have appointed themselves to the ministry of the saints; that you also be subject to men of this kind, and to everyone who works with us and labors. The Achaeans are Corinthians; and therefore he speaks to them about these men, through whom he provokes them to a work of this sort; for they are from among them. For this is a great thing in Christians, that, avarice trodden underfoot, they bestow from their labors on those who have not, made imitators of God the Father, who is rich in mercy. But when he says: That you be subject to such, he exhorts them all the more: that, having become emulators of this work, they themselves may have others subject to them.

(1 Cor. 16:17–18) But I rejoice in the presence of Stephanas, and Fortunatus, and Achaicus; because they have supplied what was lacking to you. For they have refreshed both my spirit and yours. Acknowledge therefore those who are of this sort. For the contribution which he says is made for the saints is one thing, and to make return to the ministers of the Gospel is another, as the Lord says: For the laborer is worthy of his wage (Luke 10:7). For he signifies that the house of Stephanas was devoted in both respects, both in the ministry of the saints and toward those who served the churches; so that they benefited the Apostle together with the brothers Fortunatus and Achaicus; so that those also benefited who were from among them. Hence he says: For they have refreshed both my spirit and yours. And since the Apostle denies that he will receive anything from them, for he says: This boasting shall not be broken in me in the regions of Achaia, how have they refreshed both my spirit and yours? But I believe his spirit was refreshed in those whom they had benefited; for he says that what benefits those whom he wished is given to himself. Acknowledge therefore those who are of such a kind; that is, imitate them.

(1 Cor. 16:19) The churches of Asia greet you. By their greeting he reminds them to become like those who are of one province. Aquila and Priscilla greet you in the Lord, with the church of their household. He mentions two churches, the public and the household one: he calls public the one where all come together; household the one in which people are gathered through friendship. Wherever presbyters celebrate the solemn rites, it is called a church.

(1 Cor. 16:20) All the brothers greet you. He lifts their spirits when he shows that all these are mindful of them. Greet one another with a holy kiss. The holy kiss is a sign of peace, in which he teaches that they should cleave to one another, discord having been removed.

(1 Cor. 16:21) My greeting, by the hand of Paul. He shows that he has signed this letter himself, and he added what the words of the signature are, saying:

(1 Cor. 16:22) If anyone does not love the Lord Jesus Christ, let him be anathema maranatha, which is interpreted: If anyone does not love the Lord Jesus, who has come, let him be cut off. For Maranatha means: the Lord has come. This is on account of the Jews who said that Jesus had not come; these, then, are anathema from the Lord, who has come.

(1 Cor. 16:23–24) The grace of our Lord Jesus Christ be with you. My love be with you all, in Christ Jesus. He put such a signature for this reason, on account of those who, while they were eager for dissension, did not love the Lord Jesus; seeing that they said: I am of Paul, I of Apollos, but I of Cephas (1 Cor. 1:12). For he who loves Christ does not give glory to men, because there is no hope in men. The grace of our Lord Jesus Christ be with you; and it is understood: you who love the Lord Jesus. And since they did not love one another, therefore he says this: My love be with you all in Christ Jesus; so that they might learn to love one another with that grace with which they were loved by the Apostle, not with the affection of the flesh, but in Christ Jesus.
