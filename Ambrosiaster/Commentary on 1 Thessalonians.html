Commentary on 1 Thessalonians

Latin source: the commentary on the First Epistle to the Thessalonians by the anonymous Roman writer called Ambrosiaster (written at Rome about 366–384), from the Maurist text of 1690, printed under the name of Ambrose in J.-P. Migne, Patrologia Latina 17 (Paris, 1845), cols. 441–454, verified against the page images and collated in full with the Maurist edition (Paris, 1690). Migne's misprints are corrected where the Maurist edition he reprints or Ballerini's edition (Milan, 1877) has the right reading, as are his misprinted Scripture references and verse labels. At 2:3 the lemma reads in dolo ("in guile") with Vogels' critical text, the Greek and Ambrosiaster's own comment (in dolo et fallacia), where Migne, the Maurists and the oldest manuscript (Monte Cassino 150) have in idolo; on 2:16 the comment's credulitate is emended to incredulitate ("unbelief"), as the sense requires. The lemmata are translated from Ambrosiaster's own Old Latin text of Paul, and all other Scripture as he quotes it, from his own Latin; italics mark the lemmata and the other Scripture he quotes. The edition's verse labels are given as (1 Thess. chapter:verse). This English translation is released into the public domain.

Prologue

In order to exhort others to increase in faith by the example of these men, that is, the Thessalonians, he praises them, because, persevering in the grace they had received, they had grown toward the advancement of their reward by pursuing better things; and he exhorts them to press on to perfection, so that the dignity won by their virtues might provoke the rest. For they had drunk in the spirit of such great faith that, in the hope of things to come, they endured with a devoted mind the persecutions inflicted on them for the name of Christ even by their own fellow citizens, the Macedonians. So, to set down in a letter their merit and his own joy at their advancement, he writes to them thus:

Chapter I

(1 Thess. 1:1) Paul, and Silvanus, and Timothy, to the church of the Thessalonians in God our Father and in the Lord Jesus Christ: grace to you and peace. The letter contains the names of three bishops, but the thoughts and the words are the Apostle's alone. For because they exult with a common joy at their advancement, the epistle is written to the people by common consent, to the exalting of God the Father and of the Lord Jesus Christ, wishing them peace and grace.

(1 Thess. 1:2–3) We give thanks to God always for all of you, making remembrance of you in our prayers without ceasing, mindful of the work of your faith and the labors of your love, and of the expectation of our Lord Jesus Christ, before God and our Father. Since all good things are from God, they therefore first render thanks for their advancement to him who deigned not only to give his gift to men placed under sin, but to adopt them as sons, at the same time helping them to endure every deadly peril, that they may become more worthy. For this reason they say that they remember them in their prayers, because in the work of faith they were becoming renowned among the other churches; so that by the will of God they might complete the work begun, prompt in the zeal of love, and of patience, and of hope in the Lord's coming, before God, who deigned to make his Son incarnate for human salvation. For it is God's good pleasure if progress is made in Christ.

(1 Thess. 1:4–5) Knowing, brothers beloved by God, your election; for our Gospel was not to you in word only, but also in power, and in the Holy Spirit, and in much fullness, as you know what kind of men we were among you for your sake. They signify that they were humble among them, so as to give them a pattern of meekness; not that the mighty works were lacking which might exhort them to faith; for the power of signs and wonders lent strength to the gospel teaching, in the Holy Spirit, that is, not in phantasm but in the truth of fullness; so that help was lacking to no need, but grace was seen to superabound both in teaching and in conduct and in curing the sick; so that the perfect work of the Holy Spirit appeared in the churches of the Thessalonians, so that, seeing themselves inferior in nothing to the churches of the Jews, they might rejoice in the gift of God, who deigned to make them sharers in the promise of Abraham.

(1 Thess. 1:6–7) And you became imitators of us and of the Lord himself, receiving the word in much affliction with the joy of the Holy Spirit, so that you were an example to all who believe in Macedonia and in Achaia. Eager for belief, they endured the reproaches and injuries they suffered from their fellow citizens, in confidence of the things to come. Hence they are called emulators of the apostles and of the Lord himself; because the Lord too suffered the same things from the Jews, and the apostles, following the faith of God, suffered persecutions, rejoicing that they had been counted worthy to suffer insults for the name of the Lord (Acts 5:41), knowing that great rewards had been promised for these things; for a slave is proved fit when, for love of his master, he offends his master's enemies. Mindful of this, they rejoiced in affliction in the hope of the promise. And so it is a great exhortation to advancement when he says that they have been made like both Christ and the apostles, so as to offer a pattern to the other believers in those places through which their fame went abroad.

(1 Thess. 1:8) For from you the word of the Lord has been spread abroad, not only in Macedonia and in Achaia, but your faith, which is toward God, has also gone forth into every place. This is like that saying to the Romans, when he says: That your faith is proclaimed in the whole world (Rom. 1:8). But in these there is more; for not only their faith, but the work of their faith has reached every place.

(1 Thess. 1:9–10) So that we have no need to speak anything. For they themselves announce concerning us what kind of entrance we had to you: and how you turned to God from idols, to serve the living and true God, and to wait for his Son from heaven, Jesus, whom he raised from the dead, who has rescued us from the wrath to come. They say that the report of their entrance and of the Thessalonians' conduct, together with the preaching of the Gospel, had travelled so far that there was no need to tell of these things; for it is superfluous to speak of what is known. For it had reached all round about, so that they even announced to others with what devotion they had received the apostles. And because they had been the more ready for all the things they had heard from them, leaving dead gods: and were waiting for Christ from heaven, who, they had heard, died for the sake of human salvation, and rose again with death vanquished, and was taken up into heaven with his flesh, a cloud ministering to him, to the confusion of the wise of the world, who, having regard to worldly things, think that the nature of flesh, because it is heavy, cannot be raised up into heaven. This is done by the working of the Holy Spirit, so that the earthborn wise may blush.

Chapter II

(1 Thess. 2:1–2) For you yourselves know, brothers, our coming in to you, that it was not empty: but having first suffered many things and been treated with insults, as you know, at Philippi, we made use of open freedom in our God, speaking to you the Gospel of God in a great contest. This affliction, which he now recalls (Acts 16:16 ff.), he suffered at Philippi with Silas on account of the spirit of divination which he had cast out of a girl, as has already been said in its place. He did not yield to this affliction, but was found the stronger, since he dares to preach and does not fear to speak, in the hope, of course, of the promised reward; so that these too might be secure in persecutions, rejoicing on account of the wage to come. He says, therefore, that this is the sign of a devoted and faithful preacher: if in affliction he does not fear to speak the word of God; and he signifies that man to be empty who, through present terror, is seized with dread of preaching. For he who is secure about the promised hope does not yield, so that he may be crowned.

(1 Thess. 2:3–4) For our exhortation is not from deceit, nor from uncleanness, nor in guile, but as we have been approved by God, that the Gospel might be entrusted to us, so we speak, not as pleasing men, but God, who tests our hearts. He says this on account of the false apostles, who preached in guile and deceit without being sent, so as to circumvent the Gentiles into serving the law of deeds. But he proves by many things that he preaches as one chosen and sent; because he was both found the stronger in afflictions and irreprehensible in his way of life, so that there was nothing in him that might seem less worthy of God, signs following besides. For some preached Christ for the sake of gain, serving the favor of men; because they were both rather slack in rebuking, since indeed they had no authority, and weak in tribulation; because their speech was not from the heart, but in deceit. For everyone who does anything without hope succumbs when tribulation arises.

(1 Thess. 2:5) For neither at any time have we conducted ourselves in a word of flattery, as you know, nor for an occasion of avarice. God is witness. Now he opens up what he signified above, when he says: Not on an occasion of avarice, giving God as witness that he did this from the heart, not in hypocrisy, as if despising small things so that perhaps more might be offered. Hence he also appeals to them themselves, as those who knew and could prove that he had flattered no one; for the false apostles wished a pattern of avarice to be given them, so that by that very occasion, while they saw the true apostles accepting everything that was brought, they themselves might plunder simpler men without incurring odium.

(1 Thess. 2:6–7) Nor seeking glory from men, neither from you nor from others; though we could have been a burden to you, as apostles of Christ. He weighs down the case of the false apostles so far as to say that he refuses, though it was lawful for him to require services, in order to repress those who had neither ability nor shame, and yet demanded honor for themselves; for they wished to commend themselves rather than the teaching of God. But the Apostle, who sought glory not in the present but in the future, made himself humble, so that the preaching of God might be exalted.

(1 Thess. 2:8) But we were little ones in your midst, as if a nurse were cherishing her children; so, longing for you, we desire to impart to you not only the Gospel of God, but also our own souls; because you have become dearest to us. He shows that he has so great an affection toward them (which, because it is true, he wishes to be seen proved not by words more than by deeds; for deceit can lie hidden in words), that, stirred by these exhortations, they might hold their whole mind in love of the Apostle.

(1 Thess. 2:9) For you remember, brothers, our labor and weariness, working night and day to this end, that we might not burden any of you. This is what he says in the Acts of the Apostles: All things, he says, that were necessary for me and for those who are with me, these hands have toiled for. Hence also in another epistle he says: Whoever does not work, let him not eat. And this is against the false apostles; for those who ate in idleness pretended that they were preachers. So then, to give an example of how the Christian life is to be governed, and to expose the pretenders, he applied himself to human labor, casting the false apostles into the shade: besides this, because those who apply themselves to labor turn away from evil thoughts, occupying their mind and keeping it from dangers.

(1 Thess. 2:10–12) We preached to you the Gospel of God, you are witnesses, and God, how holily and justly and without complaint we conducted ourselves with you who believe, as you know: and how toward each one of you, as a father toward his children, entreating you, and consoling, and adjuring you to this end, that you should walk worthily of God, who called you into his kingdom and glory. These things are plain; for he reminds them how they came to believe while he was preaching, and what manner of man they had found him, and what precepts he had given them, so that they might become worthy of God, who called them from earth to heaven, in order to rouse their joy.

(1 Thess. 2:13) And therefore we too give thanks to God without ceasing, that when you had received from us the word of the hearing of God, you took it up not as the word of men, but as it truly is, the word of God, which also works in you who have believed. They render thanks to God, who opened their understanding to receive his Gospel. For it is the glory of preachers when the hearers readily believe; for they received the word with such devotion that they proved they had understood it to be the teaching of God.

(1 Thess. 2:14–16) For you, brothers, became imitators of the churches of God which are in Judaea in Christ Jesus; because you too suffered the same things from your own fellow tribesmen, just as we ourselves did from the Jews, who both killed the Lord Jesus and the prophets, and persecuted us, and do not please God, and are opposed to all men, forbidding us to speak to the Gentiles that they may be saved, so as to fill up their sins always; but the wrath of God has come upon them to the end. He complains of the impiety and unbelief of the distrustful Jews, who are enemies not only of their own salvation but also of that of others, forbidding them to believe and be saved, parricides from the beginning; for they ought at least to have spared the common Lord, they who did not spare their fellow servants and their overseers: so that their sins might be filled up, to such a degree that they should never deserve to obtain mercy. Therefore he says that the Thessalonians suffered the same things from their fellow citizens, having imitated the patience of the saints and of our Lord Jesus Christ; and he brings in the Jews as the authors of that crime, so that it may be discerned both of how great glory those are worthy who suffer for the name of the Lord, and what vengeance is prepared for the hostile and the faithless.

(1 Thess. 2:17–18) But we, brothers, bereft of you for the space of an hour, in face, not in heart, hastened the more abundantly to see your face with great longing. Wherefore we wished to come to you, I Paul indeed, once and again, but Satan hindered us. They say that they are torn from them not in mind but in body; for love is not divided, nor is it separated: but for the sake of fuller instruction they say that they long to see their face as well. For in divine matters the fingers fail to take down what can be said; for a day is not enough for the pen for the things that can be uttered in one hour; not to mention that there are certain mystical things of our religion which ought not readily to be made public, on account of carnal minds, lest these suffer scandal: because not all things are to be said to all, but what is said must be tempered to the capacity of each, in one way for the untaught, in another for the grounded. Therefore the presence of the body is required, because thus the truth of love is made perfect. For although love is not divided, yet the absence of the body creates longing: thus the wish of souls is fulfilled by a kiss and an embrace. He asserts that Satan was the hindrance to this, who stretched out hands against even the apostles, lest the reasoning of the truth be laid open to men. For he inflamed the minds of the faithless to hold them back with beatings and chains, lest they speak the word of God. For the elders of the Jews said to the apostles: "Did we not strictly charge you not to speak in the name of Jesus to any man" (Acts 5:40)? But because what is not from God cannot stand, the hindrances of Satan are undone.

(1 Thess. 2:19–20) For what is our hope, or joy, or crown of glory? Is it not you, before our Lord Jesus Christ at his coming? For you are our glory and joy. It is plain that the perfection of the disciples is the joy and crown of the teacher. For these are the ones who will bring forth the labors of their fruits; for the teacher's fruit is an obedient disciple, whose good way of life, while it profits the teacher, brings forth the labor of his fruit. For the labor of the disciple in good work gives a crown to the teacher, with Christ as judge.

Chapter III

(1 Thess. 3:1–4) Therefore, no longer bearing it, we thought it best to be left alone at Athens: and we sent Timothy, our brother and sharer in the work of God in the Gospel of Christ, to this end, that he might both strengthen you and pray for your faith, that no one be moved in these afflictions. For you yourselves know that we are appointed to this; for even when we were with you, we foretold to you that we would suffer affliction, as has come to pass, and you know. He has set out the causes of the longing mentioned above. For this is why they wished to be present with them: to strengthen their minds; lest any of them should suffer scandal at the afflictions of the apostles, that men who worked such great signs should at times be made weak, so humbled as to be subjected to beatings and chains. To account for this matter he testifies that Timothy was sent, a fit teacher, to show that merits grow for Christians from this, if, having suffered for the name of the Lord, they give thanks to him from whom they expect the wage of this thing, and that at the same time they should pour out prayers to God, so that they might be steadfast. They had foretold that they would suffer this persecution for this reason: so that, by declaring that they had foreknowledge of this thing, they might show that they could have escaped and avoided these injuries: but because great hope lies in them, he therefore says that they bear them, when inflicted, willingly indeed.

(1 Thess. 3:5) Therefore I too, no longer bearing it, sent to this end, that I might know your faith; lest by some means the tempter had tempted you, and your labor should become empty. He says this on account of certain people who could be seduced by the cunning of Satan; because in every people there is a firm part and a weak one, on whose account he was anxious, lest he lose the fruit of their belief. And because he could not endure any longer not to know what was more certainly happening concerning them, he sent Timothy, the bishop created by him, from whom he could know for certain.

(1 Thess. 3:6–8) But now, when Timothy had come to us from you, and had announced to us your faith and love: and that you always have a good remembrance of us, longing to see us, as we also you; therefore we have obtained consolation, brothers, in you, over all our necessity and affliction, through your faith; since now we live, if you stand firm in Christ. He has made clear why he writes to them in eager language; for this is the reason: when the messenger came and made known the firmness of their devotion in faith and in love of the Apostle, being gladdened by their common concord, he writes to them, holding the fruit of their faith and good way of life as secure.

(1 Thess. 3:9–10) For what thanksgiving can we render to God for you, for all the joy with which we rejoice on your account before our God, praying night and day most abundantly that we may see your face, and supply what is lacking to your faith? He says that there is nothing so worthy that it could suffice as a recompense for the salvation of the Gentiles that has been gained. And since they had advanced themselves by the perseverance of their faith and by the conduct of a better life, he longs more and more to see them, so that they may thoroughly learn the things that pertain to their perfection, namely the mysteries of spiritual things, which ought not readily to be made public; so that they might know who and how immense he is whom they hoped for as judge. For it is one thing to receive the faith, and another to receive the exposition of the faith; for one discussion is about the nature of the Father and of the Son, and another about the persons. The Father is unbegotten, but the Son begotten: as far as the persons are concerned, there seems to be a difference, though the unity of nature is undivided; for the unity is not in the person but in the substance: and let the Holy Spirit not be thought inferior because he is placed third. He, therefore, who has no doubt about the second person admits the third also.

(1 Thess. 3:11) Now may God himself and our Father, and our Lord Jesus Christ, direct our way to you. He asks that his journey be arranged in its complete order: first by God the Father, because from him are all things; then by the Lord Jesus Christ, through whom are all things; so that the power and providence of the Father and of the Son may commend his coming, and that he may become acceptable to those who hear him, and that the hindrances of the devil might be loosed for the directing of his journey.

(1 Thess. 3:12–13) And may the Lord multiply you, and make you abound in love toward one another, toward you, and toward all, as we also do toward you, to strengthen your hearts unblamed in holiness before God and our Father at the coming of our Lord Jesus Christ with all his saints. To heap up his joy, with which he rejoices over them, he earnestly wishes them to be multiplied in all good things; so that, on coming to them, he may find in them more that is worthy of praise than he had heard from Timothy; so that they may be found irreprehensible with the saints in the love of God the Father, and at the coming of our Lord Jesus Christ, when the examination before his tribunal has begun to approve each one. He calls him God on account of the terror of his majesty, but Father on account of the kindness by which he deigns to adopt believers to himself as sons, which will be made manifest at the coming of the Lord.

Chapter IV

(1 Thess. 4:1) For the rest, therefore, brothers, we ask you and entreat you in the Lord Jesus that, as you have received from us how you ought to walk and to please God, as indeed you do walk, you may abound the more. This is what I said above, that to heap up his joy he wishes them to grow in good works according to the apostolic commands.

(1 Thess. 4:2–6) For you know what precepts I gave you through our Lord Jesus. For this is the will of God, your sanctification: that you abstain from fornication, and that each one of you know how to possess his own vessel in holiness and honor; not in the passion of desire, as also the Gentiles who do not know God: and that no one overstep or circumvent his brother in business; because the Lord is the avenger of all these things, as we also told you beforehand and testified. Although he praises them in the whole epistle, he nevertheless warns them not to be led by any stealthy encroachment into unlawful things, and so incur the vengeance which they have learned to be the avenger of evils: knowing that this pleases God, if they keep their bodies undefiled, and also deal simply with their brothers; lest, being found like the Gentiles, they be disinherited of the name of adoption.

(1 Thess. 4:7–8) For God has not called us to uncleanness, but to holiness. Wherefore he who despises these things despises not man, but God, who has given the Holy Spirit in us. To bind them more strongly to what he teaches, he interposes the person of God, saying: Not man does he despise who passes over the precepts, but God. And he says that God has given us the Holy Spirit for this reason, that we may live holily; because the Holy Spirit does not dwell in a body subject to sins (Wis. 1:4).

(1 Thess. 4:9–10) But concerning the affection of brotherhood we have no need to write to you; for you yourselves are taught by God to this end, that you love one another (John 15:17). For you also do this toward all the brothers throughout all Macedonia. Even while he praises them for doing this, he nevertheless warns them to do it more readily; for in saying that they have been taught by God himself, he signifies that they ought to be the readier in brotherly love. For on account of his great love God has had mercy on us; and when we have become his imitators, we show that we have not undeservedly been adopted by God as sons.

(1 Thess. 4:11) But we exhort you, brothers, to abound the more; and to strive to be quiet, and to do your own business, and to work with your own hands, as we commanded you; that you may walk honorably toward those who are outside, and that there may be in you desire of nothing. Again he exhorts them to the same things, that they should take pains to abound in good actions; for he admonishes this often in order to signify that this truly is what pleases God. For the way of righteousness must be held, so that one may come to mercy by right; so that those who are lower in substance may labor with their hands; so that by that very application they may be lifted out of common restlessness and depraved thought, and so that their discipline may be praised even by unbelievers, and they may be provoked to faith.

(1 Thess. 4:12–13) But we do not want you to be ignorant, brothers, concerning those who sleep; that you may not be grieved, as the others also who have no hope. For if we believe that Jesus died and rose again, so also God will bring with him those who have slept through Jesus. The reasoning is plain: that if the resurrection of the dead is believed to be coming, those who depart with the sign of the cross are not to be mourned. But the unbelievers are truly to be mourned, who pass from here into Gehenna, so that they may learn that what they refused to believe is true. But the faithful will rise to glory, so that those who were held to be fools in the world on account of their faith may be seen to be wise; for they provided glory for themselves. These are the ones who, departing through Jesus, that is, under the hope of this faith, will be brought with him at his coming, so that they may be seen by men. Yet while he himself forbids the passing of one who has died to be mourned, he himself says elsewhere: Lest I should have sorrow (Phil. 2:27). But this has already been resolved in its place.

(1 Thess. 4:14–17) For this we say to you in the word of the Lord, that we who live, who are left behind until the coming of the Lord, shall not go before those who have slept. For the Lord himself will descend from heaven at a command, and with the voice of an Archangel, and with the trumpet of God, and those who have died in Christ will rise first: then we who live, who are left, will be caught up together with them in the clouds to meet the Lord in the air, and so we shall always be with the Lord. Wherefore comfort one another with these words. He professes that he speaks with the mind of the Lord, when he says: This we say to you in the word of the Lord. For in order to demonstrate the faithful hope and the assured glory of the resurrection promised to the saints, he sets out the very order of how it will come about, at the bidding of Christ the judge, the Holy Spirit making it known; so that by this very hope those who have sent their dear ones on ahead may not grieve, learning that at the coming of the Lord those who die in Christ will rise first. For the Lord Christ himself, by the will of the Father, as the first angel of the heavenly army, as is contained in the Apocalypse of John the apostle (Rev. 12:7 ff.), will descend from heaven to wage war in the name of God against the Antichrist. When he has been destroyed, the dead will rise at his command. This, then, is to descend with the trumpet of God: to wage war in the name of God; for he is, as he himself said to Joshua the son of Nun (Josh. 5:14), the leader and prince of the army of the Lord, on which account he is called archangel. But when he descends with the trumpet of God, he is God; because the Scriptures contain both, as in Exodus: he whom we read appeared to Moses as an angel in the flame, we at once recognize in what follows to be God himself, that is, the Son of God, as the same Scripture says: And Moses, approaching, he says, turned himself away; because he could not look upon the face of God (Exod. 3:6). He is God, therefore, because the Son of God is true God: and leader and prince for this reason, because he is the head of all; because through him are all things. So when those who have died in Christ have risen first, then we who live shall be caught up together with them, borne on the clouds, to meet Christ in the air; so that all may come with the Lord to the battle, and that he may see alive those whom he had slain; because, as the clouds served the Lord, so too will they serve those whom he deigned to call his brothers. And so we shall always be with the Lord; for in the very rapture death will come, and as through sleep, so that the soul, having gone out, may be restored in a moment. For when they are taken up, they will die; so that, on reaching the Lord, they may receive back their souls in the presence of the Lord; because with God they cannot be dead. Therefore comfort one another with these words. With this hope he bids them comfort one another, those who still, in the manner of the Gentiles, thought that the passing of their dear ones must be mourned.

Chapter V

(1 Thess. 5:1–3) But concerning the times and the moments, brothers, there is no need for anything to be written to you; for you yourselves know diligently that the day of the Lord will come just as a thief in the night. For when they say: Peace and security, then sudden destruction will come upon them, like the pain of childbirth upon a woman with child, and they will not escape. This is what the Lord spoke in the Gospel, saying: Watch, because you do not know the hour at which your Lord will come (Mark 13:32). And again: If anyone says to you: Behold, here is the Christ, or behold, there; do not believe it (Matt. 24:23). For suddenly and unexpectedly he will appear, as lightning appears from the East to the West, having with him the host of the army of God the Father for the destruction of the Antichrist and his henchmen. For when the saints, that is, Enoch and Elijah, have been killed, and the faithless are secure about the kingdom of the devil and glad at their victory, sending gifts to one another, as the Apocalypse says (Rev. 11:7 ff.), then sudden destruction will come upon them suddenly. For at his coming Christ will destroy all these, so that the saints, seeing that they have escaped, may glory.

(1 Thess. 5:4–5) But you, brothers, are not in darkness, that that day should overtake you like a thief; for you are all sons of light and sons of the day: you are not of the night nor of darkness. By darkness he signifies ignorance and falsehood, but by the day knowledge and truth. Therefore he says that Christians are not in darkness, but that Gentiles or the unbelieving Jews are sons of darkness, because they are worshippers of falsehood. Wherefore he signifies that the faithful ought to be secure, because they are not ignorant of the future coming of the Lord, but await it anxiously, that it may come. But those who are ignorant, distrusting it, it will suddenly overtake off their guard, and will kill them.

(1 Thess. 5:6–11) Therefore let us not sleep, as the others do, but let us watch and be sober. For those who sleep, sleep at night; and those who are drunk, are drunk at night. But let us who are of the day be sober, having put on the breastplate of faith and love, and for a helmet the hope of salvation, because God has not appointed us to wrath, but to the acquiring of salvation through our Lord Jesus Christ, who died for us, so that whether we watch or sleep, we may live together with him. For which reason exhort one another, and build up one another, as you also do. What he says is plain; for he warns that we should not act negligently, secure in the name of the Savior, and that we ought therefore to be fortified with three bulwarks, that is, of faith, and of hope, and of love. For while faith remains, it rouses hope; and hope is necessarily anxious about the promise, and does not sleep; and love offers one devoted to God, for love admits nothing contrary. For it was for this reason that the Lord Jesus died for us and rose again: that whether we watch, that is, live, or sleep, that is, die, we may enjoy eternal life together with him. This is what he said above, that at the coming of the Lord the faithful, the dead as well as the living, will be taken up to meet the Lord, and will live with him always. He teaches that this is to be meditated upon, so that they may build one another up in this understanding.

(1 Thess. 5:12–13) But we ask you, brothers, to know those who labor among you, and are over you in the Lord, and admonish you, that you hold them in the highest honor in love because of their works: have peace among yourselves. This is what he says in another epistle too (1 Tim. 5:17), that presbyters who labor in the word of teaching are to be honored with double honor. For one who suffers want may grow reluctant to carry on an exercise that profits the hearers. For what does it profit to have honor without fruit? Or what great thing is it to offer carnal things to him who bestows spiritual things? For as riches beget neglect of salvation, so poverty, while it seeks to be filled, turns aside from righteousness. Hence Solomon asks to be made neither too rich nor needy (Prov. 30:8). Yet this very thing ought to be done in love, so that the honoring of the priest may be not so much from fear as from love, so that it may profit him also who does it. For a thing that is not done willingly does not attain its effect; for although for the present it may profit the one who receives, yet it will be of no profit in the future to the one who gives. And because dissension begets many evils, he exhorts them to be peaceable.

(1 Thess. 5:14–17) And we ask you, brothers: rebuke the restless, console the faint-hearted, help the weak, be patient towards all. See that no one renders evil for evil to anyone, but always pursue what is good, both towards one another and towards all. Always rejoice in the Lord, pray without ceasing, give thanks in all things. For this is the will of God in Christ Jesus in all of you. Since it is inevitable that among the people some are unrestrained, some faint-hearted, that is, timid, and some weak in faith, he therefore exhorts with entreaty those who are moderate and great-hearted and grounded in faith to take thought for these, bearing with them, so that they may be able to make progress; lest, if evil is repaid, they become worse. Therefore he exhorts that nothing other than the good is to be pursued, not only in the case of the brothers, but also in that of all. For by good works unbelievers can be drawn to belief, from which there may always be rejoicing in the Lord, that is, unto good. And one must pray without ceasing, for assiduous prayers move the mind of the judge to grant mercy. And in all good actions thanks are to be given to God, who has bestowed this teaching in Christ Jesus our Lord.

(1 Thess. 5:18–22) Do not extinguish the Spirit: do not despise prophecies. Test all things: hold fast what is good: abstain from every evil appearance. He says this so that one who speaks through the spirit should not readily be forbidden (for it is extinguished if the fervor of one beginning to speak is lulled to sleep by contradiction), lest perhaps, not understanding that spiritual things are being said by him, they do injury to the Holy Spirit and sin through ignorance; and that anyone who, as it were, reveals the Scriptures ought not to go without a patient hearing, but that all that is said should be examined, and so judged; lest perhaps, while they reject the grace given to a brother, they resist its author. He warns, therefore, that all things that are said are to be tested, and whatever has been said soberly and well is to be retained. For whatever agrees with the sayings of the apostles and of the Lord himself is to be held as well said and retained; but one must abstain from the things that seem hostile to the faith. For the spirit of the world is accustomed deceitfully, as if by imitation, to speak good things, and to slip perverse things in among them, so that through the things which are good the evil may also be credited; so that, because they are thought to be the sayings of one spirit, they may not be distinguished from one another, but through what is lawful the unlawful may be commended by the authority of a name, not by the reason of virtue. For they are accustomed, under the name of the apostles and of the Lord himself, to teach unspeakable and contrary things; and therefore he warns that all things are to be tested, because the utterances of the Lord cannot be at variance with one another. Hence it is that the epistle of the apostle John too: Do not, he says, believe every spirit, but test the spirits, whether they are from God (1 John 4:1), because if it says many good things, but in some point puts forth what is contrary to the faith, let it be known that it is not the Holy Spirit. For nothing of error can be found in the Holy Spirit. But through the exercise of speech some things are spiritual, and some carnal, because all error is assigned to the flesh; or else by a certain contrivance of cunning it feigns good things, so as to insert evil things among them, as I recalled above: just as in Montanus, and Priscilla, and Maximilla, the prophets of the Cataphrygians, the spirit of the world feigned the good by a certain imitation, so that through many things that were plausible, and true as well, it might lie that it was the Holy Spirit.

(1 Thess. 5:23) And may the God of peace himself sanctify you, perfect in all things, that your spirit whole, and soul, and body may be preserved without complaint at the coming of our Lord Jesus Christ. The God of peace is Christ, for he says: My peace I give to you, my peace I leave to you (John 14:27). Since indeed all things that the Father has are the Son's, the Apostle prays that he may sanctify us, that he may enlighten us with the godliness of his clemency, and direct our every act in perfection, so that the spirit given to us may be whole. For it is whole only if we are zealous for a good life, having a clean soul and a body without complaint, so that the whole man may be perfect. For the body can be clean and the soul be defiled through an evil thought; and therefore he says that the spirit in a man ought to be clean. But if an evil life or thought intervenes, the spirit will not be whole, because it deserts the man who is liable to sins. For while the soul and the body are at variance, although it is without feeling, they are made strangers to it, and as though losing them, the spirit will not be whole; for it is given to man for this, that it may remain in him and be, as it were, his head. It will therefore not be whole, because it loses its members, while the man withdraws from it, doing the things which the Holy Spirit hates.

(1 Thess. 5:24–25) For faithful is God, who called you, who will also do it. Brothers, pray for us. He calls God faithful in his promises, so that, secure of the reward, they may conduct themselves rightly. And because those who live rightly according to the Gospel will easily be able to obtain what they ask, he therefore exhorts them to pray for him; for many easily obtain what they desire.

(1 Thess. 5:26) Greet all the brothers with a holy kiss. Holy kisses are those in the Christian greeting; the rest are carnal, because whatever is done without Christ is carnal, and through this also mortal. For he who despises the author of life is in death.

(1 Thess. 5:27) I adjure you by the Lord that this epistle be read to all the brothers. He binds them to be careful to read the epistle to all the brothers, so that they may know both his praises and his admonitions, through which they may become perfect.

(1 Thess. 5:28) The grace of our Lord Jesus Christ be with you. Thus he wishes them to make progress in good works, so that the grace of our Lord Jesus Christ may remain in them for ever.
