Excerpts from a lost Life of St Hilda

Translated by Roger Pearse (2023) from the Latin of John Leland’s notes as printed by Thomas Hearne, Joannis Lelandi antiquarii De Rebus Britannicis Collectanea (Oxford, 1715), vol. 3, pp. 39–40 (online at Google Books). In his account of the Whitby area, Leland, the sixteenth-century “king’s antiquary”, copies these six sentences under the heading Ex vita S. Hildae, “From a life of St Hilda”; that Life is otherwise lost. Some of the sentences, such as the remark on the site of the convent, may be Leland’s own observations rather than words of the Life. Streneshalc is the Anglo-Saxon name of Whitby. Pearse suggests that the serpents turned to stone are the fossil ammonites still found on the beaches north of Whitby; the words in square brackets are his. The post gives the Latin with each sentence; only the English is given here. Taken from Pearse’s blog post “Some primary sources for the later legends of St Hilda” (2 February 2023), which states no licence. No other English translation is known.

The monastery of St. Hilda at Streneshalc was completely destroyed by Inguar and Hubba, and Titus fled to Glastonbury with the remains of St. Hilda.

The monastery of Streneshalc was restored in the time of Henry I by William Percy.

It is a wonderful thing to see serpents at Streneshalc turned into orbs, and by the judgement of heaven, or, as the monks say, by the prayers of Lady Hilda, into concrete stones.

The place where the convent now stands seems to me to be an impregnable stronghold.

A glass painting, which is in Streneshalc, shows that the Scots, who lived near the borders of the English, were cannibals even in the time of William Noth, and that this savagery was punished by the sword of William.

The river Eska rises in Eskdale, flows through the forest of Danby [Dalby], and finally empties itself into the sea at Streneshalc.
