Origen, Homilies on Numbers — Homily 14

Again concerning Balaam.

[§1] There are many things that remained over for us when we were expounding the reading about Balaam and his donkey; and since it is a discourse bound by time, held in the church for the sake of edification, it did not have space enough for us to set forth each single word of Scripture in such a way that nothing at all should be left undiscussed, and to apply an explanation to each point, since indeed a style of this kind belongs rather to commentaries. Therefore those matters that seem worthy of inquiry we take up again, and, discussing them, we shall attempt to treat them with what explanation we can and to bring them into the open. But there is a further difficulty in the explanation of the history itself, namely how, when it is said that God came by night to Balaam first and asked who the men were that had come to him, and Balaam had answered that they had been sent by Balak, the son of Zippor, saying, "Come, and curse for me the people," God answered him, "You shall not go with them, nor shall you curse the people; for it is blessed"; yet again a second time it is said that God came to him by night and told him to go with them, but to observe this, that the word which God should give in his mouth, this he should speak; and again a third time, as he was going, an angel of God met him, to whom his journey seemed so far from proper or fitting that he even wished to kill him, had not the donkey, seeing the angel whom Balaam could not see, turned aside. And nevertheless, after he is blamed by the angel for having wished to go, he is again permitted by that very angel to go; only that he should keep the word which God should give in his mouth, so as to bring forth this alone and nothing further. All these things, then, have a difficult explanation; yet, as we have already said above, we shall give you in a few words occasions of understanding, so that you too may do what is written: that when these things are heard the wise may become wiser, and, "Give occasion to the wise, and he will be wiser." But before we come to the matter, we shall not even omit this, which, attending rather curiously, we observed in this place. In the letters of the Hebrews the name of God, that is God or Lord, is said to be written differently; for one thing is written for any god whatever, and another for God himself, of whom it is said: "Hear, O Israel, the Lord your God, God is one." This God of Israel, then, the one God and creator of all, is written with a certain fixed sign of letters, which among them is called the Tetragrammaton. Whenever, therefore, God is written in the Scriptures under this sign, there is no doubt that it is said of the true God and creator of the world. But whenever it is written with other, that is, common, letters, it is held uncertain whether it is said of the true God or of one of those of whom the Apostle says: "although there are those who are called gods, whether in heaven or on earth, as there are many gods and many lords; yet for us there is one God the Father, from whom are all things, and we through him." Those, then, who read the Hebrew letters say that in this place God is not set down under the sign of the Tetragrammaton; concerning which let him inquire who can. But also, as to what God said to Balaam, as if asking who those men were, it is inquired why he seems to be ignorant; and again, as to what he says: "you shall not curse the people; for it is blessed," whether it can be so understood as if he had said: "you shall not curse my people."

[§2] We meanwhile say that by a certain dispensation and wisdom of God all things in this world are so arranged that absolutely nothing is idle with God, whether it be evil, whether it be good. But let us explain more plainly what is said. God did not make wickedness; yet, though he could prohibit it when it has been invented by others, he does not prohibit it, but uses it, together with those very ones by whom it is held, for necessary purposes. For through those very persons in whom wickedness is, he makes those illustrious and approved who strive toward the glory of the virtues. For if wickedness were destroyed, there would surely be no one to oppose the virtues. But virtue, having nothing contrary to it, would not shine forth, nor become more splendid and more tested. And a virtue not proved nor tested is not even a virtue. But if these things be said without the testimonies of the divine words, they will seem contrived rather and composed by human art than true and beyond doubt. Let us therefore inquire whether the divine volumes also contain a meaning of this kind. Let us come to Joseph. Take away the wickedness of his brothers, take away envy, take away all that parricidal scheme by which they raged against their brother, to the point of selling him: if you take these away, see how great a dispensation of God you will at the same time have destroyed; for you will at the same time cut off all those things that were done in Egypt through Joseph for the salvation of all. There would have been no interpretation of Pharaoh's dream, if through the envy of his brothers Joseph had not been sold away and come into Egypt; no one would have understood what God had revealed to the king, no one would have gathered grain in Egypt, no one would have provided for the necessity of the famine by wise foresight; Egypt would have perished, the neighboring regions too would have perished by famine, Israel itself and its seed would have perished, and seeking bread it would not have entered Egypt, nor would the sons of Israel have gone out from it with the wonders of the Lord; nowhere would there have been the plagues upon the Egyptians nor those mighty works which God did through Moses and Aaron; no one would have walked through the Red Sea with dry footsteps; mortal life would not have known the food of manna; no streams of waters would have burst forth from the following rock; the law would not have been given by God to men; the things that are recounted in Exodus, in Leviticus, in Numbers too and Deuteronomy would not have come to the knowledge of the human race; certainly no one would have entered the paternal inheritance and the land of promise. And, to come to these things which are in hand, take away the wickedness of Balak, this most evil king, by which he desires to curse Israel, remove his cunning, by which he invites Balaam to curse the people: you will likewise have taken away God's dispensation toward the sons of Israel and the favor of his providence, and nowhere will those prophecies exist which through the mouth of Balaam are prophesied both to the sons of Israel and to the nations. But if you desire this to be confirmed also by testimonies of the New Testament, if you take away the wickedness of Judas and destroy his betrayal, you will likewise have taken away the cross of Christ and his passion; and if there be no cross, the principalities and powers are not stripped, nor are they triumphed over on the wood of the cross. If there had been no death of Christ, surely there would have been no resurrection either, nor would anyone have existed as the firstborn from the dead. But if there had been no firstborn from the dead, neither would there have been for us any hope of resurrection. In a like manner, then, let us suppose concerning the devil himself, for instance, that he had been constrained by some necessity, so as not to sin, or that after his sin the will to wickedness had been taken away from him: there would at the same time surely have been taken away from us the contest against the wiles of the devil, nor would a crown of victory be looked for by him who had lawfully striven. If we did not have those who resist us, there would be no contests, nor would prizes be set up for the victors, nor would the kingdom of heaven be prepared for those who conquer, nor would this momentary and light burden of our tribulation work for us beyond measure a weight of glory in the future, nor would any of us hope, for the endurance of tribulations, for the immense glory of things to come. From all which it is gathered that God uses not only good things for a good work, but also evil things; and this is truly marvelous, that God uses evil vessels for a good work. For in this great house of the world there are not only vessels of gold and silver, but also of wood and of clay; and some indeed unto honor, but others unto dishonor, yet both necessary. But since these vessels of which we speak are to be understood as rational and of free choice, not by chance nor by accident is each one made either a vessel of honor or a vessel of dishonor, but he who has shown himself such as to deserve to be chosen is made a chosen vessel and a vessel of honor. But he who lives by unworthy and most base thoughts, this one is formed a vessel of dishonor, not by his maker, but by himself, the causes of his own dishonor having been given. The maker, then, did not make them to be such, but he dispenses minds of this kind according to their purpose by the justice of his providence and by a certain ineffable reason. Just as, for instance, in great cities, if there are men more unworthy and of hidden and most base life, they are condemned to work at all the meanest and most laborious tasks, and yet such tasks are necessary to the city (for example, they serve the furnaces of the bath for kindling the fires, that you may enjoy either the convenience or the delights, or they clean out the drains or perform other things of this kind, that for you the dwelling in the city may be made agreeable; and they indeed either act from their own purpose or suffer according to their deserving, yet their work is of use to those for whom the things that are good and useful are provided), so also with these things which we have set forth: God indeed did not make wickedness, yet, once it was invented by the intent of the purpose of those who turned aside from the right way, he was unwilling to take it away entirely, foreseeing this, that, even if it were useless to those who used it, he would nevertheless make it useful to those against whom it was exercised. And therefore we ought by all means to flee, lest wickedness ever be found in us; but in others we must be zealous to conquer it, not to destroy it, because even those in whom wickedness is confer some necessary work upon the universe. For nothing is idle, nothing empty with God, since either he uses the good purpose of a man for good things or he uses the evil purpose for necessary things. But you will be blessed, if rather from the virtues that are in you the universe of this life may be found to obtain some good thing from you, like those of whom the Apostle Peter says: that you may be as lights in this world, holding the word of life. Unto glory, therefore, are lights necessary to this world. And what is so delightful, what so magnificent as the work of the sun and moon, by which the world is illumined? Yet the world itself has need also of angels who are set over the beasts, and of angels who preside over earthly exercises. There is need also of angels who preside over the birth of animals, over the plantation of shrubs and plants and the rest of the increases of the fields. And again there is need of angels who preside over holy works, who teach the understanding of the eternal light and the acknowledgment of the hidden things of God and the knowledge of divine things. See, therefore, that you do not make yourself to be found among those angels who are set over the beasts, if you lead a beastly life, or among those who are set over earthly works, if you have loved bodily and earthly things. But be diligent, that you may rather be taken up into the fellowship of the angel Michael, who ever offers the prayers of the saints to God. And you will be taken up into this number or into that office, if you persist always in prayer and watch in it and fulfill what the Apostle says: "praying without ceasing." Or certainly that you may be taken up into the fellowship and office of Raphael, who presides over healing; if, when you see someone wounded by sins and pierced by the arrows of the devil, you apply the cure of words and confer the medicine of the word of God, that you may heal the wounds of sin through repentance and show the medicine of confession. He, therefore, who does works of this kind in this world, offers himself to be formed by the maker in the age to come as a vessel of election and useful to the Lord, prepared for every good work; but if he does the contrary, he will offer himself as a vessel unto dishonor to the divine dispensation. And for this reason I think it is that in the divine volumes both the deeds of the good and of the evil are written, and that the divine Scriptures are woven together out of acts both on the left hand and on the right, that we may understand that with God neither the works of the evil nor of the good are idle. But now, after a very long digression, yet one necessary to the matter, let us return to our subject.

[§3] This Balaam, as we said above, was a diviner, foreknowing things to come sometimes by the ministry of demons and by magic art. He is asked by King Balak to curse the people of Israel; envoys come, they carry instruments of divination in their hands, the nations stand astonished and anxious, awaiting what Balaam may answer, of whom they were persuaded that he was held worthy of divine conversations. See now how wisdom makes this vessel, prepared unto dishonor, to profit unto the usefulness not only of one nation, but of almost the whole world, and to this man, to whom demons were accustomed to appear, God appears, forbidding the journey of the evil work. Balaam is amazed and marvels at the authority of the one forbidding; for it was not usual for evil to be displeasing to demons. But meanwhile he sends back the envoys, saying that he cannot do anything except the word which God should give in his mouth. The envoys return again, again he inquires, again he is troublesome, again he desires to hear; for one who is greedy does not easily lack an eye to reward. What, then, does he hear a second time from God? "If these men came to call you," he says, "arise and go with them." In which indeed God yields to the will of his greed, that what is written may be fulfilled: "he let them go according to the desire of their heart, they shall go in their own wills," yet nevertheless the counsel of the divine dispensation is fulfilled; for it is said to him: "the word, whatever I shall give in your mouth, this you shall speak." If Balaam had been worthy, God would have placed his word not in his mouth, but in his heart. But now, since in his heart there was the desire of reward and the greed of money, the word of God is placed not in his heart, but in his mouth. For a wondrous and great dispensation was being carried out, that, since the words of the prophets, which were contained within the court of Israel, could not reach the nations, through Balaam, in whom faith was held by all the nations, the secret mysteries concerning Christ might become known also to the nations, and he might carry a great treasure to the nations, borne not so much by heart and understanding as by mouth and speech. But that we may not delay over each point—for there is no time for all things to be resolved—Balaam, having mounted his donkey, went along the way; an angel met him, that one without doubt who was present with the sons of Israel. He opens the mouth of the donkey, that Balaam may be reproved through her, and that he who seemed a diviner and wise might be confuted by the voice of a dumb beast.

[§4] But after these things it now seems fitting to touch also on something of allegory. This Balaam, who is interpreted "a vain people," seems to me to bear the person of the scribes and Pharisees of the Jewish people. But Balak, who is interpreted "exclusion" or "devouring," is himself also to be understood as bearing the person of some one of the spiritual contrary powers of this world, which desires to exclude and devour Israel—not that Israel which is according to the flesh, but that which is according to the spirit. This contrary power, then, wishing the spiritual Israel to be destroyed and utterly extinguished, uses no other ministers than the chief priests and scribes and Pharisees; it invites these, to these it promises rewards and prizes. But they, just as this Balaam does, feign that they refer all things to God and act with zeal for God; for they say: "search the Scriptures and see that a prophet does not arise out of Galilee"; and again they say: "we have a law, and according to the law he ought to die, because he made himself the Son of God." Through each of these things the scribes and Pharisees indeed seem to have zeal for God, but a feigned one. But the donkey on which Balaam sat, since Scripture says: "men and beasts you will save, O Lord," can be understood as that part of the believers which, whether for foolishness or for innocence, is compared to animals. For thus also the Apostle says: "consider your calling, brothers, that not many among you are wise, not many powerful, not many noble; but the foolish things of the world God has chosen, that he may confound the wise." These, then, such as they are—as the deeds that were done in the parables in the Gospel signify—were held in a manner subjected and bound by those most evil masters, the scribes and Pharisees, but they are loosed by the Lord, not indeed through the Lord himself, but through his disciples; for he himself says to the disciples: "go into the village that is opposite you; and you will find a donkey tied and her colt; loose them and bring them." As, therefore, in the Gospel not the Lord himself, but the disciples loose the donkey, so also here the mouth of the donkey is opened not by God himself, but by an angel; and as in the Gospels those who do not see reprove those who see, so also here those who were dumb reprove those who speak. And this is what the Lord said: "Father, I give you thanks, because you have hidden these things from the wise and prudent, and have revealed them to little ones." The scribes, therefore, and Pharisees were they who sat upon this donkey and held her bound. Against these, then, the angel is angry, and, but for a certain foresight of things to come, he would indeed have destroyed them, but would have saved the donkey, who saw and reverenced him who came to the vineyard and stood among the vineyards. Yet he crushed the feet of the one sitting upon her against the wall, and therefore perhaps that ancient rider of hers cannot walk, nor come to him who says: "come to me all you who labor and are burdened." Yet the donkey comes, led by the disciples, and on her, on whom then sat Balaam greedy for reward, now Jesus sits. And do not marvel if you see him, whom we said bears the form of the scribes and teachers of the people, prophesying about Christ; for we read that Caiaphas also did this, who said: "it is expedient for you that one man perish for the whole people"; but this, it says, because he was high priest of that year, he prophesied. Balaam therefore also prophesies about Christ, and therefore let no one be exalted, even if he prophesy, even if he deserve foreknowledge, but let him return to the saying of the Apostle, who, looking to these things, says: "whether prophecies, they will be abolished; whether tongues, they will cease; whether knowledge, it will be destroyed." And what, then, is it that remains? "Faith," he says, "and hope and charity; but the greater of these is charity." And "charity alone," he says, "is that which never falls." Therefore above prophecy, above knowledge, above faith, above even martyrdom itself, as Paul teaches, charity is to be held and charity is to be cultivated, because both God is charity and Christ his Son is charity, who may deign to grant us the perfection of charity. To him be glory unto ages of ages. Amen.
